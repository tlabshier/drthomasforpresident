# 261108 - Manners, Morals, and National Survival

# What No Army Could Take:


### Samuel Adams on Principles and Manners


*By Dr. Thomas | 08 November 2026*

>
*“A general Dissolution of Principles & Manners will more surely overthrow the Liberties of America than the whole Force of the Common Enemy.”* — Samuel Adams to James Warren, February 12, 1779


## A warning written in wartime


Samuel Adams wrote those words in February 1779, while he was serving in the Continental Congress at Philadelphia. The war was not going well. The British had just taken Savannah. The Continental currency was losing value month by month, and Adams’s own letters that winter worry over it. The most powerful empire on earth still had armies and fleets on American soil and every intention of using them.


A man in that position could be forgiven for thinking the redcoats were the only danger that mattered. Adams did not think so. Writing privately to his friend James Warren, president of the Massachusetts Provincial Congress, he named a danger he thought greater than the British army: that Americans themselves would lose the principles and the manners that had made them free.


He went on to explain why. While the people are virtuous, he wrote, they cannot be subdued; but once they lose their virtue, they will be ready to surrender their liberties to the first invader, external or internal. He told Warren, speaking just between the two of them, that he believed the principles and manners of New England had produced the spirit that established American independence, and that nothing but the opposite principles and manners could overthrow it. His conclusion was practical: those who intend to hand liberty down to their children as an inheritance must join together on public principles to support public virtue.


Adams was not writing a sermon for the newspapers. He was confiding his deepest worry to a trusted friend, in the middle of a war. That perspective adds gravity to his letter and its warning.


## What Adams meant by “manners”


We hear the word “manners” today and think of which fork to use. Adams meant something larger. In the eighteenth century, *manners* meant a people’s settled habits: how they treat one another, what they will and will not do in public, what they are ashamed of, and what they honor. *Principles* are what a people believe to be right. *Manners* are those beliefs put into daily conduct.


So Adams was not worried that Americans would forget their etiquette. He was worried that they would stop believing in right and wrong, and that their daily conduct would follow.


## Look around


Most Americans are still decent people. Most drivers let you merge. Most neighbors would help you push your car out of a snowbank. I do not want to overstate the case, because overstating it would be its own kind of dishonesty.


But something has frayed, and most of us can feel it. Grown men throw punches in the street over a parking space. Drivers make obscene gestures at strangers. People drop their trash on the sidewalk as though cleaning it up were someone else’s job. Vandals spray paint on walls that other people paid for and worked to keep up.


Americans are not imagining this, and they are not divided on it along party lines. In Gallup’s May 2026 survey, 80 percent of Americans said the state of moral values in the country is getting worse; only 15 percent said it is getting better. When Gallup has asked people to name the single biggest problem with the nation’s moral values, the most common answer has been a lack of *consideration of others*. That is almost exactly what Adams meant by manners.


## Is this just a complaint every generation makes?


It is fair to ask. Older people have complained about the young since Socrates, and every age has had its brawlers and vandals. Violent crime in America remains well below its peak of the early 1990s. A poll measures how people feel, not what they do, and what they watch shapes how they feel. A phone camera can make one ugly moment in one city look like the condition of the whole country.


I take those points seriously, and they keep me from claiming more than I know. They do not change my conclusion. Crime statistics measure what the law forbids, and Adams was talking about what the law cannot reach. Most of what is wrong in the street scenes above is not a crime at all. Rudeness is legal. Contempt is legal. Walking past a neighbor in trouble is legal. A nation can have falling crime rates and still be losing the habits of self-restraint, honesty, and regard for others that make freedom possible. When four out of five Americans, across every party, tell a pollster the same thing year after year, they are reporting something real about the life they live, even if they cannot measure it precisely.


So I stand on Adams’s side of this question. The uncertainty is real: we cannot weigh manners on a scale, and our impressions are distorted by what reaches our screens. But the thing Adams warned about is happening, and it matters more than most of what fills the news.


## Why law cannot fix it


A few of the behaviors I described break the law. Most do not, and the deeper problem would remain even if every one of them did.


A free country runs on things no statute can compel: honesty when no one is checking, self-control when you are angry, respect for a neighbor you do not like. The men who founded this country knew it. John Adams, Samuel’s cousin, wrote in 1798 that our Constitution was made only for a moral and religious people, and is wholly inadequate to the government of any other. Edmund Burke put the same idea in one sentence: men are qualified for civil liberty in exact proportion to their disposition to put moral chains upon their own appetites.


That is the heart of it. Self-government begins as government of the self. When people stop governing themselves, someone else steps in to govern them. Every lost habit of restraint invites a new rule, a new camera, a new fine, a new enforcement office. Each one may be reasonable on its own terms. Together they add up to a people who are watched and managed instead of trusted.


This is how a free people lose their liberty without a shot being fired. No foreign army needs to take it. We hand it over a little at a time, each surrender made to fix a problem that our own lack of restraint created. That is what Adams meant by the *internal* invader.


## The moral foundation


I do not argue for better manners because order is convenient, or because good behavior makes a town look nicer, though both are true. I argue for it because it is right.


Scripture is plain that the health of a nation rests on its character: *“Righteousness exalteth a nation: but sin is a reproach to any people.”* **(Proverbs 14:34)** And it is equally plain that freedom is not permission: *“For, brethren, ye have been called unto liberty; only use not liberty for an occasion to the flesh, but by love serve one another.”* **(Galatians 5:13)**


I test every public question I write about with three questions. First, is it righteous: does it conform to what God has established as right? Second, if so, is it loving: does it serve my neighbor’s real good? Third, would I want it done to me: if I put myself in my neighbor’s place, and feel what he feels in his own body, mind, and heart, would I accept it?


Run the street scenes through that test. The man who throws a punch over a parking space fails the first question before he gets to the second. The driver who makes an obscene gesture at a stranger would not want that done to his own mother. The person who drops trash on the sidewalk is asking his neighbor to clean up after him. The vandal takes what another man built and spoils it. None of these is a small thing when it becomes the habit of a nation. Each one is a refusal of the rule Christ gave us: *“Therefore all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them: for this is the law and the prophets.”* **(Matthew 7:12)**


Manners, in Adams’s sense, are simply that rule practiced in public by ordinary people on ordinary days.


## What a president can and cannot do


The office I am seeking has limits. A president cannot legislate kindness. Washington cannot pass a law that makes men hold their tempers or teaches children to pick up after themselves, but it can and should encourage these virtues, model them, and explain why they matter. A little can be done from the top; it is a leader’s job to give direction to the nation he leads. But leading from Washington is not the same as the managing, teaching, and nurturing that happen at home and in church. Building and restoring character belongs to everyone, and families, churches, neighbors, and local schools governed by local boards should take it up deliberately and keep at it. This job is far too big for one man, one office, or a Department of Manners. It cannot be outsourced, and no agency or bureau answers for any man’s conduct but his own. Each of us will one day stand before God for himself. For that day we should study His Word and learn the ways of righteousness. If we do, we and all those in our house will be ready to serve the King and His kingdom as His hand extended, giving a cup of water in His name: loving, teaching, supporting, and setting an example of good manners and of the principles we believe.


A president can lead by voice and by example. He can speak plainly about right and wrong without apology. He can also show how political opponents ought to treat one another. A candidate may rightly name the errors in his opponent’s conduct and the flaws in his character, and call on him to confess, repent, and renounce them, but never with contempt. Contempt is dehumanizing; it reduces a man to his mistakes. The better order is the one Christ gave for a brother’s fault **(Matthew 18:15–17)**: raise it first in private, then with a few others, and take it to the voting public only after the warning has been rebuffed. An opponent should be openly criticized for the positions he took and still refuses to change. Candidates should meet, establish where each actually stands, and let their real differences in ideas be the substance of the contest. A character flaw that has been confronted and repented of should be graciously dropped from talking points and attack strategies. A leader should show respect for his audience by telling the whole truth about himself and expecting his opponent to do the same.


He can honor the parents, teachers, pastors, and ordinary citizens who are doing the slow work of forming character. And he can tell the truth that Adams told Warren: that our liberty depends less on what happens in Washington than on what happens on our own streets and in our own homes.


That is why Righteousness stands at the center of The New ERA Vision. Energy and Accountability matter, but neither will save a people who no longer govern themselves in right thought, speech, and action.


## Where it starts


Adams told Warren that those who mean to pass liberty on to their children must join together in support of public virtue. That is still the assignment, and it starts small. Hold your temper in traffic. Pick up the trash, even when it isn’t yours. Teach your children to look a neighbor in the eye and treat him as they would want to be treated. Say plainly, in your family and your church, that some things are right and some are wrong.


The redcoats could not take our freedom by force. Adams understood that a nation that forgets how to behave can give it away on its own. The good news hidden in his warning is that the reverse is also true. Liberty lost a little at a time can be recovered a little at a time, by people who decide to govern themselves according to the standards of Biblical Godliness.


That is my position, and I hold it open to a better argument.
