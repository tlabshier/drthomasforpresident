# 261127 - The Ukraine War - A History and Evaluation

# The War in Ukraine


### How It Came, What It Has Cost, and What a Just Peace Would Require

**Thomas Lee Abshier, ND** **27 November 2026**


---


The largest war in Europe since 1945 is now nearing the end of its fifth year of full-scale fighting, and its twelfth year if we count from 2014. As I write, American, Russian, and Ukrainian negotiators have just met again in Abu Dhabi. They agreed to exchange prisoners. They did not agree on the one question that matters most: where the war ends and on whose terms.

Americans have been told two very different stories about this war. In one, an aggressive dictator invaded a peaceful democracy without cause. In the other, a reckless West broke its promises, pushed its military alliance to Russia's border, helped overthrow an elected government in Kyiv, and provoked a predictable response. Each story contains real facts. Each leaves out facts the other side considers decisive.

I want to tell the whole history as honestly as I can, give each side its strongest case, and then judge the war by the standard I apply to every war: the only warrants for war are an actual attack or a genuine threat of attack.

My conclusion, stated up front, is this: **Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine was an unjust war of aggression, and Ukraine's defense is a just one.**** But the West's conduct over thirty years, including broken assurances, careless promises, and meddling in Ukraine's politics, helped create the conditions for this war, and an honest peace requires us to admit it.** I will show my reasons.

## Two Peoples and a Long Shadow

Russia and Ukraine share a common origin in Kyivan Rus, the medieval state centered on Kyiv that accepted Christianity in 988. Over the following centuries the lands of present-day Ukraine were ruled at various times by Lithuania, Poland, the Russian Empire, and Austria-Hungary. A distinct Ukrainian language, literature, and national identity developed alongside, and often against, Russian imperial rule.

The twentieth century left deep wounds. In 1932 and 1933, Stalin's forced collectivization produced a famine, known in Ukraine as the Holodomor, that killed millions of Ukrainians. Ukraine was then devastated again in the Second World War. These memories shape how Ukrainians see Moscow today.

When the Soviet Union collapsed, Ukrainians voted on 1 December 1991 on whether to become independent. More than 90 percent voted yes, with majorities in every region, including a slim majority in Crimea. Whatever the shared history, the people of Ukraine chose a separate state, and Russia recognized it.

## The Promises of the 1990s

Two sets of promises from that decade still drive the arguments today.

**The Budapest Memorandum.** At independence, Ukraine held the world's third-largest nuclear arsenal, inherited from the Soviet Union, though Moscow controlled its launch systems. On 5 December 1994, the United States, the United Kingdom, and Russia signed the Budapest Memorandum, which gave Ukraine security assurances against the threat or use of force against its territory or political independence. In exchange, Ukraine joined the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty as a non-nuclear state and, by 1996, had returned all of its warheads to Russia. Russia was one of the signers who promised not to attack Ukraine.

**The assurances on NATO.** Russia's side of the argument begins earlier. During the negotiations over German reunification in 1990, Secretary of State James Baker told Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev that if a united Germany stayed in NATO, "there would be no extension of NATO's jurisdiction for forces of NATO one inch to the east." Gorbachev replied that a broadening of the NATO zone was not acceptable, and Baker answered, "We agree with that." Declassified American, Soviet, German, British, and French documents published by the National Security Archive in 2017 show a cascade of similar assurances from Western leaders in 1990 and 1991.

These assurances were never written into a treaty, and historians still debate whether they applied only to East Germany or to Eastern Europe as a whole. Gorbachev himself later said that the expansion of NATO was not discussed at the time, while also criticizing expansion as a violation of the spirit of the period. What is not in dispute is this: NATO later admitted Poland, Hungary, and the Czech Republic in 1999, and the Baltic states and others in 2004, and Russian leaders came to regard the earlier assurances as a promise broken.

## Bucharest, 2008

At its April 2008 summit in Bucharest, NATO declared that Ukraine and Georgia "will become members of NATO." The United States pushed to give both countries a formal Membership Action Plan. Germany and France, unwilling to provoke Russia, blocked it.

The result was the worst of both worlds: a public promise of membership with no protection and no plan for getting there. One later observer called it a permanent waiting room. Four months later, Russia went to war with Georgia. From Moscow's point of view, Bucharest was a declaration that NATO intended to move onto Russia's most sensitive border. From Kyiv's point of view, it was a promise of shelter that never arrived.

## The Maidan and the Fall of Yanukovych, 2013–2014

In November 2013, Ukraine's elected president, Viktor Yanukovych, suspended an association agreement with the European Union under pressure from Moscow. Protesters filled Kyiv's Independence Square, the Maidan. Over the winter the protests grew and turned violent. In February 2014, about a hundred people were killed, most of them protesters. On 21 February, Yanukovych signed a European-brokered deal with opposition leaders; the next day he fled the capital, and parliament voted to remove him.

This is where the two stories diverge most sharply.

The case that the United States meddled is real. A recording of a phone call between Assistant Secretary of State Victoria Nuland and U.S. Ambassador Geoffrey Pyatt, leaked in early February 2014, captured the two discussing in detail their preferences for who should serve in a post-Yanukovych government. "Yats is the guy," Nuland said of Arseniy Yatsenyuk, who became prime minister weeks later. The U.S.-funded National Endowment for Democracy later said its grants played an important role in the early stage of the protests. And parliament's removal of Yanukovych did not follow the formal impeachment procedure in Ukraine's constitution.

The case against calling it a coup is also real. The protests were massive and homegrown. Yanukovych fled rather than being seized, after days in which police and snipers killed protesters in the streets of Kyiv (who fired the first shots in those final days is still disputed, and a Ukrainian court in 2023 found that some shots came from a building held by protesters). Ukraine held a presidential election that May, which Petro Poroshenko won. In 2019, Ukrainians voted Poroshenko out by a landslide and elected an outsider, Volodymyr Zelensky, with about 73 percent of the vote. A government whose leaders can be voted out by their own people is hard to describe as a puppet.

My judgment: the United States did not manufacture the Maidan, but it took sides openly and sought to shape who governed Ukraine. That was foreign policy pursued by influence rather than by respect for another people's choice, and it handed Moscow a grievance it has used ever since.

## Crimea and the Donbas, 2014–2021

Within days of Yanukovych's flight, armed men without insignia seized Crimea's parliament and key sites. A year later, Putin admitted on Russian television that at an all-night meeting ending on the morning of 23 February 2014, he had told his security chiefs, "We will have to start work to return Crimea to Russia." He also admitted deploying troops to "stand behind" Crimea's local self-defense forces.

A referendum followed on 16 March 2014. Officially, 83 percent turned out and 96.77 percent voted to join Russia. Many Crimeans probably did want to join Russia: Crimea's population was majority ethnic Russian, and a Pew survey weeks later found 91 percent of Crimeans calling the vote fair. But the vote was held under military occupation, without independent observers, and the ballot offered no option to keep Crimea's existing status. A poll taken two days before the vote found that support for joining Russia fell to about 54 percent when other options were offered. The United Nations General Assembly declared the referendum invalid. I count it as an annexation by force, ratified after the fact.

In April 2014, Russian-backed separatists, joined by Russian volunteers and later regular units, took up arms in the eastern Donbas regions of Donetsk and Luhansk. The war there killed between 14,200 and 14,400 people from 2014 to 2021, according to the UN human rights office, including at least 3,400 civilians; the rest were soldiers and fighters on both sides. The civilian toll includes the 298 people aboard Malaysia Airlines Flight 17, shot down over separatist-held territory in July 2014 by a Russian-supplied missile, as a Dutch court later found. Civilians died from shelling by both sides, and the toll fell sharply after the ceasefire agreements of 2015.

Those agreements, signed in Minsk in 2014 and 2015, were meant to freeze the fighting and give the Donbas special status within Ukraine. Neither side fully carried them out. In December 2022, former German Chancellor Angela Merkel, one of the agreements' architects, told *Die Zeit* that "the 2014 Minsk agreement was an attempt to give Ukraine time," and that Ukraine "used this time to get stronger." Moscow has cited her words ever since as proof that the West negotiated in bad faith. Her defenders say she meant only that the agreement bought time; it did not make the war.

## The Road to February 2022

In July 2021, Putin published a long essay titled "On the Historical Unity of Russians and Ukrainians," arguing that Russians and Ukrainians are one people whose separation is an historical injustice. A survey in Ukraine weeks later found that only about one in eight Ukrainians agreed.

That fall, Russia massed troops on Ukraine's borders. In December 2021, Moscow published two draft treaties demanding, among other things, that NATO halt all expansion, rule out Ukrainian membership, and pull back its forces from Eastern Europe. The United States and NATO rejected the main demands in writing on 26 January 2022, while offering talks on arms control and military transparency. Less than a month later, the invasion began.

## The Invasion, 2022

On 24 February 2022, Russia invaded from the north, east, and south. The attack on Kyiv failed; by early April, Russian forces had withdrawn from the north. At its furthest advance in March 2022, Russia occupied roughly 27 percent of Ukraine.

**The Istanbul talks.** In March and April 2022, Russian and Ukrainian negotiators met in Belarus and then in Istanbul. On 29 March they produced a framework, drafted by Ukraine, under which Ukraine would become permanently neutral, staying out of NATO, in exchange for international security guarantees. Drafts were exchanged until mid-April. Then the talks collapsed.

Why they collapsed is one of the most contested questions of the war. A senior Ukrainian negotiator later said that British Prime Minister Boris Johnson came to Kyiv on 9 April and urged Ukraine not to sign anything and to keep fighting; Johnson called that account "total nonsense." Other analysts, including some sharply critical of Western policy, conclude that large differences remained unresolved before Johnson's visit, including the size of Ukraine's army, the status of occupied territory, and whether Western guarantors would commit to defend Ukraine. What all accounts agree on is that the discovery of Russian atrocities in the town of Bucha in early April, where hundreds of civilians were found dead after the Russian withdrawal, made a deal with Moscow politically toxic in Ukraine. My reading is that Istanbul was a real opening, not a finished peace, and that both the Western powers and the battlefield turned Ukraine away from it.

On 2 March 2022, the UN General Assembly demanded Russia's withdrawal by a vote of 141 to 5. That autumn, Ukraine's counteroffensives retook much of the Kharkiv region and the city of Kherson. In September, Russia held referendums in four partly occupied regions, Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia, and Kherson, and declared them annexed, including territory it did not control. The General Assembly condemned the annexations by a vote of 143 to 5.

## The Long War, 2023–2024

The war then settled into attrition. Ukraine's 2023 counteroffensive failed to break Russian defensive lines. Russia took Bakhmut in May 2023 and Avdiivka in February 2024 at enormous cost. In June 2023, the Kakhovka Dam was destroyed, flooding the lower Dnipro; that June, the Wagner mercenary chief Yevgeny Prigozhin briefly marched on Moscow before standing down. Finland and Sweden, long neutral, joined NATO in 2023 and 2024, an outcome the invasion made far more likely, not less.

In March 2023, the International Criminal Court issued an arrest warrant for Putin and his children's rights commissioner on charges of unlawfully deporting Ukrainian children from occupied territory to Russia.

In August 2024, Ukraine surprised the world by invading Russia's Kursk region, the first foreign invasion of Russian soil since the Second World War. North Korea sent some 10,000 to 12,000 troops to help Russia drive the Ukrainians out, which Russia claimed to have done by April 2025. The incursion diverted Russian troops but did not stop Russia's advance in the Donbas.

## The Peace Push, 2025–2026

President Trump took office in January 2025 promising to end the war. Congress has passed no significant new aid for Ukraine since April 2024, and the administration twice paused some deliveries in 2025. In total, the United States committed $67.8 billion in military aid from 2022 through March 2026, while NATO members and other partners committed about $130 billion. Europe now carries more of the load than America does.

Direct Russian-Ukrainian talks resumed in Istanbul in May 2025, producing prisoner exchanges but no ceasefire. On 15 August 2025, Trump hosted Putin in Alaska, the first U.S.-Russian summit in four years. No agreement came of it. Afterward, Trump said the best path was a full peace agreement rather than a ceasefire, which was Putin's position. A U.S.-drafted 28-point framework, later cut to 19 points, was never published and never adopted.

The fighting continued. Russia entered the city of Pokrovsk in November 2025 and had taken it by early 2026, after nearly two years of siege, without the breakthrough it sought. In February 2026, Ukraine launched a counteroffensive in the south. By the Institute for the Study of War's count, Russia's net gain for the first ten months of 2026 was only about 117 square kilometers, a sliver of what Moscow claimed. Russia now holds roughly a fifth of Ukraine, including Crimea.

Trilateral talks among the United States, Russia, and Ukraine met in Abu Dhabi in January and February 2026, then stalled for nine months. A brief truce held in May. In late October, a localized ceasefire brokered with the help of First Lady Melania Trump allowed aid into, and civilians out of, the Russian-occupied city of Oleshky. The parties met again in Abu Dhabi on 10 and 11 November. They agreed in principle to exchange 314 prisoners of war. Russia still demands that Ukraine withdraw from all of the Donbas while maintaining its claim to four Ukrainian regions; Before the talks, Ukraine rejected an American proposal under which Russia would receive control of Donbas territory along the front line. American negotiators report progress on security guarantees for Ukraine, but the Institute for the Study of War assesses that the approval process the United States has proposed for those guarantees could delay or even preclude them. Meanwhile, the long-range strikes continue on both sides.

## What It Has Cost

No one knows the exact toll. Both governments hide their own losses and inflate the enemy's. The best independent estimate I have found, from the Center for Strategic and International Studies in July 2026, is that Russia has suffered about 1.4 million troops killed or wounded since February 2022, including about 450,000 killed, and Ukraine 525,000 to 625,000, including 125,000 to 150,000 killed. That is more than two million soldiers killed or wounded. According to the UN human rights mission, more than 15,000 Ukrainian civilians were killed in the first four years of full-scale war, and 2025 was the deadliest year for civilians since 2022.

Behind those numbers are widows, orphans, maimed young men, children carried off to Russia, cities flattened, and millions of refugees. Whatever one thinks of its causes, this war is a catastrophe for both peoples.

## The Strongest Case on Each Side

**The case Russia and its Western sympathizers make.** NATO broke the spirit of the assurances given to Gorbachev and expanded toward Russia for thirty years. In 2008 it promised Ukraine membership, crossing a line every Russian leader had warned against. In 2014 the United States helped topple an elected, Russia-friendly president. Kyiv then waged war on Russian speakers in the Donbas, killing thousands. The West signed the Minsk agreements without intending to keep them, as Merkel later admitted. When Russia proposed a negotiated settlement of European security in December 2021, it was refused. When a peace was within reach in Istanbul in April 2022, the West discouraged it. On this view, the war is the predictable result of a great power ignoring another great power's vital interests, and the honorable course is a settlement that recognizes realities on the ground and keeps Ukraine out of NATO.

**The case Ukraine and its supporters make.** Ukraine is a sovereign nation whose people chose independence by more than 90 percent. Russia promised in Budapest never to use force against Ukraine and broke that promise. Putin has denied that Ukrainians are a real nation and has annexed land he does not even control. He admitted ordering the seizure of Crimea before any referendum. His army committed atrocities at Bucha and elsewhere and deported Ukrainian children. NATO is a defensive alliance that no member was ever forced to join; the countries of Eastern Europe ran toward it because they feared exactly what Russia has now done. On this view, rewarding conquest would invite the next one, in Ukraine or elsewhere, and the honorable course is to help Ukraine defend itself until Russia accepts a peace that does not reward aggression.

Each case contains truth. The question is what the truths add up to.

## Judging the War

I hold that there are two warrants for war: an actual attack and a genuine threat of attack. A war of liberation, to change another people's government or way of life, is not a warrant for force; that must be a campaign to change hearts and minds. I apply the same standard to my own country.

**Ukraine's defense passes.** Ukraine was attacked. Its defense of its own homes, towns, and people is the clearest warrant there is.

**Russia's invasion fails.** Ukraine did not attack Russia in 2022. Was there a genuine threat of attack? Russia's strongest argument is that NATO's approach was itself a threat. I take that argument seriously, and I do not dismiss Russia's security concerns. But the facts do not support it as a warrant for war. In February 2022, Ukraine had no Membership Action Plan; Germany and France had blocked one for fourteen years. No NATO troops were based in Ukraine. No alliance member proposed attacking Russia. An alliance's open door, however unwise its timing, is not an army on the march. And Russia's own conduct reveals its aims: Putin's essay denying Ukraine's nationhood, the annexation of four regions including territory Russia never held, the deportation of children. Those are the marks of conquest, not of self-defense.

The test I apply has three questions. Is it righteous? Is it loving? Would I want it done to me? Russia's war fails all three. It takes what belongs to a neighbor by force. It treats the neighbor's people as a possession rather than as persons. And no Russian would accept a foreign army doing to Russia what Russia has done to Ukraine.

**The West's conduct does not pass untouched.** Here the realist argument has its strongest point, and I want to meet it honestly. Would Americans accept a hostile military alliance recruiting Mexico? We would not. We have the Monroe Doctrine, and in 1962 we nearly went to nuclear war over missiles in Cuba. I would object loudly and negotiate hard. But I would not be warranted in invading Mexico unless we were attacked or faced a genuine threat of attack. The same standard binds Russia. At the same time, the same honesty binds us. Western leaders gave assurances in 1990 and then acted as though they had not. NATO promised Ukraine membership in 2008 without any plan to protect it, which was the most dangerous possible combination. American officials discussed who should govern Ukraine while its elected president was still in office. And Western leaders entered the Minsk agreements, by one architect's own account, partly to buy time. None of this justifies the invasion. All of it is part of the history, and a nation that wants to be trusted must keep just weights in its diplomacy as well as its commerce: *"A false balance is abomination to the LORD: but a just weight is his delight"* **(Proverbs 11:1)**.

## What a Just Peace Would Require

I believe a just peace would rest on the following principles. I hold them open to a better argument.


 	- **No legal reward for conquest.** *"Remove not the ancient landmark, which thy fathers have set"* **(Proverbs 22:28)**. A ceasefire may have to freeze a front line where the armies stand. But Ukraine, the United States, and Europe should never recognize territory taken by force as lawfully Russian, just as the United States refused for fifty years to recognize the Soviet seizure of the Baltic states, which regained their freedom in 1991.

 	- **Real security guarantees for Ukraine,** strong enough that Russia cannot simply rearm and attack again, approved promptly rather than delayed indefinitely.

 	- **Ukraine's future alliances are Ukraine's choice,** not Moscow's and not Washington's. If Ukrainians choose neutrality in exchange for guarantees, as they were willing to consider in 2022, that is their right. It should not be imposed on them.

 	- **The return of deported children and the exchange of all prisoners,** as humanitarian obligations that come before any political bargain.

 	- **Accountability for atrocities,** pursued through courts rather than vengeance.

 	- **Free elections in Ukraine after the fighting stops,** so that the people themselves, not foreign capitals, decide who governs them.

 	- **An honest American role.** The United States should work hard for peace, but it should not pretend to be neutral between the nation that attacked and the nation that was attacked, and it should keep the commitments it makes.


On the question of whether Ukraine should fight on to recover all of its territory by force, I believe that is a prudential decision belonging to Ukrainians, who bear the cost in their own blood. My own conviction is that the recovery of hearts in occupied lands is finally a work of persuasion and patience, not only of arms, and that the Baltic example shows that a nation which refuses to surrender its claim, while refusing to fight forever, can outlast an empire.

*"Blessed are the peacemakers: for they shall be called the children of God"* **(Matthew 5:9)**. A peacemaker is not someone who ends a war on any terms. He is someone who ends it on terms that do not plant the seeds of the next one.

## Why This Concern Remains Serious

Some will say this war is far away, that America has spent enough, and that Europe should handle its own continent. There is truth in that. Europe has the wealth to defend itself and should carry the larger share, and America has its own urgent conflicts. But the concern remains serious for four reasons.

First, the principle at stake is universal. If borders can be changed by force and then ratified at a negotiating table, every strong nation with a weaker neighbor will learn the lesson.

Second, the promise of the Budapest Memorandum was that a nation could give up nuclear weapons and still be safe. If that promise proves worthless, other nations will draw the obvious conclusion, and the world will become far more dangerous.

Third, the war has drawn in North Korea, Iran, and others on Russia's side, and it is becoming one front in a wider contest between free and unfree nations.

Fourth, the human cost is enormous and still rising. Every month of fighting adds tens of thousands of casualties. A just peace, reached soon, would save more lives than any victory.

I stand on the side of a just peace that ends the killing without rewarding the aggression. The areas of real uncertainty are the true casualty figures, what really happened in Istanbul in April 2022, how many Crimeans and Donbas residents would choose Russia in a free vote, and whether Russia will honor any agreement it signs. Those uncertainties call for humility, but not for silence.
