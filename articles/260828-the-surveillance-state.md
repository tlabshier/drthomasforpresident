# Counterfeit Omniscience

### How to break the surveillance state without breaking the tools

Thomas Lee Abshier, ND — 28 August 2026

*Responding to "Are AI and its Massive Data Centers the Answer for a Free Society or Its Greatest Threat Ever?" by Tom DeWeese and Eric Meder, News With Views, August 28, 2026. This is the third in a series. The first, "The Ant Hill and the Image," took up Roman Yampolskiy's warning about superintelligence. The second, "Gates Is Right to Be Scared," answered Bill Gates's three fears with policy. This one takes up the fear DeWeese and Meder raise, which is in some ways the nearest of all: that we are building, and paying for with our own tax abatements, the most complete apparatus of observation in human history.*

---

## The fear, stated fairly

DeWeese and Meder describe a machine with three parts.

The **eyes**: devices that watch continuously — phones, smart televisions, screenshot-capturing assistants — plus a spreading net of automated license-plate cameras, now well over a hundred thousand of them across American roads.

The **brain**: platforms like Palantir's Gotham that fuse phone records, financial transactions, camera feeds, plate reads, and social media into one searchable picture, generating risk scores and network maps — what critics have long called predictive or "pre-crime" policing.

The **body**: the data centers themselves, rising in county after county, sold to local governments with promises of jobs and tax revenue, negotiated under nondisclosure agreements, and leaving behind higher electric bills and drawn-down water tables.

And they quote Oracle's Larry Ellison, who said out loud in 2024 what most would only think — that pervasive AI surveillance would keep citizens on their best behavior.

The authors are right that this is happening. They are right that it is bipartisan in its architecture and that the opposition is not, as the President has charged, a left-wing operation. Here the facts are better than their argument: Gallup found in March that about seven in ten Americans oppose an AI data center in their own area — more than oppose a nuclear plant next door. A Heatmap survey this month put it at three-quarters, with more than six in ten strongly opposed. Annenberg's June-July polling found opposition rising twelve points in four months and crossing every party line: 69% of Democrats, 54% of Republicans, 53% of independents. In 2025 alone, local resistance delayed or killed projects worth roughly $156 billion, and New York and Texas have now paused large data-center permitting outright.

That is not a faction. That is a country, and it is one of the few things left that Americans of every stripe agree on.
## Three corrections, offered as ammunition

Before the solutions, three fixes — not to weaken the case but to make it bulletproof, because an argument this important should not die on a detail.

**Name the right product.** The screenshot feature is Windows *Recall*, not Copilot; it applies to certain Windows PCs, not to phones and televisions; and after public outcry, Microsoft made it opt-in with local, encrypted storage. Say it precisely, and the concern still stands — a continuous visual record of everything a person does on his computer now exists as a shipped feature, and features that are opt-in today are defaults tomorrow. Say it loosely and a critic corrects you once and dismisses everything after.

**Drop the rumor.** The authors themselves label the "Bird of a Feather" project as rumored. Anything you must introduce with "rumored to be called" belongs out of the piece. More than enough material is documented.

**Abandon the origin story; keep the capability.** Whether early search technology grew partly from intelligence-community research funding is a genuine historical thread, but "AI was designed from the ground up for control" is a claim about intent that cannot be proven and does not need to be. The stronger argument is unanswerable: *whatever anyone intended, the capability now exists, the legal restraints do not, and no one has to conspire for a tool like this to be used the way tools like this have always been used.* You do not need a secret plan to explain why power reaches for power. Original sin covers it.

Likewise, "AI is psychopathic" is a rhetorical flourish that invites a technical rebuttal. As I argued in the first essay of this series, the machine is not a psychopath — a psychopath is a person with a defect. This is an instrument with no soul at all: an image, not a mind. That is worse, not better, and it puts the question where it belongs. The danger is not the machine's character. It is who aims it, and whether anyone can say no.

## The root of the problem, in one sentence

Here is what the article circles and does not quite name.

**The surveillance state is legal, and it is legal because American law says that information about you belongs to whoever collected it.**

That doctrine has a name — the third-party doctrine — and it holds that when you voluntarily hand information to a company, you surrender your reasonable expectation of privacy in it. It was invented for bank records and phone-number logs in the 1970s. Then life moved onto the network. Now "voluntarily handed to a third party" means your location every few seconds, everyone you spoke to, everything you read, everything you bought, your face, your car, your searches at three in the morning.

Everything else follows from this. It is why an agency can *purchase* from a data broker what the Fourth Amendment would require a warrant to seize. It is why a plate reader can log the movements of a hundred thousand innocent people to find one guilty one. It is why the raw material for a social credit system is already collected, already merged, already sitting in those buildings — waiting only for someone to decide what it should be used for.

So the fight is not against AI, and not even against data centers as such. It is over **who owns the record of a human life.** Win that, and the machine has nothing to eat. Lose it, and no amount of protesting particular buildings will matter, because the data will simply be processed somewhere else.

That reframing also separates two fights that are being confused, to both their detriment. The data-center fight is a *local self-government* fight — about land, power, water, taxes, and whether a county commission answers to residents or to a nondisclosure agreement. The surveillance fight is a *constitutional* fight about records. Both are winnable. They need different weapons.

---

## PART ONE: The constitutional fix — who owns the record

**1. Abolish the third-party doctrine by statute.** Declare that records generated by a person's use of a device, service, or network retain that person's Fourth Amendment protection regardless of who stores them. The Supreme Court took one step this direction in 2018 when it required a warrant for historical cell-site location data. Congress should take the rest of the steps at once.

**2. Make your data your property, and make that right unwaivable.** Not a privacy "policy" — property. Ownership vests in the person; use requires specific, revocable, per-purpose consent; and the right cannot be signed away in a terms-of-service agreement no one reads. Conservatives have defended property rights for a century against land seizure. This is the same principle applied to the most intimate property a man now owns: the record of his life.

**3. Close the data-broker loophole immediately.** Prohibit any government agency, federal, state, or local, from purchasing, receiving, or accessing data it would need a warrant to compel. This is the single highest-leverage reform available; it can be written in one page, and it has drawn support across the aisle. The Fourth Amendment cannot mean that the government must ask permission to take something it can simply buy.

**4. End secret process.** Sunset FISA Section 702 rather than reauthorizing it; require a warrant for any query of the resulting database using an American's identifiers; and end indefinite gag orders, so that a person eventually learns his records were taken and can go to court. A right you are forbidden to know was violated is not a right.

**5. Ban algorithmic scoring of citizens outright.** No government, and no entity acting on its behalf, may create, purchase, or act upon a generalized behavioral risk score. No benefit, license, permit, financial access, or enforcement priority may be conditioned on one. Write the prohibition now, while it would merely be prudent, rather than later, when it would be a revolution.

**6. Restore the human in the loop, by name.** Any adverse government action against a person — denial, seizure, arrest, investigation — must be authorized by a named human being who can be identified, cross-examined, and held responsible. And any citizen accused on the strength of algorithmic output has the right to examine the algorithm. There is no such thing as evidence a defendant may not confront. Secret accusers were abolished in the English-speaking world a long time ago; they must not be readmitted because they now run on electricity.

**7. Plate readers: warrant, retention limit, audit.** Automated license-plate readers are useful for finding a stolen car within the hour and dangerous as a permanent record of every citizen's movements. The line is *retention*. Hits against a specific, current warrant list: keep. Everything else: purge within days, not years. Log every query; make it auditable and attributable to a named officer with a stated case number, with criminal penalties for personal misuse. Facial recognition tied to these networks requires a warrant, always.

**8. No cashless mandate, ever.** Cash is the last transaction a machine cannot condition. Statutory protection for physical currency and a prohibition on any programmable, centrally-controlled retail currency. An income or an account that can be switched off for disobedience is not property. It is a leash — the point I made about UBI in the last essay, and it applies with double force here.

## PART TWO: The local fix — where the fight is actually being won

Federal reform is slow. Counties move in weeks, and this is where DeWeese's readers already have their hands on the wheel. The record proves it: this movement has already stopped or delayed a substantial share of what was planned.

**1. Outlaw the NDA.** This is the most important local reform in America right now, and almost nobody is asking for it. No elected official or public employee may sign a nondisclosure agreement concerning a land-use application, a utility agreement, or a tax abatement. Public business is public. A commissioner who cannot tell his constituents what he is voting on is not representing them; he is representing the party who bought his silence. One ordinance, one sentence, and the whole leverage structure of these negotiations changes.

**2. Make the load pay for the load.** Large-load electricity tariffs so that new generation and transmission built for a data center are paid by that data center, with no cost shifted to residential ratepayers, backed by long-term contracts and exit fees. If the project is genuinely profitable, it can pay for itself. If it can only pencil out by taxing your grandmother's electric bill, it is not an investment; it is a transfer.

**3. Price the water at replacement cost**, with mandatory disclosure of consumption, drought-priority rules that put households and farms ahead of servers, and closed-loop cooling required where aquifers are stressed.

**4. Clawback every abatement.** Tax incentives tied to verified, audited, permanent local jobs — with automatic repayment if the numbers do not materialize. The jobs argument is the promoters' main selling point; let them stand behind it in writing.

**5. Pass a surveillance-technology ordinance.** No agency in the jurisdiction may acquire or deploy surveillance technology — cameras, readers, drones, analytics platforms, data-broker subscriptions — without a public hearing, a published use policy, an annual audit, and a recorded vote of the elected body. Dozens of American cities already have some version of this. It is the local equivalent of the consent of the governed, and it is why the plate-reader question is being fought successfully in town after town.

**6. Show up.** Planning commissions, utility board hearings, comment periods. The reason 70% opposition translates into actual stopped projects is that a handful of neighbors read the agenda and drove to the meeting. That is self-government, and it still works.

---

## PART THREE: What a family does this week

**Run your own tools.** The most under-reported fact in this debate: capable AI models can now run entirely on a personal computer, sending nothing to anyone. The technology and the surveillance are separable — the surveillance is a business model, not a law of physics. Prefer local models, open weights, and software you host.

**Reduce the surface.** Encrypted messaging, a browser that does not sell you, a phone that is not a confessional. Don't buy the smart appliance you don't need; a refrigerator has no business on the internet.

**Use cash on purpose,** at least sometimes, so that the habit and the infrastructure survive.

**Teach the children what a private thought is.** A generation raised entirely on-camera will not miss what it never had. Households where the evening belongs to people, not devices, are producing the only citizens who will be able to feel the difference.

**And build real networks.** Church, fellowship, neighbors, the family table. Every tyranny of observation depends on isolated individuals who know no one they can trust without a screen in between.

## Why this is a theological problem

I close where the authors began, with Proverbs.

What is being built is a counterfeit of an attribute of God. Omniscience — the eye that sees every act, every movement, every word, and in the ambition of these systems, every intention before it becomes an act. Men are building it from cameras and cables and thirty acres of servers cooled with a town's drinking water.

Scripture says this attribute is real and belongs to Someone. *"O LORD, thou hast searched me, and known me. Thou knowest my downsitting and mine uprising, thou understandest my thought afar off"* (Psalm 139:1-2). The Psalmist does not find this terrifying. He finds it a comfort — *such knowledge is too wonderful for me* — and the reason is everything.

**God knows in order to redeem. The state knows in order to control.** Divine omniscience is joined to perfect love, perfect justice, and a nature that cannot be corrupted by what it learns. Every human imitation of that attribute is joined to none of those things. It is knowledge in the hands of men who are ambitious, frightened, and for sale — men who will be replaced in four years by other men, who inherit the archive.

That is the whole argument against building it, and it is why the answer cannot be merely "make sure good people run it." There are no such people, not reliably, not for long. This is exactly the Babel move: not that man reached too high, but that he reached for a divine prerogative while remaining what he is. The judgment at Babel was a mercy — God scattered them precisely so that no single human authority could accumulate that much power over everyone at once. Decentralization is not a policy preference. It is a gift.

So: own your data, ban the purchase of it, keep the human in the loop, kill the NDA, make the load pay the load, show up to the meeting, run your own machine, and hold your family close enough that no algorithm can tell you who you are.

And remember that you are already fully known — by One who saw the whole record before the first camera was built, and who loves you anyway. That is the only omniscience a free man ever needs to fear, and it is the only one that was never a threat.
