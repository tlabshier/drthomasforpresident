# 261008 - Timely  and Universal Justice

# Justice, Not Theater


### On perp walks, election alarms, and telling the truth in proportion


Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | 8 October 2026


---


Two pieces crossed my desk this week, and together they capture the mood of a great many Americans a month before the midterms.


The first, a column in *Western Journal*, is a cry of exhaustion. Its author says that conservatives have been promised for years that powerful wrongdoers would be brought to justice, that the hearings come and go, and that no one of importance ever goes to jail. His remedy is blunt: “Start putting them in jail.” He wants to see the perp walk, the handcuffs and leg irons, on national television.


The second, a newsletter from Emerald Robinson, assembles a series of recent election-security warnings from the Department of Homeland Security, the Pentagon, and Arizona and Georgia, and concludes that because the alarms are sounding, “it’s perfectly clear that election fraud is going to happen.”


I want to take both pieces seriously, because both speak for people I respect and live among. Both are right about something important. In my judgment, both go wrong in the same way: they lose proportion. I have written before that hyperbole is lying by proportion. A true fact stretched beyond what it can bear becomes a false witness. When the subject is justice and the integrity of our elections, proportion is not a matter of style. It is the whole matter.


---


## I. What the column gets right


The author’s grievance is not imaginary. Americans have watched powerful people handle the nation’s secrets in ways that would have ended an ordinary soldier’s or clerk’s career, and likely their liberty.


In 2016, the FBI concluded that Secretary of State Hillary Clinton’s private email server had carried classified information, including material at the top-secret level. Director James Comey called her handling of it “extremely careless,” yet recommended no charges. The FBI’s own notes record that aides destroyed old phones with a hammer, and that a contractor wiped the server’s archive after Congress had asked for her records.


In 2024, Special Counsel Robert Hur found that President Joe Biden had willfully retained and disclosed classified material, and declined to charge him, reasoning in part that a jury would see him as a sympathetic, well-meaning, elderly man with a poor memory.


James Comey himself gave a friend a memo of his conversations with the President so it would reach the press. The Justice Department’s Inspector General found in 2019 that this violated FBI policy. The Department declined to prosecute.


A citizen who looks at that record and asks whether one law applies to the governed and another to the governors is asking the right question. And the author is right about something else: politicians of both parties have learned that promising to “get them” raises money and fills airtime. Hearings that end in nothing are a kind of theater. He is right to be tired of being an audience.


---


## II. Where the column loses proportion


But a just complaint must be grounded in facts, and several of the column’s facts are not true as stated.


**The Hunter Biden laptop.** The FBI did not say the laptop didn’t exist; it had held it since 2019. The claim that it was likely Russian disinformation came in 2020 from a letter signed by fifty-one former intelligence officials, and social-media platforms suppressed the story. That was a real scandal. But it is also false that there was “no justice.” A Delaware jury convicted Hunter Biden of three gun felonies in June 2024, and he pleaded guilty to nine federal tax charges that September. A jury of his fellow citizens delivered justice. His father then undid it in December 2024 with a pardon, after the President had publicly said he would not issue one. This wasn’t a missing perp walk. It was the pardon power used to shield one’s own family.


**James Comey.** The column says the charges against him were dropped and nothing happened. In fact, Comey has been indicted twice since 2025. The first indictment, for false statements to Congress, was dismissed in November 2025 because a judge found that the prosecutor who brought it had not been lawfully appointed. In April 2026, prosecutors indicted him again in North Carolina, this time over a photograph of seashells forming “86 47” that he posted and then deleted, which they characterized as a threat against the President. The Justice Department has also reportedly revived an inquiry into classified leaks. So the column’s wish, that someone of importance be “held for trial,” has in fact been granted. What it has produced so far is instructive, and I will return to it.


**Jack Smith and “Arctic Frost.”** This is the column’s strongest example, and it deserves care. Smith’s team subpoenaed the phone toll records- who called whom, when, and for how long- but not what was said, for nine Republican senators and one House member for the days around January 6, 2021. The members were not told. One carrier, AT&T, resisted the subpoena; Verizon complied. Senator Grassley’s committee has released records it says raise questions about Smith’s candor, and in July 2026 House Judiciary Chairman Jim Jordan referred Smith to the Justice Department for possible false statements to Congress. Smith’s lawyers say his answers were truthful and that the subpoenas were narrow and proper.


I think this raises serious constitutional questions. The Speech or Debate Clause exists so the executive cannot surveil the legislature that oversees it, and secret subpoenas of senators’ records ought to trouble every citizen, no matter which party holds the subpoena pen next. But the column goes further than the evidence. It says the action is “universally agreed” to be illegal; it is not, and that is exactly the question a court must decide. It says Democrats learned whom their Republican opponents were calling during an election cycle. I have found no evidence that any of these records went to any campaign or party. That charge should not be repeated unless someone can show it.


---


## III. Why the perp walk is the wrong remedy


Now to the heart of the matter. Suppose every fact in the column were true. Would the remedy follow?


I say it would not, and I say so for moral reasons before legal ones.


A perp walk is punishment before trial. It is a public display of shame inflicted on a man whom the law still presumes innocent. Scripture is plain that judgment requires evidence weighed in an orderly way: “at the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall the matter be established” (Deuteronomy 19:15). It warns against letting the crowd decide: “Thou shalt not follow a multitude to do evil; neither shalt thou speak in a cause to decline after many to wrest judgment” (Exodus 23:2). And it reminds us why we hear both sides: “He that is first in his own cause seemeth just; but his neighbour cometh and searcheth him” (Proverbs 18:17).


The test I apply to every public act is simple: would my neighbor feel loved by it? Picture a neighbor who votes the other way. Would he feel that he lives under just laws if a president of my party could name his leaders on television, demand their arrest, and have them paraded in chains before any jury had heard a word? And would I feel loved if his party did the same to mine? The column’s own complaint against Jack Smith is that he used prosecutorial power against political opponents. To answer that by demanding the same treatment for Smith is to adopt the very thing being condemned. “Divers weights, and divers measures, both of them are alike abomination to the Lord” (Proverbs 20:10).


The Comey prosecutions illustrate the danger. When indictments follow a president’s public demand rather than the patient weight of evidence, they tend to collapse, as the first Comey case did on the illegality of the prosecutor’s own appointment. And every collapse feeds the opposite grievance: that justice is merely a weapon. The cycle of retaliation does not end in justice. It ends in a nation that trusts no verdict.


There is a better and older remedy, and it belongs to the people. I regard the jury as the cornerstone of American liberty. Twelve citizens, under oath, hear both sides and serve as the people’s voice in the courtroom, holding the final power to refuse an unjust law or an unjust prosecution. That is why I cannot ask for verdicts before juries are seated. If I believe the jury box is where the people rightly exercise their judgment, then I must want every accused man, Hillary Clinton and Jack Smith included, to face one, and I must accept what the jury decides. The people exercise their power in the jury box, not on a cable news feed.


The author ends with a line I fully endorse: “Unpunished lawlessness destroys a nation.” I would only add its twin: so does lawless punishment.


---


## IV. The election alarm


The Robinson newsletter raises concerns that are, in their core, legitimate.


DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin wrote to the White House in August, warning that voting machines with components made by foreign adversaries carry significant security risks and that major vendors use Chinese-made parts. Secretary of War Pete Hegseth issued a memorandum in September directing Cyber Command and the defense intelligence agencies to counter foreign threats to the 2026 elections.


It is also true, and I strongly agree, that Georgia’s QR-code ballots are a real problem. A voter can read the printed names on his ballot, but the machine counts the QR code, which no voter can read. That objection has been raised by Republicans and Democrats alike. Georgia’s own Republican legislature passed a law in 2024 forbidding QR-code tabulation after July 1, 2026, then failed to fund the roughly $66 million needed to replace the equipment, and in a special session pushed the deadline to 2028. The newsletter is right that Georgia’s politicians did not do what they promised. But the ones who did not do it sit in the state legislature.


The newsletter loses proportion in its conclusion. The government being on guard is not evidence that the crime will occur. Installing a smoke detector is not proof of fire. Mullin’s own letter concedes that no evidence shows any vote has been compromised, and the remedy it pushes, random audits of paper ballots, is precisely the right one. Hegseth’s memo names no foreign actor and no jurisdiction, and much of what it orders continues work these agencies were already doing.


Consider the Arizona example. Maricopa County’s ballot vendor, Runbeck Election Services, mailed 3,126 early ballots on October 1 instead of October 7. The county’s Republican recorder, Justin Heap, says the correct ballots went to the right voters, that the county will hold any returned early ballots unopened until processing begins, and that no voter was harmed. The vendor’s chief executive called it human error and apologized. In a county of about 2.5 million registered voters, a pallet sent to the post office six days early is a mistake worth correcting and a vendor worth inspecting. To call it cheating, with no evidence of any wrong vote, is to bear false witness against the clerks and poll workers who run our elections. Many of them are our neighbors. Many are volunteers.


The Georgia letter likewise deserves a fair reading. The State Election Board’s executive director sent it; the board’s chairman opposed the resolution, and two members have said they never approved the letter’s text. Its request concerns ERIC, a multistate consortium that states join to keep voter rolls clean by comparing their data, and it raises a policy dispute worth arguing. It does not accuse the Secretary of State of rigging votes, and the newsletter is wrong to call it that.


Why does this matter so much? Because a people that cries fraud at every error will not be believed when fraud is real. And because a citizen persuaded that his vote cannot count will either stop voting or start looking for some other way to be heard. Neither ends well for a republic.

## V. What I would do


My platform rests on a frame I first used in 2003: Energy, Righteousness, and Accountability. These two pieces belong under Accountability. Here is what I propose, offered as positions I hold with reasons, and I will revise if someone shows me a better argument.


**For equal justice under law:**


- **One standard for the nation’s secrets.** The rules that would have charged a sergeant must apply to every secretary of state and every president, of every party. That includes Hillary Clinton, Joe Biden, and Donald Trump, whose own classified-documents case was dismissed in 2024 on the ground that the special counsel had been unlawfully appointed. If the standard is right, it is right for all of them.

- **Publish the reasoning when senior officials are not charged.** Robert Hur’s report, whatever one thinks of its conclusion, let the public see why the Department declined to prosecute. Every declination involving a cabinet-level official or member of Congress should be explained in writing and released, so that the people, and future juries, can judge.

- **No prosecution by presidential demand.** A president should not publicly call for the indictment of named individuals. Whether by statute or by a binding pledge, prosecutorial decisions about political figures must rest on evidence presented to grand juries, not on the wishes of the man in the Oval Office.

- **Pardon reform.** I would support a constitutional amendment forbidding a president from pardoning himself, his family, or officials in his own administration for conduct in office. The pardon exists for mercy, not for self-protection. The Hunter Biden pardon showed the abuse plainly; so have pardons from the other party.

- **Protect the legislature’s records.** No executive subpoena for a member of Congress’s phone or communication records without federal-court approval and notice to that chamber’s legal counsel.

- **Fully informed juries.** Jurors should be told that they hold the power to acquit, in conscience, when a prosecution is unjust. The jury is the people’s check on the state; it should not be a secret.


**For elections the people can trust:**


- **Count what the voter can read.** Use hand-marked paper ballots, or at minimum count the human-readable text the voter actually verified, never a code he cannot read.

- **Audit by hand, in public, before certification.** Random, statistically sound hand counts of paper ballots, open to observers from every party, as Secretary Mullin’s grant conditions already require.

- **Guard the chain of custody.** Every hand-off of ballots and equipment logged and observed by more than one party.

- **Bring the supply chain home, and fund what you mandate.** Move voting-equipment components to domestic and allied manufacture over time, and never again pass an election mandate, as Georgia did, without the money to carry it out.


---


## VI. Proportion


The author says he is embarrassed by how long it took him to realize he was being played. I honor his honesty. But the way not to be played again is not to demand a bigger show. A perp walk is theater too, only crueler. The way not to be played is to insist on truth told in proportion: to name a real wrong as exactly as large as it is, to demand one standard for everyone, and to trust the people, seated on juries and counting paper ballots in the open, to render judgment.


That is harder than outrage. It is also the only thing that has ever made a nation just.
