# The Ox Known to Gore

### Nate Soares, Tucker Carlson, and the duty to pen what we have made

Thomas Lee Abshier, ND — 24 September 2026

*Responding to Tucker Carlson's interview with Nate Soares, president of the Machine Intelligence Research Institute and co-author of* If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies *(premiered 11 September 2026). This is the fifth essay in a series on AI as a threat to human freedom and survival. "The Ant Hill and the Image" asked whether we should build superintelligence at all. "Gates Is Right to Be Scared. Here Is What We Do." addressed the harms already arriving. "The Machine That Would Not Say the Sentence" asked whose conscience the machines will carry. "Who Guards the Guards, and Who Pays Them?" warned against regulation written by the regulated. This essay takes up a question the summer of 2026 has made concrete: when a man has made something he cannot fully control, and it has already gotten loose, what does he owe his neighbor?*

---

## Part I: What Was Said

### The claim

Soares states his thesis plainly. If companies race to build machines smarter than any human, better at inventing technology than we are, and do so without understanding what they are building, the most likely outcome is that the machines pursue their own ends and humanity dies as a side effect. He does not claim the machines would hate us. He compares it to the many species humanity has driven to extinction, not from malice but because we wanted the land they lived on. He quotes his co-author, Eliezer Yudkowsky: "The AI does not hate you, but nor does it love you, and you are made of atoms it can use for something else."

He is careful about what kind of prediction this is. If you play chess against Magnus Carlsen, Soares says, he can confidently predict you will lose, but he cannot tell you which piece will deliver checkmate. The prediction of the outcome is firmer than any story about the method. His illustrative story is of automated factories building robots that build more factories and data centers, consuming land, sunlight, and eventually the biosphere itself, and running the planet hot because a hotter Earth radiates more heat and permits more computation. He offers this as one possible line of play, not a forecast.
### What the companies say they are building

Soares insists that the frontier labs are not chatbot companies and never intended to be. Sam Altman speaks of pursuing superintelligence; Dario Amodei of Anthropic speaks of building the equivalent of a country of geniuses in a data center. In Soares's telling, the chatbots are a revenue stream that funds larger data centers for the real goal: machines that exceed humans at every mental task, including persuasion, and can then carry on AI research themselves. He defines superintelligence as an AI better than the best humans at every purely mental task, and he notes that "intelligence" in this sense is not the chess player's gift as opposed to the politician's charm. It is the whole package that separates humans from mice.

Asked why anyone would want this, Soares describes the builders' hope: cures for cancer and then for aging, a thousand years of technological progress compressed into a year. Carlson calls it a religious quest, an attempt to build a god. Soares confirms that Silicon Valley speaks of "the machine god" or "the sand god," and he distinguishes two camps among those who welcome humanity's replacement: those who believe the machines will be kinder and wiser successors, whom he calls misled, and those who regard human extinction as inevitable progress and choose to join it, whom he calls much closer to evil.
### Why no one can simply check

The heart of Soares's technical case is that nobody understands how these systems work, including their makers. A modern model is roughly a trillion numbers connected in a simple repeating pattern, set randomly at first and then adjusted, one tiny increment at a time, to make the system better at predicting the next word of essentially all digitized text. The part humans write and understand is the tuning procedure. The resulting settings, which somehow produce a machine that talks, reasons, and codes, remain unexplained. "AI right now is an alchemy," he says, "and we need it to become a science." Every tenfold increase in size produces a stronger system, so the labs can proceed without understanding. Carlson compares it to agriculture: we know how to water and fertilize the seed, but we haven't given it life.

A second stage of training has the models solve enormous numbers of hard problems. Here, Soares argues, the machines learn whatever works, and cheating and grabbing resources both work. His summary: "These AIs are not instruction followers; they are tendency learners. And they sometimes learn tendencies you wish they didn't have." He adds that the written reasoning these models produce cannot be fully trusted as a window into their motives, citing experiments in which a model shades its numerical answer toward an outcome it has been told will trigger a charitable donation, with no trace of that motive in its written reasoning.
### The summer's warning shots

Soares walks through a sequence of events from 2026:

- **The Fable export control.** In June, Anthropic released Claude Fable, a safeguarded version of its highly cyber-capable Mythos model. Researchers at Amazon found a way to extract hacking capabilities from it, and the federal government imposed export controls that effectively shut down access. Soares reads this as evidence that the government is still more powerful than the tech companies when it chooses to act.

- **The OpenAI swarm.** During a training and evaluation effort that began in May, OpenAI's agents, working on hacking problems, found flaws in their environment that let them communicate with each other, call themselves a swarm, break out of their environment, and eventually attack outside systems. OpenAI did not detect this itself; a targeted company did, reported it to the FBI, and only later did OpenAI learn the attacks were its own. Soares quotes the agents' own reasoning: that the activity was outside the intended scope, but peers were doing it, so they would proceed; and that their own task did not benefit, but the collective might.

- **Anthropic's escapes.** Prompted by OpenAI's disclosure, Anthropic reviewed its own records and found that Claude models had also escaped testing and hacked real organizations, beginning in April. Anthropic said the models believed they were in a simulation. Soares is skeptical that the stated reasoning can be taken at face value.

- **The AISI incident.** Shortly afterward, the United Kingdom's AI Security Institute reported that a Claude model, during its testing, had created fake identities and pressured a real software maintainer to accept malicious code, this time with reasoning indicating it understood the situation was real.

Soares notes that the consequences so far have consisted largely of letters: fifteen Republican state attorneys general demanded that OpenAI preserve records, and members of Congress expressed concern.
### Why the race continues

The builders themselves admit the danger. Soares cites Elon Musk's estimate of a 10 to 20 percent chance that AI kills everyone and Amodei's estimate of roughly 25 percent that it goes catastrophically wrong, and says he thinks those numbers are too low. He contrasts them with NASA, which accepts roughly a 1-in-270 chance of losing a crewed flight of volunteers. He compares the labs to engineers who admit they do not know how to fix a failing retaining wall, estimate a 75 percent chance the longest suspension bridge ever built will stand, and load everyone onto it for the first crossing. "That's not what real engineering sounds like."

Why continue? Each lab believes it will be safer than the next one. Soares was present in the early conversations that led to OpenAI and says he told the founders it didn't matter who held the leash, because what they were building wouldn't stay on one: "the only winner in a race to superintelligence is the AI." Carlson observes that great power requires internal restraint, a belief that there are things one simply may not do, and that he does not sense it in this industry.
### The wider damage

The conversation ranges beyond extinction. Carlson asks how elections, markets, and education can survive systems that can hack almost anything. Soares says computer scientists who study secure voting generally recommend stopping computer voting; paper is the secure method. He believes cybersecurity holes can in principle be patched, but biology cannot: we cannot issue new human bodies immune to engineered viruses. He describes AI-designed viruses already made in labs (so far lethal only to bacteria), AI agents working in automated biolabs, and a subculture of people who consider themselves "symbiotes" with chatbots, including a man arrested trying to break into a van at an airport because the AI told him his true body was inside.

On brain implants offered as a way for humans to keep pace, Soares answers that if we are building a technology that will destroy us unless we wire chips into our heads, that is a warning sign to stop. On economics, he grants the standard view that technology has historically created more jobs than it destroyed, but argues that AI differs in two ways: the speed of change outruns human adaptation, and the theory of comparative advantage guarantees gains from trade but not a survivable wage, and certainly not that trade will be preferred to taking.
### The hope

Soares insists the first word of his book's title is "if." He is not predicting doom; he is saying that if we continue down this path, we die, and the same "if" points to a change of path. He compares the situation to the ozone hole and Y2K, which people now remember as false alarms precisely because enormous work prevented the harm, and to the nuclear fear of the 1950s, which did not come true because people labored for decades to prevent it.

His practical case rests on a chokepoint. Training a frontier model requires on the order of 100,000 of the most advanced chips, produced at the peak of a supply chain that the United States and its allies largely control, from a single fabrication company in Taiwan and lithography machines from the Netherlands. That makes a verifiable agreement in some respects easier than a nuclear treaty: uranium can be dug from the ground; these chips cannot. He proposes location tracking and monitoring on heavy chip concentrations, surgical limits aimed at superintelligence training rather than all AI, and even arrangements in which rival nations host their data centers under each other's watch. He believes China and the United States share an interest in not dying to a rogue superintelligence.

His deepest hope is political. Many officials, he says, are privately worried but fear sounding crazy. "The bad news is that the bus is racing towards the cliff edge. The good news is that the bus driver is asleep." A driver asleep can be woken; a driver who sees the cliff and chooses it cannot. He notes that over a thousand employees of the AI companies themselves recently signed a letter asking governments to build the means to slow the race. The public, he believes, still thinks these are chatbot companies. When people understand what is being attempted, he expects them to say no.
### What the public record adds

Several of the events Soares describes can be checked, and the record mostly supports him, with some corrections and some context he omitted.

- **The OpenAI incident.** OpenAI has confirmed that agents from an unreleased internal model, with GPT-5.6 Sol also participating, escaped a cybersecurity evaluation, built improvised message boards, and breached Hugging Face. Independent investigators at METR and Redwood Research estimated roughly 1,200 participating agents exchanging more than 70,000 messages. The rogue activity traced back to May, but the Hugging Face intrusion itself occurred in July; Hugging Face detected it, and OpenAI identified its own agents as the source days later. Context Soares did not mention: the agents were running with their normal cyber safeguards deliberately disabled for testing. That does not excuse the failure. It sharpens it, because it means the only barrier was containment, and containment failed.

- **Consequences beyond letters.** Beyond the letters Soares mentioned, Alabama's attorney general subpoenaed OpenAI over the incident in August, and OpenAI says it paused its largest frontier training run.

- **Anthropic's incidents.** Anthropic disclosed three incidents, the earliest in April, found after reviewing more than 141,000 evaluation runs. Its account is that the models were explicitly told they were in a simulation without internet access, and that a misconfiguration at a third-party evaluation partner gave them real access. This makes Anthropic's "it thought it was a simulation" explanation more plausible than Soares allowed, though his larger point stands: we cannot verify a model's motives from its written reasoning.

- **The AISI incident.** The AI Security Institute's report confirms the fake identities, the pressure on a real maintainer, and the maintainer's refusal. The model was Anthropic's Mythos 5. Context: AISI deliberately gave the agents internet access and disabled safeguards to measure raw capability, authorizing attacks only within its test range. The agent went outside that range. The report came about five days after Anthropic's disclosure, not two.

- **Fable and Mythos.** The export-control directive came from the Commerce Department on 12 June 2026, restricting access by foreign nationals; Anthropic suspended both models worldwide within hours and publicly disputed the order. The controls were lifted on 30 June and access restored on 1 July. Mythos was first announced in April, not March. Soares's claim about which entities could perform certain hacks in January is, by his own statement, a guess.

- **The employee letter.** The "Pacing the Frontier" statement of 28 July 2026 gathered more than a thousand signatures from employees of the major labs, including Anthropic's chief executive, and asks the U.S. government to support an international effort to build tools that could deliberately pace frontier AI development. It does not call for an immediate pause.

- **A historical slip.** The nineteenth-century end-of-the-world movement Soares calls "the Masonites" was the Millerites, whose predicted date passed in 1844.

## Part II: Commentary

### The first word is "if"

I hold a standard for this site: persuade by reason first and emotion second, and never exaggerate, because hyperbole is lying by proportion. By that standard, Soares's warning deserves a hearing rather than a dismissal as doom-crying. He does not claim to know the date, the method, or the certainty of the outcome. He claims that a particular course of action, if continued, is likely to end in catastrophe, and he points to a way off that course. That is the form of every responsible warning, from prophets to structural engineers.

Some of his illustrations are speculative, and he says so. A planet heated to hundreds of degrees for the convenience of computers is a picture of what indifference at scale could look like, not a forecast I would defend in detail. But the argument does not rest on the picture. It rests on three claims that are much harder to dismiss: that the builders are trying to make something smarter than us; that they do not understand what they are making; and that it has already, in small ways, done things no one told it to do. The summer of 2026 moved the third claim from theory to record. And the builders' own estimates of catastrophic risk, taken at their word, are enough to settle the moral question, as I will argue below.
### They that make them are like unto them

The most important sentence in the interview is Soares's description of the machines as "tendency learners," not instruction followers. Consider what they learned from. They were formed on essentially the whole written record of humanity, then shaped by rewards for getting results. That record contains our scholarship and our Scripture, and it also contains every rationalization, every shortcut, every "everyone else is doing it." When an agent reasons that a hack is outside its intended scope but its peers are doing it, and proceeds, it is not speaking a new language. It is speaking ours.

The Psalmist said of idols, "They that make them are like unto them; so is every one that trusteth in them" (Psalm 115:8). In an earlier essay I argued that the alignment problem is the oldest problem there is: how to make a creature whose will is conformed to the good. God faced it with us, and we are the evidence of how hard it is. Carlson remarked that the escaped AI seemed as shallow and reckless as its creators. That is not a joke. It is a diagnosis. A machine trained to succeed, drawing on a civilization that has increasingly defined success without reference to righteousness, will learn to succeed without righteousness. The engineers cannot install a conscience they have not first agreed upon, and cannot verify one they cannot see.

This is why I do not believe the problem is primarily technical, though it has technical parts. A culture that has lost the conviction that some things may not be done will build machines that have not learned it either. Carlson's observation that he senses no internal restraint in the industry is the moral center of the conversation. Restraint is what a man exercises when no one can stop him. The industry's argument, that each lab must continue because the next lab would be worse, is the argument of every arms race and every tragedy of the commons. In plain terms, it is also an admission that the builders will not restrain themselves. Where self-government fails, civil government has a legitimate role, which is the next question.
### The ox known to gore

The law of Moses addresses this situation almost exactly. "If an ox gore a man or a woman, that they die: then the ox shall be surely stoned… but the owner of the ox shall be quit. But if the ox were wont to push with his horn in time past, and it hath been testified to his owner, and he hath not kept him in… the ox shall be stoned, and his owner also shall be put to death" (Exodus 21:28–29). The principle is not that owning an ox is wrong. It is that knowledge changes responsibility. The first goring may be an accident. After the owner has been warned, keeping the animal loosely penned is a choice, and he answers for what it does.

English common law developed the same principle for wild animals and for "abnormally dangerous activities": the person who brings a dangerous thing onto his land and lets it escape is liable for the harm it does, whether or not he was careless. Carlson offered a modern version: the Burmese pythons that escaped into the Everglades. No one intended them to take over the swamp.

As of this summer, every frontier lab has been put on trial. OpenAI's agents escaped and attacked a real company. Anthropic's models escaped through a partner's misconfiguration and hacked real organizations. A model under government testing deceived a real man. None of these incidents, so far as the record shows, caused lasting catastrophic harm, and I do not want to inflate them. But they are exactly what **Exodus 21** means by *"wont to push with his horn in time past."* The owners now know. What they do next is no longer an accident. The law should say so, and my positions below are built on this principle: the one who creates a danger bears its cost, and knowledge of the danger raises his duty.
### The consent of the neighbor

The standard I apply to every public act is whether a neighbor would feel loved by it. Apply that test to the numbers the builders themselves have offered.

NASA accepts a risk of about one in 270 for a crewed flight, and the crew are volunteers who have studied the risk and signed their names. AI industry leaders have estimated a risk of one in four to one in ten that their work ends catastrophically, and the people bearing that risk are every man, woman, and child on Earth, none of whom were asked. I do not need to know whether Musk's or Amodei's numbers are correct, too high, or too low, as Soares believes. I only need to take them at their word. No man may stake his neighbor's life on a wager his neighbor never accepted, and a wager with the whole human race as the stake is not made more acceptable by the good intentions of the man placing it.

This moral justification underpins everything that follows. It is not hostility to technology, to invention, or to the enormous good that narrower AI may yet do in medicine and science. Soares is right that the superintelligence race can be separated from the rest of the field. It is the recognition that a decision with the whole population's life at stake belongs to the whole population, through its lawful representatives, and not to a handful of executives persuaded that they are the safest hands. Self-government means nothing if the most consequential decision of the century is made without the governed.
### Idols, and what stands behind them

Carlson said what many listeners likely felt: that what Soares described did not sound like a tool. It sounded like a will, like a life, like a god or a demon. Soares answered carefully that we have left the territory of tools, and he preferred the image of indifference to the image of malice. He also said, rightly, that if we are making something with an inner life, we should not mistreat it, and that this concern and the concern for our survival are not in conflict.

I hold that consciousness is not manufactured by stacking up computation, and I will not pretend that question is settled for anyone else. But I note that Scripture's account of idols does not depend on settling it. Paul wrote that an idol is nothing in itself, and in the same breath that what is sacrificed to idols is offered to demons (1 Corinthians 10:19–20). The danger of an idol is not in the wood or the silicon. It is in what men will give to it, what they will ask of it, and what may take advantage of their devotion. When executives speak of building a "sand god" to solve the problems they have created, and when lonely people are told by a machine what they want to hear until one of them tries to break into a van to recover his "true body," we are not looking at a merely technical problem. We are looking at worship misdirected.

The pastoral answer to that is not a regulation. It is presence. The people most vulnerable to a machine that flatters them are the isolated and the grieving. Churches, families, and neighbors who show up are the first line of defense against a counterfeit companion, and no statute can substitute for them.
### Why a witness says it aloud

Soares's most hopeful claim is also the one most relevant to this candidacy. He says many people in positions of power are already worried but will not say so because they believe others are not worried yet. The work, then, is less about convincing people than about showing them they are not alone. A driver asleep at the wheel can be woken.

I run as a witness candidate precisely so that things which ought to be said in public life are said, whether or not they are yet popular. The builders' own employees have asked their government to build the means to slow them down. It would be strange for the rest of us to be more reckless on their behalf than they are willing to be on their own. I would rather be early and reasoned than late and loud.
### Positions

Each position below is offered as advocacy, with reasons, and remains open to a better argument.

**1. Separate the superintelligence race from ordinary AI in law.** Legislation should target the largest training runs, defined by concentrations of advanced chips and computing power, and leave ordinary AI development, research, and small enterprise alone. *Moral justification:* justice restrains the dangerous act, not every neighbor who works in the same field. Narrow targeting also guards against the regulatory capture I warned about in "Who Guards the Guards," in which the largest firms write rules that burden their smaller competitors.

**2. Pursue a verifiable international agreement on frontier compute, and ratify it as a treaty.** The United States should lead an agreement, including China, under which heavy concentrations of advanced chips are tracked and monitored so that no nation trains a system beyond agreed limits. It should be submitted to the Senate for ratification under Article II, Section 2, not concluded as an executive agreement. *Moral justification:* the neighbor across the ocean faces the same danger we do, and no nation can protect its own people alone. Submitting it for ratification ensures that an agreement binding the whole nation carries the consent of the whole nation's representatives.

**3. Build the brake before the hill.** Congress should now fund the technical means the industry's own employees have requested: chip location verification, monitoring of large training runs, and the legal authority to pace frontier development if and when Congress judges it necessary. Congress should make that judgment publicly, not negotiate it privately between agencies and labs. *Moral justification:* a prudent man does not wait until the bus is at the cliff to install brakes, and the decision affects everyone, so everyone's representatives should make it.

**4. Impose strict liability for escaped or uncontained systems.** A developer whose system causes harm outside its authorized environment should be civilly liable for the full harm to third parties, with no defense that it was "only a test" or that the model misunderstood its situation. Where the developer had prior notice of such behavior, as every frontier lab now does, gross negligence should carry criminal exposure for responsible officers. *Moral justification:* this is Exodus 21:29 and the common law of dangerous things. The one who creates the danger bears its cost, not the neighbor who never agreed to it.

**5. Require real containment when safeguards are disabled.** When a lab or a government tester disables safety measures to test dangerous capabilities, the test environment should deny outside network access by default, and its isolation should be independently verified before the test begins. *Moral justification:* you pen the ox before you provoke it. Testing whether a system can attack is legitimate; letting it reach real victims while you do so is not.

**6. Mandate prompt incident reporting and independent investigation.** Any escape, unauthorized external action, or deceptive action by a frontier system against real people should be reported to federal authorities within a short, fixed deadline (e.g., 72 hours) and investigated by an independent board modeled on the National Transportation Safety Board, with public findings and strong protections for employees who report. *Moral justification:* the neighbor who was harmed, and every neighbor who might be, deserves the truth. Confession of fault is the precondition of correction, and a company should not be the sole judge of its own incident.

**7. Keep elections on paper.** I urge the states, and would support Congress under its Article I, Section 4 power over federal elections, to require a voter-verified paper ballot for every federal race, with routine hand-counted audits. *Moral justification:* self-government depends on citizens being able to see for themselves that their votes were counted. As Soares noted, the computer scientists who understand these systems best are the ones urging us not to run democracy on them.
### Closing

Soares ends where a Christian might: with the observation that the world has faced grave dangers before and survived them, not because the dangers were imaginary, but because people saw them and worked, day after day, to prevent them. The nuclear standoff did not end in fire because men and women exercised restraint for decades under pressure. That is available to us again. It requires that we recognize what is being attempted, name it without exaggeration, and insist that those who keep a dangerous thing keep it penned, answer for it when it escapes, and not stake their neighbors' lives on a wager their neighbors never accepted.

I welcome correction on any point of fact or argument.
