# 261121 - What Will Make Iran Surrender

# One Purse: Why Iran Will Not Surrender, and What Will Make It Stop


### A Response to Eric Garland on the Network Behind the Regime

*A position paper by Thomas Lee Abshier, ND* | *21 November 2026*

## Plain language summary

Iran has lost its air force, its navy, its Supreme Leader, and most of its oil income. People ask why it has not given up. Eric Garland gives a good answer: Iran is not only a country. It is the hub of a business network that runs through China, Hong Kong, the Emirates, Turkey, Iraq, Oman, Malaysia, and Russia. That network sells oil, launders money, builds drones, and hires killers, and the men in it make money doing so. As long as the network works, the regime feels it can still fight. Garland says the United States should take the network apart, piece by piece, with sanctions and prosecutions, including the Hong Kong and Chinese banks that have been spared so far.

I agree, and I think this is the most righteous part of the war. It aims at the men who knowingly profit from murder, not at the Iranian people. But I add four things. First, the money is not the deepest bond. The regime's belief is, and only the Gospel answers a belief. Second, the pressure must not fall on the sick and the hungry. Medicine and bread must actually get through, not only on paper. Third, if we go after China's banks, we must count the cost and tell Americans what it will be. Fourth, the war needs a clear endpoint: inspectors everywhere and the enriched uranium shipped out. When that one condition is met, the strikes end. But that is an armistice, not a true surrender. The regime would still have the knowledge and the will to build a bomb and to spread its creed by force. Real peace comes only when hearts change, and that is the work of the Gospel.

## Where things stand

Nine months after the first strikes of 28 February, the conventional war is largely won and the political war is not. Iran's navy and air force have been destroyed, the American blockade has stopped most of its crude from reaching market, and on 24 August the Treasury opened what it calls Operation Economic Outcast, a campaign of secondary sanctions against anyone who does business with Tehran. The United Arab Emirates halted all trade and financial dealings with Iran in August after attacks on its tankers. In September Turkey revoked the license of Bank Mellat's Istanbul branch and stopped Mahan Air's flights.

This week a third American aircraft carrier joined the forces in the region, and the Treasury Secretary predicted that Iran's economy will shrink by more than a quarter in a year. Bread rationing is reported in two provinces. Yet Tehran's answer is unchanged. Its officials say the Strait of Hormuz stays closed until Washington accepts their seven conditions: an end to the war on every front, an end to the blockade and the sanctions, the release of frozen funds, compensation, and more. Iran's Assembly of Experts has called for a review of its defense doctrine, which opens the door to reversing its stated ban on nuclear weapons. Its trade officials claim six months of stockpiled basic goods.

That is the puzzle Garland sets out to solve.

## Garland's argument

Garland's case, in my words, is this. Nations that surrender nearly always do so under invasion, occupation, or the loss of territory. Only Serbia in 1999 gave up under bombing alone, and it did so because it had one patron, Russia, and Russia told it to quit. Iran has two anchors, Russia and China, and neither commands it. So no one will surrender on the mullahs' behalf.

More important, Iran is a hybrid: a state with an army, and the center of a transnational illicit economy. Chinese refineries in Shandong buy its sanctioned crude. Dubai and Fujairah settle the accounts. Hong Kong forms the shell companies. Istanbul banks them. Basra supplies false paperwork so Iranian oil can be sold as Iraqi. Tatarstan builds the drones. Tankers swap cargo off Johor, Malaysia. Oman is the door to Yemen. And the regime's hired violence reaches Stockholm, Brooklyn, Sydney, and Maryland. Garland estimates the whole enterprise at about $50 billion a year, close to a tenth of Iran's economy.

The bombs destroyed the state's hardware. They did far less to the overseas network, and the network is what Iran has always used to strike the West. Garland suggests that the regime will not negotiate seriously until it believes it can no longer strike back. So, he argues, Washington should take that ability away: designate the Hong Kong formation agents and at least one major Chinese bank, and prosecute Iran's agents abroad, using recent cases as the template.

## Where I stand

I stand with Garland on the diagnosis and on the main remedy. The war against the network is the most righteous front in this war, and it should be pressed with the same resolve the military brought to the first sixteen days.

My reason is moral before it is strategic. A bomb falls on whoever is near the target. A designation falls on the man who chose to put his name on the shell company, his bank on the wire, his tanker under a false flag. The financier who launders money for a regime that hangs its own citizens and hires assassins to kill Americans is not a bystander. Scripture describes him exactly:

*"My son, if sinners entice thee, consent thou not. If they say, Come with us, let us lay wait for blood ... Cast in thy lot among us; let us all have one purse"* **(Proverbs 1:10–11, 14)**.

That is the network: one purse, shared by men who lie in wait for blood. And Scripture names the man who profits from such a partnership without lifting a knife himself: *"When thou sawest a thief, then thou consentedst with him"* **(Psalm 50:18)**. The trader in Hong Kong who forms the shell, the clerk in Fujairah who clears the payment, the captain who turns off his transponder off Johor have each consented. Cutting them off from the dollar is the restraint of evildoers that civil authority is given to carry out **(Romans 13:4)**. It is also the most precise weapon we have. It reaches the guilty and spares the innocent, which no bomb can promise.

Garland's description of the regime as *"closer to a crime family run by religious mob bosses"* fits what I have argued for months. And his observation about alignment matters: anyone who does business with men who lead chants of "Death to America" has at least made peace with a world that holds less of America. Money buys the network's cooperation. Agreement with its aims keeps the cooperation willing.

## The bond beneath the money

Here I would carry Garland's argument one step further.

He suggests that the network may be the psychological foundation of the regime's confidence, and that destroying it will both disarm the mullahs and break their spirit. I believe it will disarm them. I am less sure it will break their spirit, because the network is not the deepest thing that holds them together.

The middlemen in Dubai and Shenzhen are in it for profit. Take away the profit and many will walk away; the Emirates already have. But the men at the center are not in it for profit. They believe they are building God's dominion over the earth, and they have shown, from the boys sent across the minefields in the 1980s to the hungry queuing for bread today, that they will spend their own people to keep that dream alive. A man who believes his cause is holy is not demoralized by a frozen account. He is confirmed in his sense of martyrdom.

So the network war removes their means. It cannot remove their motive. Only a better belief displaces a false one, and that is the work of the Gospel, carried to the Persian people by Persian believers, as I set out in "Winning the Long War with Iran." *"Not by might, nor by power, but by my spirit, saith the LORD of hosts"* **(Zechariah 4:6)**. The Treasury can starve the regime's machinery. It cannot give the regime's soldiers a reason to lay down their arms. The truth about God can.

The same is true at home. Garland notes that some in Tehran may have been waiting on our elections. They were, and the midterms gave them reason to hope that we will tire first. A sanctions campaign is a long campaign. Garland himself says it cannot be rushed and must be sustained. Whether it is sustained depends on the will of the American people, and that will depends on what we believe is worth enduring for. A nation that has lost its sense of righteousness cannot hold a siege for long.

## What I would do

**1. Name the nodes, and show the evidence.** Every designation should be public, specific, and tied to evidence a reasonable man can weigh: this company, this transaction, this tanker. Accountability, the A in The New ERA Vision, applies to our own government as much as to the regime. A sanctions list that names the guilty and explains why persuades the honest banker to walk away. A list that seems arbitrary teaches every bank to treat every Iranian as radioactive, which hurts the wrong people.

**2. Close the Hong Kong channel, in stages, with the cost counted.** Garland is right that Hong Kong is the soft spot: the largest share of the shadow banking FinCEN has traced ran through Hong Kong shell companies holding accounts at Chinese banks, and no major Chinese bank has yet been designated. I would act in this order: first the formation agents whose shells carry the oil trade; then a published deadline for named Chinese banks to close the accounts, as the Treasury already gives every other country; then designation of any bank that refuses. But designating a major Chinese bank is not a small step. It could bring retaliation against American companies, higher prices, and a confrontation with a nuclear power. *"What king, going to make war against another king, sitteth not down first, and consulteth whether he be able"* **(Luke 14:31)**. I believe the step is justified if Beijing will not act. A president who takes it should tell the people in advance what it may cost them, and should leave Beijing a door: close the accounts, and the matter ends.

**3. Prosecute the hired killers as criminals, because they are.** The regime's violence abroad does not depend on oil money the way its drone program does. A murder can be bought cheaply. That is why law enforcement matters as much as Treasury. This month the FBI added an IRGC member accused of plotting to kill John Bolton to its most wanted terrorists list. Each such case, tried in open court with full due process, does three things: it removes a killer, it exposes the network that hired him, and it shows the world that we answer murder with law, not with murder.

**4. Cut the money, not the bread.** This is the condition I hold to most firmly. Reports from Iran describe shortages of hundreds of medicines, prices for basic drugs several times what they were, and patients abandoning treatment. American law permits the sale of food and medicine to Iran, but a legal sale means little when no bank will process the payment and no shipper will take the risk. A siege that empties pharmacies falls on the Iranian father whose daughter needs insulin, not on the Guard commander who has his own supply. Scripture forbids punishing the son for the father's sin **(Ezekiel 18:20)**, and it surely forbids punishing the captive for his jailer's. So alongside every new designation, the Treasury should name and protect at least one trusted channel, with full transparency, through which food and medicine payments can actually clear. That is not softness. It denies the regime its favorite propaganda, and it shows every Iranian which government is starving him.

**5. Define the armistice in one sentence, and do not mistake it for peace.** Garland asks why Iran will not surrender. Part of the answer is that we have not told it plainly what we require. Is it regime change? Hormuz open? An end to enrichment? A shifting list invites bargaining. I proposed one condition in my earlier paper, and I repeat it here: total, permanent access for inspectors to every site, and the 60 percent enriched uranium dug out, counted, and shipped out of the country. Meet that condition, and the strikes stop and relief begins in verified stages. The network war is the lever that makes the condition look better to Tehran than the alternative. Without a condition, the lever has nothing to move.

But I must be honest about what that condition buys. It is the right endpoint for the conventional war. It is not a full surrender. Letting inspectors in does not make the mullahs give up the knowledge of how to build a bomb, or the will to dominate the world with their creed. What follows is an unstable balance between our watchfulness and their hidden resistance. And that balance favors the side with the deeper devotion. On their side is religious fervor; on ours, for the most part, only a wish for safety and quiet. Over decades, fervor outlasts fatigue. A complete surrender, and an actual peace, comes only when the men who rule Iran, and those who enforce their rule, have given their hearts to Jesus as Lord and renounced the god of their domination. That is a spiritual campaign, and I take it up in a companion essay.

**6. Take away the reason the network exists.** The illicit oil network exists because the world runs on Gulf oil and will pay for it through any channel. The lasting cure is abundant energy, the E in The New ERA Vision: conventional nuclear plants built now, and thorium molten-salt reactors brought to operation. When the world no longer needs the regime's crude, the purse is empty for good.

## The moral test

**Is it righteous?** Cutting off men who knowingly finance terror and the pursuit of a bomb, and prosecuting the killers they hire, is the restraint of evildoers that God assigns to civil government. Doing so with evidence and due process is just. Starving the sick by design, or punishing a man for his nationality rather than his acts, is not, and this plan forbids both.

**Is it loving?** It is loving to the American families the regime has targeted on our own soil, to the Gulf sailors and neighbors under its fire, and to the Iranian people, who gain nothing from the regime's oil money and suffer most from its rule. *"Have no fellowship with the unfruitful works of darkness, but rather reprove them"* **(Ephesians 5:11)**. Reproof, applied to the man who chose the purse, is a call to him as well: walk away, and the sanction can be lifted.

**Would I want it done to me?** If I were a businessman in Dubai who had been laundering money for murderers, I would want to be stopped before I went further down that road, and I would want to know exactly what I must do to be restored. If I were an Iranian father, I would want the world to break the grip of the men who rule me without emptying my pharmacy, and I would want to hear the truth about God from my own countrymen. This plan aims at both.

## The strongest objection

The strongest case against this approach is that sanctions rarely topple a determined regime. Cuba, North Korea, and the Islamic Republic itself have endured them for decades. The burden falls first on ordinary people, as the medicine reports show. Iran claims six months of stockpiled goods and new overland routes to the north. And going after Chinese banks risks a confrontation far larger than the one we are in.

I take each point seriously. My answer is that this is not the sanctions regime of the past. For the first time, financial pressure is joined to a naval blockade that stops the cargo, and the participants in the network now face two risks at once: losing the ship and losing the bank. Two of Garland's six hubs, the Emirates and Turkey, have already changed course. The burden on ordinary Iranians is real, and it is why I make the food and medicine channel a condition, not an afterthought. As for China, I would rather confront Beijing's banks with a deadline than leave the regime a permanent paymaster. But I would do it openly, in stages, and with the cost told.

Most of all, I do not claim that money alone will end this. The network war removes the regime's means. The Gospel addresses its motive. And our own repentance supplies the endurance to see both through. *"Righteousness exalteth a nation: but sin is a reproach to any people"* **(Proverbs 14:34)**.

I hold this position open to a better argument.

## Sources and their limits

This paper leans on Eric Garland's article and on mainstream reporting (Reuters, AP, Al Jazeera, Boston Globe, Euronews, SCMP, CNN, Iran International, The National, Stars and Stripes) and on U.S. Treasury and FinCEN publications. Official statements from Washington and Tehran are the testimony of interested parties and are treated that way: the Treasury Secretary's forecast of a 25 percent contraction and Iran's claim of six months of stockpiles are both claims, not findings. Garland's $45 billion and $50 billion figures are his own estimates from open sources; I have not verified them. FinCEN's finding of about $9 billion in Iranian shadow banking in 2024 is confirmed in reporting on its October 2025 analysis; Garland's figure that 49 percent of it originated in Hong Kong comes from his reading of FinCEN data, which I have not checked line by line. Reports of medicine shortages come largely from Iranian officials and patients quoted by foreign outlets working under Iranian press restrictions, and from outlets with their own editorial leanings on the war. Iranian and Chinese perspectives reach me mostly through English-language and Western-curated reporting, and that curation may hide what officials in Tehran and Beijing actually intend.
