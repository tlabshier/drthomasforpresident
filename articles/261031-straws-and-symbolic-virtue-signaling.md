# 261031 - Straws and Symbolic Virtue Signaling

# The Straw and the Beam:


### What a Korean Study Teaches About Symbolic Environmental Policy


*Dr. Thomas Lee Abshier, ND  |  31 October 2026*


A drinking straw is a small thing. That is exactly why it makes a good case study. When a government spends years of political energy forcing a switch from plastic straws to paper ones, it is fair to ask whether the switch did any good. A study commissioned by South Korea’s own environment ministry has now answered that question, and the answer is largely no.


I stand with those who say the paper-straw mandate was symbolic policy that did not deliver what it promised. But the lesson goes beyond straws.


## What the study found


South Korea’s Ministry of Climate, Energy and Environment commissioned the Korean Society for Life Cycle Assessment to compare four kinds of disposable straws: conventional plastic (polypropylene), paper, biodegradable plastic, and “bio-PE,” a polyethylene made from plants such as sugarcane. The work ran from May 2025 to April 2026, and lawmaker Kim Wi-sang made it public on October 21. A life-cycle assessment follows a product from raw materials through manufacture, use, and disposal, and measures its burden across many categories. This one used sixteen.


Conventional plastic straws had the smallest impact in ten of the sixteen categories, including climate change, acidification, fine particulate matter, and human toxicity. Their only worst-place finish was fossil-resource depletion, which is unsurprising for a petroleum product.


Compared with plastic, paper straws scored about 6.3 times higher on the modeled carcinogenic-impact measure and about twice as high on acidification. Biodegradable plastic straws scored about 3.3 times higher on carcinogenic impact. Bio-PE straws scored worst of all, about 16 times higher on carcinogenic impact and nearly 6 times higher on acidification, and ranked last in ten categories.


The study also found that, in practice, straws of every material are nearly impossible to recycle. They are too small for sorting equipment, and coatings and glues defeat recycling even where it is technically possible. In South Korea, about three-quarters of discarded straws are incinerated, and the rest are landfilled.


## What the study did not find


Here the popular coverage, including the Breitbart article that prompted this essay, runs ahead of the evidence.


The “6.33 times the carcinogenic impact” figure does **not** mean that drinking through a paper straw gives you six times the cancer risk. It is a modeled estimate of cancer-related effects from emissions across the whole life cycle: pulping, chemical treatment, energy use, and burning or burying the waste. The researchers and the Korean press were explicit on this point. Separate studies have found PFAS (“forever chemicals”) in many paper and bamboo straws, which is a real and distinct concern, but that is not what this number measures.


The study also did not conclude that plastic straws are good for the environment. The researchers recommended that shops stop handing out disposable straws of any kind by default and offer one only on request. They concluded that banning plastic straws alone, while allowing every other disposable straw, was not appropriate. Kim Wi-sang concluded that government should reduce disposable use rather than fixate on substitute materials.


So the honest summary is this: the substitute was not better than the thing it replaced, and in most measured categories it was worse. The best option was using fewer straws.


## Why the concern remains plausible


Some will say a single study from one country should not settle anything. That is a fair caution, and one study is not the last word. But there are good reasons to believe this result is not a fluke.


First, it agrees with what most life-cycle work on single-use items has shown for years: lightweight plastics often carry a lower manufacturing and transport burden than heavier paper, glass, or plant-based substitutes, because paper requires more energy, water, and chemical processing per item. A paper straw that must be thick, glued, and coated to survive a drink is not a simple object.


Second, the substitute materials sound natural but behave industrially. Bio-PE is chemically identical to ordinary polyethylene; it does not biodegrade. A label that suggests otherwise misleads the buyer.


Third, the incentives favored symbolism. A straw ban is cheap for government, visible to voters, and lets officials claim action without confronting harder problems. Paper-straw suppliers had a commercial interest in the mandate. Neither fact proves the policy wrong, but they explain why it was adopted without first asking the life-cycle question. And every consumer who held a collapsing paper straw sensed something was off, an intuition the data now supports.


## The larger lesson


Jesus warned against straining at the small fault in a brother’s eye while ignoring the beam in one’s own (Matthew 7:3–5). Environmental policy too often strains at straws while the beam goes untouched.


Plastic straws were never a significant share of plastic in the ocean. Most marine plastic comes from fishing gear and from mismanaged waste in a handful of countries with poor collection systems. The far larger environmental questions in our own country concern how we produce energy, how we manage industrial waste, and how we regulate chemicals that persist in water and blood. A government that spends its credibility on straws has less left to spend on those.


This is also a matter of honest government. A law imposed on every citizen and every business should first be shown to work. When a mandate is adopted because it signals virtue rather than because it was measured, the people bear the cost and receive nothing in return. That is not stewardship. It is the appearance of stewardship.


## The test


*Is it righteous?* Stewardship of creation is a real duty (Genesis 2:15). But righteousness requires truth in proportion. Mandating a substitute without first measuring whether it is better fails that standard.


*Is it loving?* Burdening millions of people and businesses with a worse product, without environmental benefit, is not loving to them or to the creation the policy claimed to protect.


*Would I want it done to me?* No one wants to be compelled to pay more for a product that works worse, based on a promised benefit that was never checked.


## What I would do


- **Measure before mandating.** No federal environmental mandate on a consumer product should take effect without a published, independent life-cycle comparison showing that the required substitute is actually better.

- **Prefer reduction to substitution.** Where single-use items are a real problem, the honest goal is to use fewer. “Straw on request” is a sensible practice for businesses, adopted voluntarily, not a federal crusade.

- **Truth in labeling.** Products marketed as “eco-friendly,” “plant-based,” or “biodegradable” should meet clear definitions. A plant-based plastic that never biodegrades should not be sold as if it does.

- **Spend credibility on the beam.** Focus on what matters: abundant clean energy (my answer for long-term energy abundance is thorium molten-salt reactors), proper waste collection, and regulating persistent chemicals, including the PFAS that show up in coated paper products.


## Conclusion


President Trump’s 2025 executive order ending the federal paper-straw purchasing push looks, in light of this study, like the better call. But the deeper lesson is not partisan. Good intentions do not substitute for measurement, and a policy that feels green is not therefore good. Care for creation is a serious duty. Serious duties deserve honest arithmetic.
