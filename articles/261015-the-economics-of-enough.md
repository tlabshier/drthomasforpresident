# 261015 - The Economics of Enough

## The Economics of Enough, Reconsidered: Who Gets to Work When No One Needs More?


*A chapter addition to Chapter 8 of* Transforming America


In Chapter 8, I answered Isak’s second economics question: would the economy collapse if Americans stopped overindulging? I said no. Money that is not spent is saved and invested. Consumption patterns shift from ice cream to vegetables, and from disposable goods to durable ones. The change would take a generation, not happen overnight, so the economy would have time to adjust, as it did when people stopped buying typewriters. And I quoted Adam Smith: what is prudent for a family cannot be folly for a kingdom.


All of that is true as far as it goes. But when I reread the chapter, I found I had added a paragraph that didn’t fit the rest of the argument. I wrote that the trouble is a mismatch between the number of people who need work and the number of workers it takes to supply their needs, and that the advertising and sales industry makes up the difference by creating demand. I left that paragraph sitting in the middle of a chapter that says the opposite. It deserves its own chapter, because I think it shows something the first answer missed.


The first answer said the economy would adjust. It never said how. “The market adjusts” is not a mechanism. It promises that a mechanism exists. So let me look for it.


### What the first answer assumed


The argument that the economy would adjust to temperance rests on two assumptions.


The first is that saving turns into investment. A dollar I don’t spend on shoes goes to the bank, the bank lends it to a business, and the business builds a workshop. That is how it works when there is something worth building. But a business borrows to build a factory only when it expects people to buy what the factory makes. If a whole nation decides it has enough shoes, no one builds a shoe factory with its savings. John Maynard Keynes made this point in the 1930s: savings do not automatically become investment. Investment follows expected demand. A nation that saves more and wants less does not automatically invest more. It may simply produce less.


The second assumption is that the money we stop spending on one thing goes to another. Some of it will. The ice cream becomes vegetables. But temperance is not only a change in what we buy. It is a decision to want less overall. A man content with one good tool, a modest house, and a reliable car won’t redirect the money he used to waste into some other kind of consumption. If he did, he would not be temperate. He would be intemperate in a new direction.


So the honest question is this: if a nation really wants less, what happens to the people whose work used to supply the difference?


### Needs are finite, and the labor to meet them shrinks


A man needs a home, food, clothing, power, transportation, medical care, education for his children, and some recreation. These needs are real, but they are limited. A man can only eat so much.


The number of workers it takes to meet those needs is not fixed. It shrinks as we learn to produce more efficiently. In 1900, roughly forty percent of American workers worked on farms. Today it is less than two percent, and they feed more people, and feed them better (if you ignore the chemicals, the genetic modifications, and the nutritional differences due to soil depletion). The other thirty-eight percent did not starve or sit idle. They went to work making things their grandparents never knew they wanted: cars, radios, refrigerators, air travel, television, computers, and the countless services of a modern economy.


That is the gap I was trying to describe. Nothing in the market connects how many people need to work with how much work it takes to meet basic needs. Each improvement in productivity frees workers. Something has to employ them. Historically, new wants have employed them.


In 1958, the economist John Kenneth Galbraith named this pattern in *The Affluent Society*. He called it the dependence effect: in a rich society, production also creates the wants it satisfies. The factory makes the product, and the advertising makes the desire for it. Production is then justified by the urgency of the wants it created. If the wants were real and independent of the producer, he argued, the producer would not need to spend so much creating them.


So my thesis is not new, and I am glad to find that an economist saw it before I did. But I think its moral implications remain unaddressed. Our employment system is calibrated to appetite.


### How the market closes the gap


When productivity rises, and basic needs are already met, an economy has only three options for the freed labor.


It can create new wants. That is the path we took: new products, new markets, and the sales and marketing that make people feel they need them.


It can reduce working hours. If it takes fewer hours to meet our needs, everyone could work fewer hours and live as well as before. In 1930, Keynes predicted that by about 2030 his grandchildren’s generation would need to work only about fifteen hours a week, because productivity would have solved the economic problem. He was right about the productivity. He was wrong about the hours. The workweek fell from about sixty hours around 1900 to about forty by 1940, and then it mostly stopped falling. We treated the gains as more goods rather than more time. The sociologist Juliet Schor called this the work-and-spend cycle: we work to buy, buy because we work, and never cash in the gains as rest.


Or it can leave some people without work at all.


That third path is where the market goes when it cannot do the first two, and I think that’s where our system’s hidden cost lies. When wants stop growing, the adjustment does not fall evenly. The market does not tell every worker to work thirty hours instead of forty. It keeps most workers at forty hours and lets some go. The ones it lets go are usually the ones with the least to sell: the poorly trained, the mentally ill, the addicted, the old, and the unlucky.


This is the mechanism my first answer lacked. The market can adjust to temperance. In principle, the adjustment is simple: fewer hours, less income, and the same contentment. But the institutions we have built resist that adjustment. A job comes bundled as forty hours. Income is tied to the job. Health insurance is tied to the job. Debt payments assume the income will continue. So when demand falls, the adjustment comes as layoffs rather than shorter weeks, and the people at the margin fall out of the game.


### Who is outside the game


In our system, a person gets a claim on the nation’s production in one of four ways: he sells his labor, he owns something that produces income, he receives charity, or the government transfers it to him. The first two are the honorable routes in a free market. A man with neither marketable labor nor property must rely on the second two.


The market rewards those who tap into what people want. The entrepreneur finds an unmet desire and fills it. The salesman persuades people they want it. The worker is willing to serve someone who has done one of those things, and earns a wage. These three kinds of people form the economy’s productive core, and many of them live well.


But some people are outside that circle, and their exclusion is not primarily because they consume too much. It is because they have nothing the market will buy. The addicted man is outside it. The mentally ill woman is outside it. The young man who never learned a skill, never learned to show up on time, and never had a father to teach him how to work is outside it. Some of them are outside because of their own choices, and some because of what was done to them. Most are outside because of both.


This is why I am no longer convinced that excess consumption is the central economic problem. Intemperance costs us dearly; the ledger in Chapter 8 still stands. But the deeper problem may be distribution: the way our system ties a man’s claim on the nation’s goods almost entirely to his ability to sell something into the market of other people’s wants.


### Where my thesis overreaches


I want to hold my own argument to the same standard I hold everyone else’s, so here is where I think it goes too far.


First, advertising is not the whole engine. Advertising in the United States accounts for roughly 1 to 2 percent of the economy, and much of it shifts customers from one brand to another rather than creating new wants. Many of the last century’s new jobs served real goods: medicine that keeps people alive longer, education, safer cars, cleaner water, and tools that made our work lighter. A man who had never seen a refrigerator did not need to be manipulated into wanting one. He needed to be told it existed. So I need to distinguish between selling that informs and selling that inflames. Telling a man there is a better tool that serves him. Making him ashamed of the tool he has so he will buy one he doesn’t need is a different trade, and it is the one at odds with a simple, contented life.


Second, the economist’s maxim of unlimited wants and limited resources is a postulate, not a law of nature. It describes how fallen men behave, not how they must behave. Proverbs already tell us that some appetites say, “It is enough.” **(Proverbs 30:8-9)**: *“Remove far from me vanity and lies: give me neither poverty nor riches; feed me with food convenient for me: Lest I be full, and deny thee, and say, Who is the LORD? or lest I be poor, and steal, and take the name of my God in vain.” *But the postulate builds that appetite into the model’s foundation and then treats it as permanent. If a changed heart can reduce wants, then an economy built on the assumption that they cannot will face a real transition when hearts change. That is not an argument against changed hearts. It is an argument for preparing the economy for them.


Third, the gap between needs and jobs has not yet produced mass unemployment. For two hundred years, every round of labor-saving machinery was followed by new work. Those who predicted that machines would make men permanently idle have been wrong every time so far. That history should humble anyone who predicts it now, including me.


### The limiting case: the robot economy


But the logic points somewhere, and the limiting case is coming into view. Suppose AI-driven robots can do most of the work: farming, manufacturing, construction, transportation, and much of the clerical and professional work as well. Production no longer needs many workers. But everyone still needs food, housing, power, medical care, and the amenities of a good life.


In that world, wages can no longer be the main ticket to a share of production, because there aren’t enough wages to go around. The goods exist. The machines produce them. The question is entirely who has a claim on them.


Under our current rules, the answer is: whoever owns the machines. If ownership stays concentrated, the owners will receive nearly all the income, and everyone else will depend on their charity or on the government’s taxing them. That is the situation the Bible warned against: *“Woe unto them that join house to house, that lay field to field, till there be no place, that they may be placed alone in the midst of the earth!”* **(Isaiah 5:8)** The robot is the field of the future. The man who owns all the robots has joined field to field.


### Three ways to distribute what the machines make


I see three possible answers, and they are not equally good.


**The first is a transfer:** a universal living wage, paid by taxing the owners of production and sending checks to everyone. It is the simplest answer, and it would keep people fed. But it collides with what I wrote in Chapter 5. There I argued that when government takes from one man to give to another who is not helpless, that is robbery by proxy. A universal living wage financed by taxes is exactly that kind of transfer. It would also make every citizen dependent on the state for his bread, and a state that feeds everyone can starve anyone who displeases it. I don’t think I can advocate this answer without first resolving that contradiction, and I have not resolved it yet.


**The second is ownership.** Instead of redistributing income, distribute the ownership of production itself. That is the Jubilee principle. In Israel, land was returned to the families it was originally given to every fifty years, so no family could be permanently dispossessed of its means of production. The prophets’ picture of peace was *“But they shall sit every man under his vine and under his fig tree; and none shall make them afraid: for the mouth of the LORD of hosts hath spoken it.”* **(Micah 4:4)**. In an AI-driven robot economy, the vine and the fig tree are shares in the machines. If every family owned a real share of the nation’s productive capacity, the income would flow to them as owners, not as dependents. A Christian writer, G. K. Chesterton, argued for something like this a century ago under the name of distributism: the problem with capitalism, he said, is not too many capitalists but too few. A working model is close to home. Since 1982, Alaska has paid every resident an annual dividend from a fund built on the state’s oil revenue. The fund is owned in common, and the dividend is paid as a return on that ownership, not as charity. I don’t claim this solves every problem. But it is the answer most consistent with the stewardship argument of Chapter 2: real property, held in trust, with no family permanently shut out.


**The third is time.** If the machines do the work, people can work less. The adjustment Keynes predicted, and our culture refused, becomes possible again: shorter working lives, shorter weeks, and more time for family, church, craft, study, and service. The man who no longer needs to sell forty hours a week to survive is free to spend more of his life on what the market never paid for: raising his children, caring for his parents, teaching, building his community, and worshiping God.


I think the right answer is some combination of the second and third, with charity and family carrying those who cannot participate, and a reparative system that helps the addicted, the untrained, and the broken become whole and able to work.


### The motivation problem


The strongest objection to any managed system is motivation. Chapter 2 described the Soviet worker’s joke: “They pretend to pay us, so we pretend to work.” When a man’s bread no longer depends on his effort, many men stop putting in effort. Paul’s rule was blunt: *“If any would not work, neither should he eat.”* **(2 Thessalonians 3:10)**.


Notice what Paul’s rule addresses. It addresses the man who *will not* work. It does not address the man for whom there is no work. In a robot economy, that distinction becomes central. A just system must keep consequences for the man who refuses, without punishing the man the economy no longer needs.


But I think the deeper answer lies elsewhere. Work did not begin with the curse. Before the fall, God put Adam in the garden: *“And the LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to dress it and to keep it.”* **(Genesis 2:15).** Work was given as stewardship and delight. The curse added toil: thorns, sweat, and the threat of hunger as the driver. For most of history, hunger has been the main motivator, and it works. But it is not the motivator God intended. The motivator He intended is the thrill of living in His world: of making, tending, discovering, serving, and building, because it is good and because He made us to do it.


If we ever reach the economy of abundance that machines make possible, the old motivator will weaken. Only the better one will remain. A nation of people who work only because they are hungry will decay when they are no longer hungry. A nation of people who work because they love God, love their neighbor, and love the world He made will flourish. That is why the people’s moral transformation is not separate from the economic question. It is the precondition for surviving prosperity.


### What this means for temperance


So I am not withdrawing the call to temperance. The ledger of intemperance in Chapter 8 is real, and a man who says “this is enough” is still richer than a man who always wants more.


But I now see something I didn’t see when I first wrote that chapter. We have built an economy whose employment system depends on appetite. It finds work for the freed laborer mainly by persuading the rest of us to want more. If we teach a nation temperance without also changing how a man claims his share of what the nation produces, we will create a real transition problem, and those least able to bear it will suffer the most.


The manna in the wilderness is the pattern. *“He that gathered much had nothing over, and he that gathered little had no lack.”* **(Exodus 16:8) **God did not only teach Israel to be content with enough. He built the distribution into the provision itself. Every family received its portion. No one could corner the supply, because hoarded manna spoiled. Temperance and distribution were taught together.


That is what I think a righteous economy must do. Teach men to want enough, and make sure every family has a real claim to enough, through its own work, its own property, its family, and its neighbors, rather than leaving that claim to depend on whether someone can be persuaded to want more.


I hold this the way I hold every argument in this book: as the best case I have found so far. The questions about transfer, ownership, and motivation are open, and I welcome a better answer than mine.
