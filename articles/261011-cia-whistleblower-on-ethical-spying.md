# Watchmen Under the Law


### An intelligence policy for a nation that intends to stay free


Oct 11, 2026 · Dr. Thomas


## Plain language summary


A nation needs spies. Scripture sends spies into Canaan, and a wise king learns his enemy’s plans before the battle. But a spy service is a dangerous tool. It works in secret, it outlasts every president, and it can turn its eyes from foreign enemies to its own citizens. When that happens, it no longer guards the people; it governs them.


This paper responds to an [October 2026 interview with John Kiriakou](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wwn5GUgcq80&list=PLkL7BvJXiqSQu3i72hSrG4vUkDuaneHuB&index=2), a former CIA officer who went to prison after exposing parts of the agency’s torture program. Some of what he says needs correcting, and I note where. But his main charges hold up against the public record: career officials can quietly ignore an elected president; Congress oversees tens of thousands of officers with a few dozen staff and depends on what the agencies choose to report; far too much is classified; the torture program was illegal, did not produce reliable truth, and has now wrecked the trial of the 9/11 planners; and the man who disclosed the program went to prison while those who ran it, and those who destroyed its videotapes, did not.


I propose: make it a crime to withhold a required report from Congress; put an expiration date on every secret; treat authorized leaks by the powerful the same as unauthorized leaks by the weak; ban torture permanently, with criminal penalties and no secret prisons; permit lethal force abroad only under law, and never with weapons that cannot tell a fighter from a child; never spy on Americans without a warrant; give whistleblowers a channel that works, and a jury that hears why they acted; and guard against foreign penetration by every nation, friend or rival. The test behind every proposal: is the act lawful before God, and if so, is it loving toward the people the watchman serves?


*Provenance: Claude drafted this essay in my voice at my request on 11 October 2026, from the transcript of John Kiriakou’s interview on the Modern Wisdom podcast and from the public record. It builds on my August paper, “Counterfeit Omniscience,” on the domestic surveillance state. I mark points where my position is still open to a better argument.*


## Why a nation needs watchmen


Some Christians are uneasy with espionage, as if the work were itself a sin. Scripture does not take that view. Moses sent twelve men to spy out Canaan (Numbers 13). Joshua sent two into Jericho, and Hebrews 11 honors Rahab for hiding them. Proverbs counsels that war is made *“by wise guidance”* **(Proverbs 24:6; 20:18)**. And God told Ezekiel that a watchman who sees the sword coming and does not blow the trumpet carries the blood of those who die **(Ezekiel 33:6)**.


So the question is not whether a nation should gather knowledge of those who intend it harm. It should, and a president who neglects that duty fails the people he serves. Knowing an enemy’s plans can prevent a war, shorten one, or save the lives of soldiers and civilians alike.


The question is what a watchman may do, and to whom. A watchman stands on the wall facing outward. When he turns to watch the people inside the city, record their conversations, and decide who is suspect, he becomes something else. The history of intelligence services everywhere, including ours, shows that the turn happens gradually, in secret, and usually with good intentions.


## The test I apply


In my earlier papers I asked whether a neighbor would feel loved by an act in the public square. That test points the right way but misses the center. The center is righteousness: what God has established as right and wrong. So I now ask two questions, in order:


- **Is it lawful?** Not merely legal under some statute or secret memo, but righteous before God. Torture of a helpless prisoner, the killing of the innocent, and false witness are not made right because a lawyer writes an opinion saying so.

- **If it is lawful, is it loving?** Does it serve the people the government exists to protect, including the stranger and even the enemy, in the way Christ would act?


I add a practical test from “Counterfeit Omniscience”: *if you would not hand an authority to your enemy, do not hand it to your friend.* Every power we give an intelligence agency will be inherited by the administration we fear most.


Paul condemns those who say, “*Let us do evil, that good may come”* *(Romans 3:8)*. Intelligence work lives under constant pressure to do exactly that. A policy for it must resist that pressure, because the men inside the agencies are no better or worse than the rest of us.


## What the Kiriakou interview gets right, and where it needs correcting


John Kiriakou served fifteen years at the CIA, led the 2002 raid in Pakistan that captured Abu Zubaydah, and in 2007 confirmed to ABC News that the CIA waterboarded prisoners. In 2012, he pleaded guilty to disclosing a covert officer’s identity to a reporter, violating the Intelligence Identities Protection Act, and served about two years of a thirty-month sentence. He was the only CIA officer imprisoned in connection with the torture program, and he went to prison for a disclosure, not for torture.


He is one witness, and Scripture requires two or three **(Deuteronomy 19:15)**. I have checked his main claims against the public record. Most hold up. Several details are wrong, and I list those corrections in the editorial notes so critics cannot use small errors to dismiss large truths. The charges that stand are these:


- **The permanent government outlasts the elected one.** Career officers serve 25 or 35 years; presidents serve 4 or 8. An officer who disagrees with an order can say “Yes, Mr. President” and then let it die. Kiriakou says the CIA welcomes presidents without foreign-policy experience, because the daily intelligence briefing draws them into the agency’s own view of the world.

- **Congressional oversight runs on the honor system.** The Senate and House intelligence committees were created in 1976 and 1977, after the Church and Pike investigations exposed assassination plots, mail opening, and spying on Americans. Each committee’s staff numbers in the dozens; the agencies they oversee number in the tens of thousands. They learn what the agencies tell them. Kiriakou describes a station chief instructing him not to report a lost source to Congress, and says that was not unusual.

- **Too much is classified, and the powerful leak whenever it suits them.** Former officials, including a former head of the government’s classification oversight office, have long testified that much classified material should not be. Meanwhile, senior officials leak classified information to shape news coverage and rarely face punishment.

- **The torture program was illegal, cruel, and counterproductive.** The Senate Intelligence Committee’s 2014 study documents this, discussed below.

- **The agencies collect on Americans despite laws forbidding it**, now largely by buying data from private brokers, which my August paper addressed.

- **Foreign intelligence services are working hard to penetrate ours.** China’s effort is the largest, but allies spy on us too.


## The problems, in order


### 1. The watchman answers to no one in particular


The Constitution makes the president commander-in-chief and gives Congress the purse and the power to make government rules. Intelligence agencies sit uneasily between them. Both branches are supposed to know what the agencies do; in practice, the agencies decide what each branch learns.


The law already requires intelligence agencies to keep congressional committees “fully and currently informed” and to notify them of covert actions. But no one goes to prison for failing to report. A cable that is never sent leaves no record, and the officers who chose not to send it are the same officers who would have to investigate themselves.


This is the deepest problem, because every other abuse depends on it. An agency that must report its failures will commit fewer.


### 2. Secrecy beyond its purpose


Secrecy has a legitimate purpose: to protect sources, methods, and plans whose exposure would cost lives or lose a war. It also has an illegitimate use: to hide embarrassment, error, and crime. The second is far more common than the first, and the classification system cannot tell them apart, because the person deciding what to classify is usually the person who would be embarrassed.


Jesus said nothing is covered that will not be revealed **(Luke 12:2)**. A government that hides its wrongs does not escape judgment; it only delays it and loses its people’s trust in the meantime.


The double standard compounds the harm. When a cabinet officer leaks a classified detail to a friendly reporter to win a policy fight, nothing happens. When a mid-level officer discloses wrongdoing, he faces the Espionage Act, a law with no public-interest defense, under which a jury cannot weigh why he acted. The law is applied by rank, not by conduct.


### 3. Torture


The Senate Intelligence Committee’s study of the CIA detention and interrogation program, whose 525-page summary was released in December 2014, documents what was done: Abu Zubaydah was waterboarded 83 times and at one point became completely unresponsive; Khalid Shaikh Mohammed was waterboarded 183 times; prisoners were kept awake for up to 180 hours, often standing or in stress positions; and in November 2002 a prisoner named Gul Rahman died of hypothermia, partially nude and chained to a concrete floor, at a CIA site in Afghanistan. The committee concluded that the techniques did not produce intelligence that could not have been obtained otherwise, and that the CIA misrepresented the program to the White House, Congress, and the public. In 2005, the CIA destroyed ninety-two videotapes of interrogations. No one was charged for the tapes or for the death.


Torture fails the first test. It treats a helpless man, made in God’s image, as an object to be broken. Federal law (18 U.S.C. 2340A), the Convention Against Torture that the United States helped draft and ratified in 1994, and the moral law that stands above both forbid it. After World War II, the United States prosecuted Japanese officers for waterboarding American prisoners; the law did not change between 1947 and 2002. We changed.


It also fails on its own terms. A man in agony will say whatever he believes will stop the pain. Senator John McCain, tortured in Hanoi, gave his captors the names of the Green Bay Packers’ offensive line as his squadron mates. False leads cost analysts months, and the real threat moves on while they chase them.


And it has failed justice. In August 2026, the military judge in the Guantanamo case against the alleged planners of September 11 threw out Khalid Shaikh Mohammed’s 2007 statements to FBI agents. The judge found that the CIA’s years of abuse left coercion in place even during the later “clean” interviews. Nearly three thousand Americans were murdered that day. A quarter-century later, their families still have no verdict, and torture is a principal reason. The program meant to bring the guilty to justice may have ensured they never face it.


Congress has since limited every agency to the interrogation methods in the Army Field Manual (2015). That is a statute; Congress can repeal it later, and a future administration can find lawyers to read around it, as the last one did.


### 4. Turning the eyes inward


The National Security Agency and the CIA are forbidden to target Americans. Yet Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act sweeps in Americans’ communications whenever they talk with a foreign target, and the FBI has searched that database for Americans’ names hundreds of thousands of times without a warrant. Agencies buy location and browsing data from brokers they could not legally compel to turn over without a warrant. National security letters let the FBI demand records with no judge involved and forbid the recipient from telling anyone.


Section 702 lapsed on June 12, 2026, for the first time since 2008, after the House voted down an extension in a dispute over the acting Director of National Intelligence. Collection continues regardless, under court certifications that run into 2027. The lapse is therefore an opportunity, not a solution: Congress must decide on what terms, if any, to restore it.


I set out my reforms in “Counterfeit Omniscience” and repeat only the core here.


### 5. Killing and capture abroad


Kiriakou distinguishes two practices. *Rendition* sends a captured man to his own country for trial. *Extraordinary rendition* sends him to a third country chosen because its jailers will torture him. The first can be lawful. The second is torture by proxy and fails the first test as surely as torture by our own hands.


Lethal force is harder. A nation at war may kill enemy combatants, and a president may act to stop an imminent attack. But the American practice since 2001 has drifted far from either standard: strikes in countries where Congress never authorized war, killing on the basis of “patterns of life” rather than identity, and in 2011 the killing of an American citizen, Anwar al-Awlaki, in Yemen without charge or trial. His sixteen-year-old son, also an American, was killed in a separate strike weeks later.


Kiriakou calls Israel’s 2024 operation against Hezbollah, in which thousands of pagers exploded at once across Lebanon, an act of genius. It was a feat of craft. But the craft is not the question. The devices exploded in homes, shops, and streets, wherever their owners happened to be. Children were among those killed, and thousands were wounded. Just-war teaching, which Christians developed over centuries from Scripture, requires that force discriminate between combatant and non-combatant. A weapon set off without knowing who is standing beside its target cannot meet that standard, however brilliant its design. I would not authorize an American operation like that.


### 6. Punishing the messenger


The record of the torture program’s aftermath is a study in proportion. The officers who ran it were not prosecuted. The officer who destroyed the tapes was not prosecuted. The lawyers who wrote the memos authorizing it became a federal judge and a law professor. The one man imprisoned was the officer who confirmed it existed, convicted for a disclosure he made to a reporter in the years that followed.


I do not excuse what Kiriakou pleaded guilty to. Exposing the identity of a covert officer can endanger that officer and his contacts, and the law forbidding it is just. But a justice system that imprisons the man who revealed a crime while sparing those who committed it is not doing justice. It is protecting an institution. Kiriakou reports that a cabinet member told him he would not be pardoned because the agency fears a pardon would encourage other whistleblowers. If that is true, it is the clearest possible admission of why a pardon is warranted.


### 7. Foreign penetration


Kiriakou rightly calls China the most serious intelligence threat: Chinese services recruit congressional staff, cultivate researchers in American universities, and pressure Chinese dissidents in the United States by threatening their families at home. He notes that China has made the chips in American passports. Spies are recruited through a predictable cycle: they are spotted, assessed for access, developed through a vulnerability such as debt, ambition, or love for a sick wife or a child’s tuition, and finally recruited.


Allies spy on us as well. Jonathan Pollard, a Navy analyst, was convicted in 1987 of passing large volumes of classified material to Israel. A sound counterintelligence policy guards against every foreign service and applies the same scrutiny to friends as to rivals. Kiriakou made further claims about recent Israeli efforts that I have not been able to verify, and I do not rely on them here.


## What I propose


These proposals are positions I hold and will defend. They are open to better arguments; they are not final.


### A. Make oversight real


- **Make it a crime to withhold a required report from Congress.** Any officer who knowingly suppresses a required notification, or orders a subordinate to suppress one, commits a felony. The officer who is ordered to do so has a protected channel to report the order directly to the committees and the inspector general.

- **Scale oversight to the agencies.** Give the intelligence committees staff, cleared auditors, and the Government Accountability Office full access to intelligence programs and budgets. A committee that must ask permission to look cannot oversee.

- **Publish the top-line budget of every agency**, as the overall intelligence budget is already published. Citizens cannot judge what they cannot measure.

- **Hold career officers accountable to elected authority.** Carry out a lawfully given presidential order, or formally refuse it in writing with reasons so the refusal can be reviewed. Silent non-compliance is grounds for dismissal. This protects both sides: the president cannot be quietly ignored, and an officer who refuses an unlawful order has a record that protects him.


### B. Put an expiration date on secrets


- **Every classified record carries a sunset**: declassification after ten years by default, twenty-five at most, unless a named official certifies, in writing and subject to review, that release would endanger a living person or an active operation.

- **The classifier signs his name.** Classifying material to hide error, embarrassment, or crime is already forbidden by executive order; make it a punishable offense, with an independent declassification board empowered to find and reverse it.

- **One rule for leaks.** Treat an authorized leak of classified information to the press exactly like an unauthorized one. If you can share the information with a reporter, you can declassify it and share it with everyone.

- **Release the full Senate torture report**, all 6,700 pages, with only names of covert officers and foreign sources withheld. A nation cannot repent of what it refuses to read.


### C. Forbid torture permanently


- **Make the Army Field Manual limit permanent and criminal.** Any officer, contractor, or official who authorizes or carries out an interrogation technique outside it commits a felony, and no legal opinion is a defense.

- **No secret prisons and no extraordinary rendition.** An American agency registers every person it holds and makes them visible to the International Committee of the Red Cross within days. No one is transferred to a country where torture is likely.

- **Close Guantanamo.** Roughly fifteen men remain. Those who can be tried should be tried in federal court, where terrorists have been convicted hundreds of times since 2001. Those who cannot be tried because the evidence was tainted by torture are the clearest lesson in why torture must never be repeated.


### D. Bind lethal force to law


- **Use lethal force abroad only under current congressional authorization of war, or to stop a specific, imminent attack**, with the president’s personal written approval and notice to the committees within forty-eight hours.

- **No American is killed by his own government without due process.** If a citizen has joined an enemy at war, the government must present evidence to a federal judge before targeting him, except to stop an attack already underway.

- **Methods that cannot discriminate are forbidden.** No booby-trapped consumer goods, no mass-casualty devices triggered without knowledge of who is near them, regardless of how many enemies they would kill.

- **An annual public accounting** of every strike, its location, and the government’s count of civilians killed, reviewed by the inspectors general.


### E. Keep the watchmen’s eyes outward


- The core reforms of “Counterfeit Omniscience”: a warrant for any search of foreign-intelligence databases using an American’s name or number; a ban on any government agency buying data it would need a warrant to compel; an end to indefinite gag orders, so that a citizen eventually learns his records were taken and can go to court. Section 702 should not be restored on any other terms.


### F. Protect the honest messenger


- **A whistleblower channel that works**: an inspector general independent of the agency’s chain of command, with direct access to the intelligence committees, a deadline to act, and protection from retaliation enforceable in court.

- **A public-interest defense under the Espionage Act.** A defendant who disclosed classified information may show the jury that he disclosed evidence of a crime or serious abuse, that he first tried proper channels or could not safely use them, and that he disclosed no more than necessary. The jury weighs it.

- **Juries told the truth about their role.** In every prosecution for disclosure, as in every criminal case, jurors should know they are the conscience of the community and may acquit when conviction would be unjust. I regard jury nullification as a cornerstone of liberty; it is the people’s last check on a prosecution that serves the government rather than justice.

- **Clemency for John Kiriakou.** He broke a just law and served his sentence. Those who ran the program he exposed, and those who destroyed its evidence, were never charged. A pardon would restore his rights and tell every honest officer that reporting a crime is not the greater crime. I hold this position open: if the officer he exposed, or that officer’s contacts, came to harm because of his disclosure, I would reconsider.


### G. Guard the gates against every foreign service


- **Domestic supply for national security documents and hardware**: passports, identity cards, and the chips in military and critical infrastructure systems are made in the United States or by trusted allies under inspection.

- **Counterintelligence screening applies equally** to officials with ties to any foreign government, ally or adversary, and the Foreign Agents Registration Act is enforced regardless of which country the agent serves.

- **Contractors are under the same law as agencies.** Private firms doing intelligence work, and firms in which the CIA’s venture fund invests, face the same oversight, inspector-general jurisdiction, and limits on collecting data about Americans as the agencies themselves. An agency cannot buy from a contractor what it may not do itself.


## Why this is finally a moral problem


Carved in the lobby of CIA headquarters are the words of Jesus: *“And ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free”* **(John 8:32)**. It is a fitting motto for an intelligence service, and an indictment of one that hides its own truth from the people it serves.


No legal structure will keep an intelligence service righteous if the men in it, and the citizens who fund it, do not care whether it is. Structure restrains the wicked; only changed hearts reduce the need for restraint. The reforms above are the restraint. The deeper work is a people who will not accept evil done in their name because it is done in secret, who will sit on a jury and refuse to convict a man for telling the truth, and who will elect leaders who would rather lose an advantage than torture a prisoner.


That is a demanding standard. I believe it is also the only one under which a nation can keep both its security and its freedom. A watchman who serves God and the people on the wall is a city’s best protection. A watchman who serves himself is the worst danger, because he already holds the gate.


## Open questions


- **How short can classification sunsets be** without exposing sources still living? Ten years may be too short for human sources recruited young.

- **Lethal force against fighters for groups with no state**, outside any declared war: when, if ever, does an imminent-threat standard apply to someone not yet acting?

- **The public-interest defense and its abuse:** how to keep it from becoming a shield for anyone who leaks for ideology or pay, while preserving it for the honest man.

- **Section 702 itself:** should a warrant-requirement version be restored, or should the authority end entirely and foreign collection rest on Executive Order 12333 with its own new limits?

- **Accountability for the torture program:** prosecution is largely barred by time and prior decisions. Is a full public accounting, with names, the most that justice now allows?


## Editorial notes


**Corrections to claims in the interview.** These do not change the main argument but should be known before anyone cites the interview:


- *“Federal Torture Act of 1946.”* There is no such statute. The federal torture statute is 18 U.S.C. 2340–2340A, enacted in 1994 to implement the Convention Against Torture (signed 1988, ratified 1994). Torture was also already a crime under general law and the laws of war.

- *Japanese soldiers were “executed” for waterboarding.* Japanese officers were convicted of war crimes that included water torture; the commonly cited case, Yukio Asano, received fifteen years of hard labor. In other cases, executions involved additional crimes.

- *The 1968 waterboarding photograph.* The Washington Post published the photograph in January 1968, and the soldier was investigated and court-martialed; the twenty-year sentence Kiriakou mentions should be verified before it is repeated.

- *Sleep deprivation “for 12 days.”* The Justice Department’s legal opinions approved up to 180 hours (seven and a half days). The Senate study documents prisoners kept awake for up to 180 hours.

- *Cockroaches in Abu Zubaydah’s confinement box.* The CIA sought approval to place an insect in the box; it was approved, but the Senate study reports it was not used. He did spend more than 260 hours in a coffin-sized box.

- *The cold cell.* Gul Rahman’s death from hypothermia in November 2002 is documented in the Senate study. “Murdered people” (plural) by this technique is Kiriakou’s characterization.

- *MKUltra records.* CIA Director Richard Helms ordered the program’s files destroyed in 1973, before the Church Committee formed; about 20,000 surviving financial and administrative documents were found in 1977. Kiriakou’s account that Senator Church warned the director, who then shredded files, conflates these events.

- *The CIA’s tech investments “after 9/11.”* In-Q-Tel, the CIA’s venture fund, was founded in 1999. It was an early investor in Palantir.

- *National security letters “since the Patriot Act.”* They existed before 2001; the Patriot Act greatly broadened who could issue them and for what. A 2007 Justice Department inspector general report found widespread misuse.

- *“Nothing to hide” was first said by Goebbels.* This attribution is unsupported; it was not used here.

- *Carlos the Jackal.* Ilich Ramírez Sánchez was captured in Khartoum in August 1994 and handed to France. The ground-glass toothpaste and dentist account is Kiriakou’s; the published accounts I know of describe Sudanese authorities sedating him during a medical procedure.

- *Claims not independently verified and not relied on:* the cabinet member’s statement about the pardon (it is Kiriakou’s report); the recent Defense Intelligence Agency email about Israeli operations; the share of classified material that need not be classified (Kiriakou attributes “three-quarters” to J. William Leonard, the former head of the Information Security Oversight Office; published estimates by former officials vary widely and should be checked before a figure is cited).
