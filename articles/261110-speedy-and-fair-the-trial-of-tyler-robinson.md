# "261110 – Speedy and Fair: The Trial of Tyler Robinson

Speedy and Fair

## The Trial of Tyler Robinson

*Dr Thomas Lee Abshier, ND · November 10, 2026*

**Resource:** Jack Posobiec, *Human Events* SITREP, November 10, 2026 (email newsletter), item 1, quoting attorney Andrew Branca.


---


## Plain language summary

A Utah judge has set the trial of Tyler Robinson, the man accused of murdering Charlie Kirk, to begin in June 2028, nearly three years after the killing. The prosecution is seeking the death penalty. Charlie Kirk's widow says that is too long. The defense says it is too soon. I hold that both a speedy trial and a fair trial are demands of justice, and that June 2028 is a defensible balance, provided the date holds. Three things threaten this trial more than the calendar does. The first is public pressure, including the President's call to try the case "within a month," which hands the defense grounds for appeal. The second is a jury-selection process that teaches citizens to hide their consciences. The third is any weakness in the courtroom's command. Mr. Robinson is accused, not convicted. If he is guilty, the verdict should be one that no appeals court can overturn.


---


## The facts

Charlie Kirk was shot and killed on September 10, 2025, while speaking at an event on the campus of Utah Valley University. Tyler Robinson, now 23, is charged with aggravated murder and has pleaded not guilty. After a preliminary hearing in June, Fourth District Judge Tony Graf found enough evidence to send the case to trial on a charge that makes him eligible for the death penalty, along with six other counts. Utah County prosecutors are seeking a death sentence.

On November 9, Judge Graf set jury selection to begin June 21, 2028, and reserved 105 court days, about five months, for the trial. The parties had proposed very different dates. Erika Kirk's attorneys asked that the trial begin no later than October 2027, invoking a victim's right under the Utah Constitution to a speedy disposition. Prosecutors said they could be ready by then, but proposed January 31, 2028, to avoid running the trial through the holidays. The defense asked for the third quarter of 2029, citing the volume of evidence, a planned challenge to the death penalty, a possible change of venue, and other cases on its lawyers' calendars. The judge chose a date between them. A readiness hearing is set for April 2028, and further motions could still delay the trial.

## Two rights, both righteous

Real tension exists here, and I do not want to resolve it by pretending one side has no claim.

The Kirk family's claim is grounded in Scripture and Utah law. *"Because sentence against an evil work is not executed speedily, therefore the heart of the sons of men is fully set in them to do evil"* **(Ecclesiastes 8:11)**. Delay is not neutral. It prolongs the grief of the victim's family. It tells the public that a political assassination, an attack on the free speech of every citizen, is just another file in a slow system. It also weakens witnesses' memories. A justice system that takes three years to begin a trial on a killing seen by thousands should be ashamed of that number, even if each step can be explained.

The defendant's claim is grounded just as deeply. *"He that is first in his own cause seemeth just; but his neighbour cometh and searcheth him"* **(Proverbs 18:17)**. The accused has the right to search the case against him, and in a capital case the stakes are absolute. An execution cannot be reversed. If the State intends to take a man's life, it must be certain that it has the right man and that the process was clean. The law of Moses required more than one witness before a man could be put to death **(Deuteronomy 17:6)**. The principle behind it is that the weightier the penalty, the higher the standard of proof and care.

Attorney Andrew Branca's point in the SITREP is correct: capital cases carry heightened constitutional protections, and both sides must prepare thoroughly. A careful defense protects the accused, and it also protects the verdict. A conviction obtained in haste is a conviction that will be fought on appeal for decades, and the Kirk family would relive this case for twenty years instead of three.

So I stand here: *"That which is altogether just shalt thou follow"* **(Deuteronomy 16:20)**. Altogether just means speedy *and* fair, with neither sacrificed to the other. June 2028 is later than I would like, but it is a defensible balance, provided we hold to it. The defense asked for 2029, and the court did not give it. The court should not now let the date slide by motion after motion. The readiness hearing in April 2028 should be the last place the calendar is fought over, not the first.

## The President should stop speaking about this case

Last month President Trump posted that Robinson should be tried "within a month." I understand the anger behind it. Charlie Kirk was the President's ally and friend, and many Americans feel his murder as an attack on themselves. But the post did not serve justice, and it did not serve the Kirk family.

The defense cited the President's post in asking the court to bar cameras from the November 9 hearing. It will cite it again in arguing for a change of venue, and again on appeal if Robinson is convicted, to argue that the jury pool was poisoned by the nation's most powerful voice. Every public demand for a quick result from the head of the executive branch becomes a brief for the defense.

Scripture is plain about the pressure of the mighty and of the crowd on a court. *"Thou shalt not follow a multitude to do evil; neither shalt thou speak in a cause to decline after many to wrest judgment"* **(Exodus 23:2)**. *"Ye shall do no unrighteousness in judgment: thou shalt not respect the person of the poor, nor honour the person of the mighty: but in righteousness shalt thou judge thy neighbour"* **(Leviticus 19:15)**. The President's role in a state prosecution is no role at all. He should leave the Kirk family's case to the Kirk family's lawyers, the Utah prosecutors, and a Utah jury.

## The jury box

Branca made a second point that deserves more attention than it will get. In a capital trial, the same jury decides guilt and then decides the sentence. Before trial, prospective jurors are questioned about whether moral or religious objections would keep them from imposing death when the facts and law support it. Those who say yes are excluded. Branca warned that some prospective jurors conceal their views to get a seat in a high-profile trial.

He is right that they do. I have argued before that this is a predictable result of how we select juries. In my paper on jury nullification, I wrote that a nation in which a man may follow his conscience only if he hides that he would is a nation being trained to lie during jury selection. The death-qualification process is a sharp instance of that. The honest citizen who says "my conscience may not permit me to vote for death" is sent home. The citizen who feels the same and says nothing is seated. The process rewards concealment and removes candor.

I hold that capital punishment can be just. *"Whoso sheddeth man's blood, by man shall his blood be shed: for in the image of God made he man"* **(Genesis 9:6)**. The image of God in the victim is the reason the penalty is so grave, not a reason to abolish it. I also believe that grace is available to any man who repents, and that the State's sword and God's mercy do not cancel each other. But a just penalty, imposed by a jury selected through a process that teaches lying, has a weakness at its foundation.

I do not have a quick reform to offer for this trial; the Supreme Court's decisions set the rules, and Judge Graf must follow them. What I can say is where the real remedy lies. As I concluded in that earlier paper, the jury is downstream of the nation's heart. No screening question can make twelve citizens honest. Only a people formed in honesty, in their homes, churches, and schools, will produce jurors who tell the truth when asked and then judge rightly. That is slow work, and it is the work that matters.

## Command of the courtroom

The SITREP reports that Judge Graf had been on the bench about a month when this case arose, and that during the preliminary hearing defense questioning about DNA science went on for hours. Branca warned that either side could exploit a judge it perceives as weak, and that the trial will need firm rulings and control.

I do not know enough to judge Judge Graf's performance, and I will not pretend to. His scheduling order does show a judge willing to refuse both sides what they asked for, which is a good sign. A lengthy examination of DNA evidence is not, by itself, a failure. Scientific evidence in a capital case should be examined closely, and the defense is entitled to test it. What matters is that the examination serves the search for truth rather than delay. A firm judge is not a harsh one. He gives each side its full due and nothing beyond it, and he makes the calendar mean what it says.

## What justice requires here

Measured by my three questions:

**Is it righteous?** God's law and ours require a trial that is both prompt and thorough, before a fair jury, under a firm judge. A rushed trial or an endless one both fall short.

**Is it loving?** It is loving to the Kirk family to bring this case to a verdict that will stand. It is loving to the accused to give him every protection the law affords before the State takes his life. It is loving to the public to show that political murder will be met with justice, not vengeance or indifference.

**Would I want it done to me?** If my son or my brother had been murdered, I would want the trial to begin soon. If I stood accused, I would want time to answer the charge and a jury that the President had not told what to decide. Both are true at once, and the court's task is to honor both.

So my position is this. Hold the June 2028 date. Let the President and every other public figure leave the case to the court. Let the lawyers prepare as if the verdict must survive twenty years of appeals, because it must. And let us remember that Tyler Robinson is presumed innocent until a jury of his neighbors says otherwise. If he is guilty, the State's justice should be beyond reproach, and he should still be offered the call to repentance that is open to every man.
