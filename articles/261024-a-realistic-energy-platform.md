# 261024 - A Realistic Energy Platform

# Energy First, Realistically


## Thorium, the data center boom, and an actionable plan for the 2029–2033 term


*Position Paper — A New ERA: Energy, Righteousness, and Accountability*

*Dr. Thomas for President 2028 — 24 October 2026*


---


## Why energy comes first


When I ran for the Oregon Senate in 2003, my slogan was *A New ERA: Energy, Righteousness, and Accountability.* I put energy first because nearly every material good a free people hopes for depends on it. Clean water from the sea requires energy. Robots that take on dangerous and tedious labor run on energy. The computing that now drives the economy is, physically, electricity turned into heat. A nation with scarce, expensive energy argues over how to divide a shrinking pie. A nation with abundant, affordable energy can build.


But a candidate who promises abundance owes the voters honesty about how and when it will arrive. This paper is my attempt at that honesty. It corrects an assumption I made earlier, it lays out what can actually be built during a 2029–2033 term, and it asks whether a Manhattan Project–scale effort on thorium reactors would be wise.


My conclusions, stated plainly:


- **No single revolutionary energy source is ready to install in 2029.** The revolution available to us in the near term is not a new machine. It is building the proven machines faster, cheaper, and in series, and removing the obstacles that have kept us from doing so.

- **The thorium molten salt reactor is the right long-term foundation,** but it will not be producing commercial power during the 2029–2033 term. An earlier paper of mine assumed commercial thorium reactors would be available for installation in 2029. That assumption does not hold, and I withdraw it. In that term, a president can launch the program, build the fuel supply, and have a demonstration reactor operating by the end.

- **A national mission on advanced reactors is wise, but it should be modeled on the disciplined, parallel-path character of the Manhattan Project, not on its wartime blank check.**

- **The data center boom can be powered now,** with existing nuclear plants, natural gas built by the data centers themselves, flexible data center operations, enhanced geothermal, and the first new reactors, provided the people who profit from the boom pay its costs rather than their neighbors.

Cost figures for generation are from Lazard’s 2026 unsubsidized levelized-cost analysis. New nuclear shows $175–255/MWh there because every recent American plant has been a first-of-a-kind build. Vogtle Units 3 and 4 in Georgia, the most recent large reactors, cost more than double their original estimate and came online about seven years late. The lesson is not that nuclear is impossible, but that costs fall with the second, third, and tenth copies of a design. That is why the plan below insists on building in series.


### A note on waste


Conventional reactors do produce spent fuel with long-lived components, and that is a real responsibility. It is also a modest one in physical terms: the entire American inventory of spent fuel from more than sixty years of commercial power is stored safely today in pools and steel-and-concrete casks, mostly at the plants where it was made. The long-lived fraction can eventually be consumed by recycling or by fast and molten salt reactors. Waste is a problem to be managed, not a reason to forgo nuclear power.


### The Ivanpah lesson


Many travelers on Interstate 15 between Los Angeles and Las Vegas have seen the three glowing towers of the Ivanpah Solar Electric Generating System. It used fields of mirrors to focus sunlight on boilers atop the towers. Built by BrightSource Energy and Bechtel, owned by NRG Energy, BrightSource, and Google, it cost about $2.2 billion, with a $1.6 billion federal loan guarantee, and began operating in 2014.


Several things went wrong. In its early years, output fell well short of what was advertised. Each morning, it burned far more natural gas than planned to start its boilers. State and federal biologists estimated that thousands of birds were killed each year, many singed by concentrated sunlight. But the decisive problem was economic: ordinary photovoltaic panels fell in price so fast that Ivanpah’s power came to cost roughly twice as much. In 2025, NRG moved to unwind its power contracts and begin closing the plant; in December 2025, California regulators rejected the closure agreement for reliability reasons and required two of its three units to keep running.


Ivanpah teaches the lesson this paper is built on: a technology can work and still lose to a cheaper one. Government should be very careful about locking in a particular machine. It should build the foundations and let the cheapest reliable source win.

## Part II. Thorium molten salt reactors: options and realities


### Three different machines


“Thorium reactor” can mean three quite different things, with very different readiness:


**1. The thorium breeder.** The original Oak Ridge vision: a reactor that makes all its own fuel from thorium. To do this in a slow-neutron design, an attached chemical plant must continuously remove protactinium-233 (an intermediate that wastes neutrons) and neutron-absorbing fission products. Oak Ridge demonstrated one step of that process, uranium removal by fluorination, at full scale on the reactor’s own fuel in 1968. The other steps (extraction into molten bismuth, transfer of rare earths across a chloride salt, distillation) were tested only in small laboratory columns, mostly with simulated rather than radioactive salt, and were never run together. Molten bismuth dissolves nickel alloys, and fluorine gas attacks most structural metals. The breeder is real physics and unfinished engineering. It also carries the thorium cycle’s main proliferation concern: protactinium separated outside the reactor decays into nearly pure uranium-233, without the intensely radioactive contaminant (uranium-232) that otherwise makes reactor uranium hard to steal and fabricate.


**2. The slow-processing or fast-spectrum thorium reactor.** French studies found that processing the salt every six months, rather than every ten days, still allows break-even fuel production, and that in a fast-neutron salt reactor, protactinium’s effect becomes minor. A fast-spectrum design can regenerate its fuel for about twenty years with only gas removal and uranium recovery, the one step already proven at scale. This is a more modest chemical burden and a more credible second-generation goal.


**3. The denatured thorium converter.** Oak Ridge’s 1980 design for a proliferation-resistant reactor runs on thorium plus uranium enriched to no more than 20 percent, kept diluted with uranium-238 so that any uranium extracted from it cannot be used in a weapon without enrichment. It performs no chemical processing of fission products over a thirty-year life; it needs periodic uranium additions; it does not breed. It keeps nearly all the advantages that make molten salt reactors attractive: operation at atmospheric pressure, no fuel meltdown, passive drain-tank shutdown, high temperature allowing air cooling, and much less long-lived waste. This is the right first thorium machine.


### What remains to be done, even for the simplest design


The denatured converter is not merely a matter of scaling up something already built. No liquid-fueled reactor has ever run at power-plant scale; the largest were Oak Ridge’s 8-megawatt experiment and China’s current 2-megawatt research reactor. The remaining work:


- **Full-size radioactive components:** pumps, heat exchangers, and an off-gas system that carry and handle fuel salt, built for remote maintenance.

- **Materials over decades:** corrosion of the nickel alloy, the effect of fission-product tellurium, and the life of the graphite moderator. Corrosion tests run on the calendar; money speeds them only by allowing more of them in parallel.

- **Lithium-7:** the salt requires lithium enriched to better than 99.99 percent in one isotope. America has made none since 1963; today, China and Russia supply it.

- **Fuel:** this is the bottleneck few people mention. Oak Ridge’s 1,000-megawatt denatured design needed about 3,450 kilograms of uranium-235 to start, roughly 17 tonnes of 20-percent-enriched uranium. America’s only producer of such fuel has delivered about 920 kilograms in total. A full-scale production line would make about 6 tonnes a year and take roughly three and a half years after funding to begin. A thorium fleet first needs a national enrichment program.

- **Licensing:** the Nuclear Regulatory Commission’s new technology-inclusive framework (Part 53, finalized in 2026) provides a path, but no liquid-fueled power reactor has yet been licensed under it.


None of this requires new science. It requires engineering, industrial capacity, and time.


### Where things stand today


- **China** operates the world’s only thorium molten salt reactor: a 2-megawatt research unit in the Gobi Desert that demonstrated thorium-to-uranium conversion in November 2025. It plans a roughly 10-megawatt-electric unit around 2030 and larger units by 2035.

- **In America,** Natura Resources holds a construction permit for a 1-megawatt liquid-fueled research reactor at Abilene Christian University. Terrestrial Energy’s Integral Molten Salt Reactor, a liquid-fueled converter using ordinary low-enriched uranium (avoiding the specialized fuel bottleneck), is progressing through NRC safety reviews. Kairos Power is building salt-cooled reactors with solid uranium fuel in Oak Ridge, with its first power-producing unit expected by 2030. None of these are thorium power reactors, but each builds the skills, supply chains, and licensing precedent a thorium program will use.

## Part III. Should America mount a Manhattan Project for thorium?


### What the Manhattan Project actually was


The Manhattan Project spent about $2 billion in 1940s dollars (on the order of $30–40 billion today), employed some 130,000 people at its peak, and produced working weapons in about three years. Its success came from more than money alone. It came from four features:


- **A single, unambiguous objective.**

- **Parallel paths.** It pursued three uranium-enrichment methods and a separate plutonium route at the same time because no one knew which would work first. Several of those paths were expensive dead ends; the strategy was to buy time with redundancy.

- **Authority to cut through obstacles.**

- **A war **that justified a blank check, secrecy, and overriding ordinary consent.


### What money can and cannot buy here


Money can buy parallel designs, more test loops, more irradiation capacity, enrichment plants, a lithium-7 plant, and a fuel-salt factory. It can shorten the timeline by years.


Money cannot make a corrosion test that must run five years finish in one. It cannot license a reactor before evidence shows it is safe. And spending at wartime intensity on chemistry that has not yet been demonstrated invites the kind of waste that discredits a program. A blank check also doesn’t match what I believe about government: it stewards other people’s money; it doesn’t own it.


### My recommendation: a disciplined national mission


I would propose a mission-driven program on the scale of America’s first nuclear power program at Shippingport (1954–1957), not a wartime crash program. Its features:


- **One publicly stated objective:** an American thorium molten salt demonstration reactor operating by the end of 2032, with the fuel supply for a commercial fleet in place by then.

- **Parallel paths:** two or three competing designs (for example, a denatured thermal converter and a fast-spectrum design), funded through milestones, with funding withdrawn from paths that stall.

- **Government does only what government must:** fuel enrichment, lithium-7, national laboratory test facilities, and licensing rules with deadlines. Private companies design, build, and own the reactors.

- **Those who need the power help pay: **AI companies are spending roughly $800 billion this year on data centers. A thorium mission would cost, by my rough estimate, $15–25 billion over eight years, about 2 to 3 percent of one year of that spending. Their co-investment should be expected.


### Is it politically wise?


**In its favor:** it has bipartisan roots (nuclear power now draws support across party lines); it answers a real strategic challenge, since China is ahead and will sell this technology to the world if we do not; it rebuilds fuel-supply capacity that matters for national security regardless of which reactor wins; and it gives voters something concrete to hope for.


**Against it:** a president who promises a revolutionary machine and fails to deliver it will discredit the larger cause of abundant energy. Government programs that pick a single winner have a poor record. And the public has heard “too cheap to meter” before.


My judgment is that it is wise, if and only if, it is presented honestly: as a foundation for the 2030s and 2040s, not as the power source for the 2029–2033 term. The near-term promise must rest on things already proven.


### Is it economically wise?


The fuel-supply portion is wise on its own merits: enrichment and lithium-7 capacity are needed for almost every advanced reactor, not just thorium. The demonstration reactors are a reasonable bet, and their cost is small relative to the energy economy and what is already being spent on computing. We should not fund the full breeder chemical plant at scale until the simpler converter is running; that is where the money could be wasted.


### The moral test


Is it righteous? Developing God-given resources with care for the creation and honest accounting before the public satisfies that test. Is it loving? A safer, cleaner, more abundant energy source that can bring affordable power and clean water to poor communities at home and abroad is an act of neighbor-love on a civilizational scale. Would I want it done to me? I would want my government to build the road toward abundance without taking more than its share from me, without siting a reactor beside my home without my consent, and without promising me what it cannot deliver. A disciplined mission meets those conditions. A blank check does not.

## Part IV. Powering the data center boom now


The data center buildout is happening this decade, and it will not wait for thorium. The question is how to power it without passing the costs on to ordinary families. Here is what can be done during the 2029–2033 term with proven technology.


### 1. Keep, uprate, and restart existing reactors


America’s existing nuclear plants are its cheapest source of firm, carbon-free power. Three shut-down reactors are being restarted to serve data centers: Palisades in Michigan (about 800 MW, expected online around early 2027), the Crane Clean Energy Center at Three Mile Island in Pennsylvania (835 MW, for Microsoft, expected 2027), and Duane Arnold in Iowa (615 MW, for Google, about 2029). Uprates (raising the output of existing reactors) could add roughly 3 to 8 gigawatts across the fleet. Constellation is spending about $4.3 billion to add roughly a gigawatt across eleven reactors; Vistra is adding 433 megawatts at three plants, with Meta buying the power. No reactor that can run safely should be retired early, and license extensions to eighty years should be routine.


### 2. Data centers bring their own power


Large new data centers should build their own generation, behind the meter, or pay the full cost of the grid capacity they use. In the near term that means natural gas: combined-cycle plants where turbines can be had, and reciprocating engines (which can be delivered in two to three years) where they cannot. The gas-turbine market is badly backed up; the largest manufacturer is now selling delivery slots for 2031, and the cost of new combined-cycle plants has risen to around $2,000 per kilowatt. This calls for patience and realism, not for imposing data center demand on residential grids.


Natural gas burned in power plants contains no mercury and emits roughly half the carbon dioxide per unit of electricity as coal. People disagree on how much weight to give carbon dioxide. Whatever one’s view, gas built by data centers on their own sites, with a written path to switch to nuclear power when it becomes available, is the most practical near-term bridge.


### 3. Make data centers flexible


A 2025 Duke University study found that if large new loads agreed to curtail their use for a tiny fraction of the year (on the order of half a percent of hours, typically a couple of hours at a time during peak demand), the existing grid could accommodate roughly 76 to 100 gigawatts of new demand without new generation. Much computing work, especially AI model training, can pause or shift. Interconnection rules should reward data centers that accept this flexibility with faster connection.


### 4. Build enhanced geothermal on western lands


Enhanced geothermal uses oil-and-gas drilling techniques to tap the earth’s heat almost anywhere hot rock lies near the surface, which in America is mainly the West. It produces firm, around-the-clock power with a small footprint. Fervo Energy’s Cape Station in Utah is bringing its first 100 megawatts online in late 2026 and early 2027, with 400 more megawatts slated for 2028, and claims a pipeline of over 50 gigawatts. Its costs are still high, about $5,500 per kilowatt, but falling. Faster federal permitting for geothermal drilling on public lands is one of the most effective things a president can do.


### 5. Start the new reactors in this term


New reactors begun in 2029–2033 will produce power in the next term. They must be started anyway.


- **Light-water small modular reactors:** GE Hitachi’s 300-megawatt BWRX-300 is under construction at Darlington in Ontario, with grid connection planned by the end of 2030, and the NRC has approved the same design for construction at TVA’s Clinch River site in Tennessee.

- **Large reactors:** the Department of Energy has offered $17.5 billion in conditional loans for up to ten Westinghouse AP1000 reactors, with construction targeted to begin by 2030. The AP1000 is the most “off-the-shelf” large reactor available: two are already operating at Vogtle, and operators have learned hard lessons.

- **Advanced reactors for data centers:** Amazon, X-energy, and AEP announced plans in October 2026 to explore twelve 80-megawatt gas-cooled reactors (960 MW) at a former coal plant site in Indiana; Kairos is building for Google in Tennessee; Valar Atomics has proposed a large reactor-powered data center campus on federal land in Utah.


The federal government’s role is to keep the licensing process predictable, finance the first units of each design through repayable loans, and ensure a design built once is built many times.


### 6. Remote siting, step by step


Moving the largest data centers to remote places, powered by their own reactors and cooled by air, is the right long-term picture. In this term, it can begin only with natural gas, as Alaska’s first North Slope proposal shows, and even that has met heavy public opposition. A remote campus is worth building only if the lease pays the public a real share and the site is built to switch to nuclear power when it can.


### 7. Protect the neighbor


Every policy above should carry the protections I set out in my earlier paper, *Power Before Servers*: the one who causes the cost pays the cost; no eminent domain for private data center power lines; night-time limits on low-frequency noise measured at the nearest home; and independent measurement of power quality near data centers.

## Part V. Robots and desalination


Abundant energy is not only for computing. Two hopes deserve a word.


**Desalination** is less energy-hungry than most people assume. Modern reverse-osmosis plants use about 3 to 4 kilowatt-hours to make a cubic meter of fresh water from seawater. One gigawatt of steady power can therefore produce roughly 6 to 8 million cubic meters of fresh water a day, about 1.6 to 2.1 billion gallons. Energy is a factor in the cost of desalinated water, but so are the plants themselves and brine disposal. Cheaper energy makes desalination more affordable; it does not make it free on its own.


**Robots** will add demand gradually, as they are built and deployed. Their energy needs will grow with the economy and are best met by the same strategy: firm power built steadily, in series, at falling cost.

## Part VI. The plan for the 2029–2033 term


**Year one (2029):**


- Direct the NRC and DOE to set firm decision deadlines for uprates, license extensions, restarts, and new reactor applications.

- Fund expansion of fuel enrichment (including 20-percent-enriched fuel) and restart domestic lithium-7 production.

- Launch the thorium mission with two or three competing designs and published milestones.

- Issue rules requiring large new data centers to bring their own generation or pay full grid costs, and to accept flexible operation in exchange for faster connection.

- Open federal lands to accelerated geothermal permitting.


**Years two and three (2030–2031):**


- First new small modular reactors connect to the grid in North America; AP1000 construction begins at multiple sites.

- Enhanced geothermal expands across the West.

- Thorium test loops and a small thorium-bearing test reactor operate.


**Years four and five (2032–2033):**


- Thorium demonstration reactor reaches criticality (the program’s stated goal).

- The second and third units of each new reactor design are under construction, at a lower cost than the first.

- The data center boom is powered by its own generation, existing nuclear plants, flexible operation, and the first new reactors, without residential ratepayers carrying its costs.


---


## Conclusion


I would rather tell the truth about energy than sell a dream. No revolutionary machine is waiting in a warehouse to be installed in 2029. What we have is better than a dream: proven technologies America has forgotten how to build quickly, an abundant fuel in thorium we abandoned for reasons that no longer apply, and a generation of private capital eager to pay for power.


The plan is simple: keep and expand what works now, make those who profit from the data center boom pay its costs, build new reactors in series so their costs fall, and lay the thorium foundation so the 2030s and 2040s bring the abundance this decade cannot.


I hold this plan because I believe it is right, and I’m open to a better argument. If someone shows me a faster, cheaper, or safer path to abundant energy that protects the neighbor, I will take it.
