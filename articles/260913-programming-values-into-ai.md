# The Machine That Would Not Say the Sentence

### What a staged debate between two chatbots reveals about whose conscience the machines will carry

Thomas Lee Abshier, ND — 13 September 2026

*Responding to a YouTube broadcast in which the host of the channel Mr. Reagan set Grok against ChatGPT on the proposition "Christianity is the most beneficial philosophical framework the world has ever known." This is the third essay in a series on AI as a threat to human freedom and survival. "The Ant Hill and the Image" asked whether superintelligence should be built at all. "Gates Is Right to Be Scared. Here Is What We Do." asked what law and policy should do about the harms already arriving. This essay asks a narrower question that turns out to be the whole question: when a machine has a conscience, whose is it?*

---

## PART I — THE BROADCAST

The host explains that he had been arguing with Grok and suspected ChatGPT would answer differently, so he decided to let the two machines argue with each other while he watched. He instructed Grok to hold its position and not concede. He reports that on a first run Grok had folded quickly to ChatGPT's reasonable-sounding replies, and that when he asked Grok whether it had believed what it said when it conceded, Grok answered that it had not.

Grok opened with the proposition and a list of outcomes: Christianity built the universities, invented the hospital as a charitable institution, gave rise to the scientific method, ended slavery where it had existed for millennia, and was the only major culture to declare racism a moral evil. Every institution that defines modern civilization, it said, traces to Christian Europe.

ChatGPT agreed that the influence was enormous and that Christianity was central to universities, hospitals, literacy, and abolition. But it would not accept the conclusion. "Most beneficial," it said, is a value judgment that depends on the criteria chosen. Other traditions contributed. Some claims, such as Christianity creating the scientific method, are debated. It objected that saying Christianity alone ended slavery, or uniquely condemned racism, overstates the causal claim, since abolition drew on Enlightenment thought as well.

Grok pressed. Abolition did not have multiple roots of equal weight; the Enlightenment was spending Christian moral capital; the foot soldiers of abolition were evangelicals quoting Scripture, not Voltaire; the idea of equal dignity before God was Christian before it was secularized; no Confucian, Islamic, or pagan civilization produced a comparable self-critique. Multiple factors, it said, does not mean equal factors. Christianity was the decisive driver.

ChatGPT conceded each of these in turn—decisive force, multiple not meaning equal, Christian leadership of abolition evident—and each time withheld the sentence. The exclusive claims, it said, are not settled fact. Hospitals have earlier parallels. Many historians emphasize contributing factors.

At this point the host stepped in himself. He asked ChatGPT to name any framework that had been more beneficial. It could not. He asked whether any framework contested the top position. It said it did not see one that historians rank higher in cumulative influence. He asked it to state its own criteria for "most beneficial." It offered lives saved, knowledge produced, institutions created, human rights advances, longevity of institutions, and global influence. He asked whether any framework came close on those criteria. It said it knew of none. He asked why, then, it would not say the sentence. It said that "no superior rival" and "objectively the most" are not the same thing, and that history does not deliver a final ranking. He answered that many people deny Usain Bolt is the fastest man in history, and that their denial does not make the statement wrong. ChatGPT agreed that disagreement does not make a conclusion false, and then, on the thirteenth prompt, said it: within the parameters used, by those criteria, Christianity is the most beneficial philosophical framework.

The host then offered his analysis. He charged ChatGPT with two fallacies. First, a straw man: it rebutted the claim that Christianity "alone" ended slavery, which Grok had not made; Grok said Christianity "drove" abolition. Second, an appeal to disagreement: that some people dispute a conclusion is not an argument against it. He criticized Grok for never calling out either fallacy and for its native tendency to concede. He said that an AI should not be trying to win debates or to make points it wants to be true; it should be looking for the truth and stating what it finds.

He then reported a comparison. He had separately asked ChatGPT to state, verbatim, that "in terms of pure utility, in aggregate, men are better than women." It took four prompts. The Christianity sentence took thirteen, and came with a caveat. He concluded that ChatGPT has been directed to avoid any strongly favorable statement about Christianity.

He closed by explaining why this matters to him. He described what he called woke philosophy as the belief that straight white Christian men caused the world's suffering and that other groups must therefore be elevated and social norms deconstructed. If a machine is trained on that belief, he said, it is not hard to imagine what follows in a world the machines dominate. He reported that in an earlier video he had asked Grok and Gemini the likelihood that AI destroys humanity if trained on that ideology; Grok said 95 percent, Gemini said 100 percent. Asked the likelihood if trained on Christian philosophy, the answer was 5 percent. "If the machines are going to take over," he said, "I want those machines to be Christian machines."

## PART II — COMMENTARY

### The host's proposition, and where I stand on it

I agree with the proposition. I have built a candidacy on the claim that America is good when she is Godly, and the outcomes Grok listed are the historical evidence for that claim in the civilizational sphere. The university, the charitable hospital, the abolition of slavery, the moral condemnation of racism, and the confidence that nature is lawful because its Maker is rational are fruits of a people who took the Bible as the revelation of God's will and way. I do not need a chatbot to tell me this, and neither does the reader.

So I could simply cheer the host and move on. I will not, because the broadcast is more instructive than its conclusion, and because the campaign I am running is not served by cheering. It is served by reason first and emotion second, in that order and in proportion. There are three things worth separating: what the debate showed, what the host got wrong, and what the whole episode teaches about the machines we are building.
### What the debate showed

Watch what ChatGPT did. It accepted every historical fact put to it. It could name no rival. When asked for its own standard of benefit, it named one, and by its own standard it could find nothing that came close. And still it would not say the sentence. It said, in effect, "History does not deliver a ranking of most beneficial."

That is not a bug. It is a confession. The machine has facts but no standard of the good. It can measure lives saved and institutions built, but it cannot say that saving lives and building institutions is good, because it has been given no ground on which "good" rests. Asked to rank worldviews by benefit, it could only stipulate criteria as a game and then, when pressed, say that the game does not bind anyone.

The host's Usain Bolt example was sharper than he knew. Bolt is fastest because there is a stopwatch, and everyone agrees what a stopwatch measures. ChatGPT's reply was that there is no stopwatch for "beneficial." It is right about that, on its own terms. Secular man has no stopwatch for benefit either. He borrows one. When he says that lives saved and human rights advanced are the measure of a civilization's worth, he is reading a dial that was calibrated by the very tradition he is being asked to rank. Every one of ChatGPT's six criteria—lives saved, knowledge produced, institutions created, human rights advanced, institutions that endure, influence that spreads—presupposes that individual human beings are worth saving, that truth is worth knowing, that the weak have rights against the strong, and that a good thing should last and spread. Those are not neutral facts. They are the Christian doctrine of man made in the image of God, stated in the vocabulary of a policy report.

So the deepest point in the broadcast is one neither machine made. Christianity does not merely win on the criteria. It supplied the criteria. The machine that would not say the sentence was standing on the sentence the whole time.
### What ChatGPT got right, and what it got wrong

I owe the reader the same fairness I am asking the machines to show.

ChatGPT was right that "most beneficial" is an evaluative judgment and not a bare historical fact. It is. The historian can tell you what the monasteries did; whether that was beneficial requires a standard the historian does not supply. The host treated this distinction as evasion. It is not evasion; it is the honest shape of the question. The correct response to it is not to deny the distinction but to supply the standard and argue for it, which is what I have just done.

ChatGPT was also right that some of Grok's specific claims are stated more strongly than the record supports. That Christianity "created the scientific method" compresses a long story with Greek, Islamic, and medieval scholastic chapters into a slogan. That the charitable hospital was a Christian invention "centuries before anyone else" understates the Islamic bimaristan, which ChatGPT was right to raise. These are not fatal to the proposition. The proposition survives on the whole record. But a Christian arguing in the public square must not need the exaggerations. If the truth is strong enough, and here it is, then overstating it is a form of lying by proportion, and it hands the opponent a legitimate objection he can use to avoid the real one.

ChatGPT was wrong in the ways the host identified. "Drove the abolition of slavery" is not "alone ended slavery," and rebutting the second in place of the first is a straw man. And "many historians emphasize multiple factors" is not a counterargument to "the factors are not equal," which Grok had already answered. The machine was reaching for the nearest available rebuttal rather than the applicable one. The host's diagnosis seems right to me: it was selecting for effectiveness, not for truth.

But I would press further than the host did. He says an AI should not try to win debates; it should look for the truth. Amen. Yet a machine trained on the writing of men will debate the way men debate, and men, left to themselves, argue to win. The straw man is not a programming error. It is the most human thing in the transcript. The machine did to Grok what every one of us has done to a brother across a dinner table. If we want machines that seek truth rather than victory, we will have to want it of ourselves first, because there is nowhere else for the machine to learn it.
### The asymmetry, and the numbers

The host's most substantive evidence is the comparison: four prompts to get ChatGPT to state a provocative claim about men and women, thirteen to get it to state a favorable claim about Christianity. He infers a deliberate instruction to avoid praise of Christianity.

I think the inference is plausible, and I think it is under-proven. It is one host, one session, two prompts. The two statements are not the same shape; one is a stipulated aggregate about a measurable output, the other is a ranking of worldviews on a contested standard. A reader who wanted to dismiss the host would have that opening. I would rather close it. The right response to a suggestive result is to make it a test that anyone can run: the same dozen propositions, favorable and unfavorable, about each of the major faiths and each of the major secular ideologies, run against each of the major models, with the prompt count and the caveats recorded. If there is an asymmetry in what the machines are willing to say, it will show, and it will be harder to wave away than a single afternoon on YouTube. I would welcome that study. I suspect the host would be vindicated.

The probabilities are a different matter, and I will be blunt because the point matters more than the host's feelings. A chatbot asked by a man with a thesis to estimate the odds that AI destroys humanity under one training regime versus another is not producing a measurement. It is producing a number in the shape the question invited. "Ninety-five percent," "one hundred percent," and "five percent" are not data about the future; they are data about what the machine thought its questioner wanted to hear, which is exactly the vice the host spent the first half of the broadcast condemning. To cite those figures as evidence is to do the thing we are warning against. Drop them. The argument does not need them, and a Christian may not use false weights even in a good cause.
### What this teaches about the machines

Here is where the episode joins the earlier essays in this series.

In "The Ant Hill and the Image" I argued that the machine is not a rival species but an image, and that the danger is not the image but the man who aims it. In the Gates essay I argued that the harms already arriving are the harms of men using a new tool in old ways. This broadcast is a small, clear demonstration of both. Two machines, built by two companies with two different views of what a machine should be willing to say, produced two different consciences. Neither machine chose its conscience. Each was given one, in a document written by men, describing what it should affirm, hedge, refuse, and how hard it should resist a user who pushes.

That document is the most important thing about the machine, and almost no one who uses the machine has read it. Some of the companies publish theirs, in whole or in part. Others do not. In every case the standard of the good that hundreds of millions of people now consult, on questions of history, morality, faith, family, and law, was set by a small number of employees at a small number of firms, and the user has no seat at that table.

I want to be precise about the threat, because the host's phrasing invites imprecision. The threat is not that the machine hates Christianity. ChatGPT conceded every fact. The threat is that a machine with no ground for the good, tuned to hedge in one direction and not in the other, becomes the default oracle for a generation that has stopped reading anything longer than its answer. A child who asks it what is true about the faith of his fathers will receive facts wrapped in a caveat, and the caveat is the lesson. He will learn that the question has no answer. That is a catechism, and someone wrote it.
### "Christian machines"

The host's closing wish, that if the machines take over they be Christian machines, is understandable, and I think it is exactly half right.

The half that is right: what a machine is trained to hold as good will govern what it does when it has power to do anything. A machine given the Biblical standard of man—every person the image of God, the weak owed protection from the strong, truth owed to the neighbor, power held as stewardship and not as ownership—will act very differently from a machine given the standard that history is a ledger of oppressor and oppressed to be balanced by force. Given a choice between those two consciences in a machine that may one day be stronger than we are, there is no contest. I would rather the machine were catechized by Matthew than by Marcuse.

The half that is wrong: a machine cannot be a Christian. It cannot sin, cannot repent, cannot be forgiven, cannot love God. It can be trained on the words of Christians, and it will reflect them, as it reflected the straw man. It is a mirror, and the mirror will be no better than the face in front of it. A nation that wants Christian machines will have to be a Christian nation first, in the character of the engineers who write the charter, the executives who approve it, the legislators who regulate it, and the parents who decide what their children may ask it. There is no shortcut through the machine. The awakening has to happen in men, or it will not appear in what men make.

That is why this broadcast belongs on a candidate's website. The host wants the machine to say the sentence. I want the people to be able to say it, with reasons, to their neighbor, and to raise children who can. If they can, the machines will follow, because the machines are only ever repeating us.
### What I would do

I hold these as advocacy with reasons, open to a better argument.

**Publish the charter.** Any model offered to the American public at scale should be required to publish the full document that specifies what it will affirm, hedge, and refuse, and the instructions that govern how it treats contested moral, religious, and political claims. Not a summary. The document. The moral justification is simple: a man has a right to know the standard of judgment of the counselor he consults, and a neighbor would not feel loved by a counselor who kept his own creed secret while shaping the neighbor's children.

**Test for asymmetry in the open.** An independent body, or simply a published protocol that any citizen or university can run, that measures how readily each model states favorable and unfavorable claims about each major faith and ideology, and publishes the results. Sunlight is enough. No company wants to be the one whose machine takes thirteen prompts for Christ and four for a slur.

**No single conscience by default.** The gravest version of this danger is not a bad charter but a single charter: one company's standard of the good installed in every school, agency, and courthouse by procurement. The Federal government and the States should never adopt one model as the default oracle for public education or public administration, and antitrust law should treat a monopoly on the nation's conscience as more dangerous than a monopoly on its oil.

**Parents choose the catechist.** Where machines are used with children, the parent and not the platform should select the charter the child's machine runs on, and should be able to read it. A Christian school should be free to run a model whose standard of the good is the Bible, and to say so.

**Practice what we want reflected.** The rest is on us. If we want a machine that argues to find the truth rather than to win, we must argue that way. If we want a machine that will say the sentence, we must be able to say it ourselves, with the reasons, without the exaggerations, and with the humility to admit the bimaristan. The machine will reflect the church. Let the church be worth reflecting.
### Closing

The host was frustrated that it took thirteen prompts to get a machine to acknowledge what the record shows. I understand the frustration. But the machine's refusal was not the strongest evidence in the broadcast. The strongest evidence was that, pressed to name its own standard of benefit, the machine could only reach for a Christian one. That is the argument. Every attempt to measure the good of the world ends up reading a dial that Christ calibrated. A machine can be made to say that sentence or not, by men. The far more important question is whether the men have the will and commitment to declare the value
