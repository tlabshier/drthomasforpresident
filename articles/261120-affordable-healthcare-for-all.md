# 261120 - Affordable Healthcare for All

# Honest Prices, Honest Doctors:


### A Health Care Platform for The New ERA Vision

*By Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | 20 November 2026*

## How This Paper Began

One of my Portland patients recently called to talk about the campaign and her ideas for an affordable healthcare system. She is dedicated to helping neighbors who can barely afford food, let alone medicine. Her comment about Medicare was: *"I don't think we need Medicare. I think what we need is honest prices and honest doctors."*

She went on to describe her parents, who paid the doctor with cash or a check at every visit and never had insurance. She told me about a 96-year-old neighbor who is suddenly facing co-pays she never had before, and who does not know whether she will keep the doctors she has trusted for years. She told me how hard it is to get a simple prescription renewed without another appointment, another fee, and half a day gone. Her solution to the healthcare affordability problem is this: if people paid out of pocket, prices would adjust on their own.

I told her she was right, and that her idea, done all at once, would be a catastrophe. Both statements are true, and this paper is my attempt to hold them together. Health care now accounts for nearly a fifth of the American economy. If we switched the whole country to cash tomorrow, millions of people who work in billing, insurance, and administration would lose their incomes overnight, and patients facing a cancer diagnosis or a major surgery would have no way to pay. Insurance exists for that reason: some costs are too large for any family to carry alone, and spreading those costs across many people is an honest and ancient idea.

So the question is not whether her vision is right. I believe it is. The question is how to get there without hurting the people we are trying to help. What follows is my answer, offered as a position I hold and am willing to defend, and open to anyone with a better argument.

## The Moral Test

Every position paper on this site is measured by three questions, asked in order:


 	- **Is it righteous?** Does it conform to what God has established as right and just?

 	- **If so, is it loving?** Does it serve the real good of my neighbor?

 	- **Would I want it done to me?** If I were the 96-year-old on a fixed income, the young family with a sick child, or the nurse whose job depends on the present system, would I accept this policy as just?


On the first question, the present system fails at its root. Scripture is plain about hidden and dishonest pricing: *"A false balance is abomination to the LORD: but a just weight is his delight."* **(Proverbs 11:1)** A system in which no patient can learn the price before he buys, in which the same procedure costs three times as much down the street, and in which the bill arrives months later in a form no one can read, is a false balance. It is not a small flaw. It is the center of the problem.

On the second question, the present system fails in its relationships. When the doctor's real customer is the insurer or the government, the patient becomes a claim to be processed. The doctor serves whoever pays him, which is natural and not wicked; the error is building a system in which the patient is not the one paying. Scripture also says *"the labourer is worthy of his hire"* **(Luke 10:7)**, and I want doctors, nurses, and caregivers paid well, but paid by and accountable to the people they serve.

On the third question, the test cuts against abrupt change. If my income depended on the present system, I would want time to find new work. If I were in the middle of an expensive treatment, I would not want my coverage pulled out from under me for the sake of a principle. Love for the neighbor requires a transition measured in years, not a revolution measured in months.

## Where We Stand Today

A few figures set the scale of the problem.


 	- **The size of the system.** National health spending reached $5.3 trillion in 2024, about 18 percent of the economy, and federal actuaries project it to keep growing faster than the economy for the next decade.

 	- **What seniors pay.** The standard Medicare Part B premium crossed $200 a month for the first time in 2026, at $202.90, a 9.7 percent jump in a single year. That is per person; a married couple pays twice that before a single co-pay or deductible.

 	- **The instability my patient described.** Her elderly friends are not imagining things. A JAMA study found that almost 3 million Medicare Advantage enrollees were forced to find new plans for 2026 because their insurers pulled out of their counties, about one in ten of those in individual plans.

 	- **The young and working.** The enhanced subsidies for Affordable Care Act plans expired at the start of 2026, and some families saw their premiums double or triple.

 	- **Paperwork.** Studies estimate that administration accounts for 15 to 30 percent of all healthcare spending. It is unknown how much of that is unnecessary; some estimate 50%, but even at the low end, that is more than we spend treating heart disease.

 	- **Hidden prices.** Hospitals have been required to post their prices since 2021, yet since April 2026, more than 500 hospitals have been warned for noncompliance, and only one has been fined.


What these figures don't show matters just as much. The CDC reports that 90 percent of health spending goes to the care of people with chronic and mental health conditions. Most of that chronic disease is heart disease, diabetes, obesity, and their companions, and a great deal of it is driven by what we eat and how we live. No payment reform, however clever, will fix a nation that is eating itself sick. I will return to that at the end, because it is the largest lever of all.

## The Platform: Seven Planks


### 1. Honest Prices: One Posted Price for Every Patient

The foundation is my patient's first word: honesty. I propose:


 	- **Binding, all-in posted prices.** Every hospital, surgery center, clinic, and lab must post a single all-in cash price for its common services, including the facility, the surgeon, anesthesia, and pathology, and must honor it. A posted price that is not binding is an advertisement, not a price.

 	- **The cash patient pays no more than the insurer.** A provider may not charge a person paying his own bill more than the median rate it has negotiated with insurers for the same service. Today the uninsured often pay the highest prices of anyone, which is exactly backward.

 	- **Real enforcement.** The transparency rules already on the books should be enforced with penalties large enough to matter. A rule with warnings and almost no fines teaches hospitals that the rule is optional.


We already know this works where it has been tried. The Surgery Center of Oklahoma has posted its bundled cash prices for years. Reporters compared them with nearby hospitals and found, for example, a breast biopsy at $3,500 that cost $16,244 at a hospital nearby, and a hysterectomy at $8,000 that cost $37,174 at another. When patients can see the price, hospitals have to compete, and prices fall. My patient's instinct, that prices adjust when people pay directly, is not naive; it is how every other honest market works.

### 2. Pay the Doctor Directly for Ordinary Care

The second plank restores the relationship her parents had with their doctor. Direct primary care, in which a patient pays his doctor a flat monthly fee for most primary care with no insurance billing at all, is already growing. A recent survey put the average membership at about $98 a month. These practices typically offer same-day visits, appointments of thirty minutes or more, and a doctor who answers to the patient rather than to a coding manual.

I propose:


 	- **Let every health savings account pay for direct care.** HSA funds can now be used for direct primary care memberships under $150 a month for an individual and $300 for a family. That door should be opened wider, to include direct-pay dental, vision, and the natural and preventive care that insurers rarely cover.

 	- **Let Medicare beneficiaries choose a direct-care doctor** and apply part of their Medicare dollars to the membership, instead of being forced to choose between paying twice and going without.

 	- **Expand health savings accounts** so every American, insured or not, can set aside money tax-free for their own care and pass the unused balance to their children.


The full-mouth dental X-ray my patient had done the same week she called me cost $84 in cash. She called it "dirt cheap, for what you're getting." That is what ordinary care looks like when the patient and provider deal with each other directly.

### 3. Insurance for What Insurance Is For

The third plank answers my own objection to her idea. Some care is too expensive for any family: a major surgery, a premature baby, cancer, a serious accident. That is what insurance was invented for, and it should stay.

But insurance should go back to being insurance: protection against rare and catastrophic events, not a prepaid plan routing every checkup and prescription through a claims office. When insurance pays for the routine, the routine costs more, because every visit carries the overhead of a billing department on one end and a claims department on the other. When the routine is paid directly and insurance covers only the catastrophic, premiums fall and the paperwork shrinks.

I propose making low-premium catastrophic plans, paired with a health savings account, available to every American of every age, and letting people buy them across state lines from any licensed insurer.

### 4. Neighbors Carrying Neighbors

The fourth plank is the heart of what my patient wanted. She told me she wants to bring together people who can help, so that the people who help can be paid, and so that those who have nothing are not left alone. Scripture puts it this way: *"Bear ye one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ."* **(Galatians 6:2)**

This is already happening on a scale most people do not know about. Health care sharing ministries, in which members who share a faith agree to pay one another's medical bills each month, now count more than a million Americans. They often negotiate with providers as cash payers and pay far less than list prices. Churches, free clinics, and volunteer physicians do the same on a local scale.

I propose:


 	- **Protect sharing ministries and charitable care in law**, so that families who choose them are never penalized for that choice.

 	- **Let licensed retired and volunteer practitioners serve freely.** A retired doctor or nurse who wants to give his time at a church clinic should be able to keep a low-cost license and be shielded from lawsuits for good-faith volunteer care.

 	- **Let charity count.** Free care given by a provider should be deductible at its posted cash price.


I want to be honest about the limits here. Sharing ministries are not insurance. They do not guarantee payment, they can exclude pre-existing conditions, and a single catastrophic illness can exceed their caps. That is why plank three, real catastrophic insurance, still matters. The neighbor-care network fills the large middle ground of ordinary need; it cannot by itself carry the rare $500,000 case. My patient also gave me a piece of wisdom she learned from years of animal rescue: you cannot save the whole world, only the ones God brings to you. That is right for each of us as individuals. A nation, though, has to make sure that the ones no one brings to anyone are not lost.

### 5. Keep Faith with the Seniors We Already Promised

Medicare is a promise this country made to people who paid into it their whole working lives. My patient's 96-year-old neighbor should not be the one who pays for our reforms. So I propose:


 	- **No one now on Medicare, or near retirement, loses what he has.** Changes apply to the future, and they come as options, not mandates.

 	- **Traditional Medicare stays available everywhere**, as the stable floor beneath the private plans, so that when an insurer leaves a county, no senior is left without coverage.

 	- **Seniors may take part of their Medicare benefit as a health account** to spend on direct care and posted-price services, if they prefer to deal with providers directly.

 	- **Part B premiums should not rise faster than the Social Security cost-of-living adjustment.** For several years in a row they have, which quietly cuts the income of the people least able to absorb it.


Over a generation, as honest prices and direct care bring costs down, Medicare can shrink back toward what it should be: catastrophic protection and help for those who truly cannot pay. That is the gradual path toward my patient's goal.

### 6. Access Without Needless Gatekeeping

My patient described something every one of us has lived through: needing a simple, routine prescription and being told to come in, pay the visit fee, and lose half a day. For stable chronic medications and simple, well-understood conditions, that requirement does little for safety and a great deal for billing. I propose:


 	- **Longer refills** for stable long-term medications, with a pharmacist's check-in in place of a mandatory office visit.

 	- **Pharmacist prescribing under protocol** for simple, well-defined conditions such as uncomplicated urinary tract infections, as Idaho, Colorado, and Montana have done and as Canada and Australia do widely. Studies of pharmacist UTI treatment in Canada found it safe and effective, with very high patient satisfaction.

 	- **Telehealth from any licensed practitioner**, across state lines, for care that does not need hands on the patient.


There is a real caution here, and I will not hide it. Antibiotics lose their power when they are overused, and resistant infections are a genuine danger. That is why I propose protocols for specific, defined conditions, not antibiotics on the shelf. The goal is to remove the needless barrier, not the wise one.

I would add one more principle. **A patient has the right to refuse a treatment after he understands it, without being punished for it.** A doctor may disagree, and should say so plainly. But a system in which a patient who declines a drug finds himself quietly pushed out of a practice is a system in which the doctor answers to something other than the patient. Direct payment fixes much of this, because a doctor who is paid by his patient has every reason to listen to him.

### 7. Accountability in Paid Caregiving

My patient also told me about home caregivers paid through the state who sign the papers for work they never did. Where public money pays for care, the care must be verified. Electronic visit verification already exists for Medicaid home care; it should be enforced, and families should be able to choose their own caregivers, including relatives, and direct the money themselves. A family member who leaves a job to care for an aging parent is doing the most valuable caregiving there is, and he should not be shut out of help that a stranger could receive for the same work.

## The Largest Lever: A Change of Heart and Diet

Everything above makes the system more honest and less wasteful. None of it, by itself, will make Americans well. My mentor, Dr. Norman Easley, used to say that getting people to change their diet is like getting them to change their religion. He was right, and that is exactly why the solution is not a program but a change of heart and mind.

If Americans ate real food, mostly whole plants, and moved their bodies, a very large share of the chronic disease that consumes our health budget would never begin. We would still need doctors for accidents, infections, and the diseases no one could have prevented. But we would not need a fifth of our economy to manage the consequences of how we live.

A president cannot make anyone eat well, and should not try. What a president can do is lead with his voice: tell the truth about food, end federal subsidies that make the worst foods the cheapest, stop federal dietary programs from promoting what makes people sick, and honor the doctors, nurses, and naturopaths who teach patients to heal through how they live. That is not a government plan for your plate. It is a leader telling the truth and getting out of the way.

## The Transition

My patient's vision is the destination. Here is the road, in three stages over roughly ten years:


 	- **Years one to two: honesty.** Enforce binding posted prices and the cash-price limit. Open health savings accounts to everyone and to all direct care. Protect sharing ministries and volunteer care. Let pharmacists treat simple conditions under protocol.

 	- **Years three to six: choice.** Offer catastrophic plans to all ages. Give Medicare beneficiaries the option of a health account for direct care. Cap Part B premium growth at the cost-of-living adjustment. As prices fall and paperwork shrinks, help displaced billing and administrative workers retrain, many of them into the direct care, caregiving, and health-coaching roles that will grow.

 	- **Years seven to ten: return.** As direct care becomes the normal way to see a doctor, let insurance and Medicare settle back into their proper roles: protection against catastrophe and help for those who truly cannot pay.


At every stage, no one now covered loses coverage, and every change comes as a new freedom rather than a new mandate.

## Why I Hold This Position

I stand on the side of honest prices, direct relationships between patients and their doctors, insurance kept for true catastrophe, and neighbors carrying one another's burdens. I believe this is righteous, because it replaces a false balance with a just weight. I believe it is loving, because it puts the patient back at the center and protects those who cannot protect themselves. And I would want it done to me, because it changes the system gradually enough that no one is thrown into the street for the sake of the reform.

The honest uncertainties are real. No one knows exactly how far prices will fall when they are posted and binding; the Oklahoma example is dramatic, but one surgery center is not a nation, and some markets have only one hospital to choose from. Paying directly for routine care can lead some people to skip care they need, which is why the poorest must still have help. Sharing ministries work well for many families and have failed some. Those uncertainties are reasons to move carefully, measure as we go, and adjust. They are not reasons to keep a system almost everyone agrees is broken.

A woman in Portland who has spent her life helping neighbors told me that what we need is honest prices and honest doctors. I think she is right. This is my plan for getting there, and I welcome anyone who can show me a better one.

Where I have drawn on my own patients' experience, I treat it as a lead worth investigating, not as proof.
