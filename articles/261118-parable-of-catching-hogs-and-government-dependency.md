# 261118 - Parable of Catching Hogs and Government Dependency

# The Corn and the Gate:


### How a Free People Is Caught

*By Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | 18 November 2026*

## The Story

The social media post goes like this:

"Do you know how to catch wild pigs?"

You find a spot in the woods, he said, and put corn on the ground. The pigs find it and come back every day. Once they are used to coming, you build one side of a fence. They hesitate, then go back to eating. You add a second side, then a third. Finally you build the fourth side with a gate in it. The pigs walk through the gate for the corn as they always have, and you slam it shut. They run in circles for a while, then settle down and eat, because they have forgotten how to forage in the woods. They accept captivity.

This is what is happening in America.

I think the story is worth taking seriously.

## The Trap Is Real

This isn't just a fable. It is very close to how wild hogs are actually trapped today. Agricultural extension services in Texas, Alabama, Florida, and other states tell landowners to "pre-bait" a corral trap: put corn inside, wire the gate open, and let the whole group of hogs (the sounder) get comfortable going in and out, sometimes for as long as two weeks. Only when the whole sounder is feeding inside do you set the gate. The Texas A&M guidance says it plainly: trapping hogs is a process, not an event.

That is the point of the parable. The pigs are not driven into the pen. They walk in, one comfortable habit at a time, and each new section of fence seems small next to yesterday's corn. Nobody uses force until the very end, and by then force hardly matters.

## The Question Under the Question

Is this a parable about our movement toward "Communism/Socialism?" The danger to a free people is not mainly a revolution that takes their liberty. It is a provider that takes over their responsibilities, so that liberty becomes something they no longer know how to use.

Alexis de Tocqueville saw this coming in 1840. He warned that democracies might fall not to tyrants but to an "immense and tutelary power" that looks after its citizens' needs, makes their lives easy, and keeps them in a permanent childhood. That power would not break men's wills, he wrote. It would soften them and steer them.

Scripture saw it first. When Israel asked Samuel for a king like the other nations had, God told Samuel to warn them about what that king would take: their sons for his chariots, their daughters for his kitchens, the best of their fields, and a tenth of their grain and flocks. The warning ends: *"and ye shall be his servants.** And ye shall cry out in that day because of your king which ye shall have chosen you"* **(1 Samuel 8:17–18)**. They chose the arrangement and asked for it. The gate shut on people who walked in.

## Where the Email Needs Correcting, and Why That Makes the Point Stronger

I hold to the parable's central warning.

**The post's list of "free corn" lumps unlike things together.** It puts welfare in the same list as tobacco subsidies, dairy supports, and payments to farmers for not planting (the Conservation Reserve Program). Tobacco price supports were actually bought out and ended in 2004, which shows how old the email is. The deeper point is that much of the corn doesn't go to the poor at all. It goes to farmers, corporations, industries with lobbyists, and the middle class. The fence is not just around the poor. It is around almost all of us.

**Most benefits go to people who are old, disabled, or working.** Census survey data from the early 2010s found that about half of Americans lived in a household where someone received at least one government benefit. That number is striking. But an analysis from the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, which supports these programs and should be read with that in mind, found that more than nine-tenths of entitlement dollars went to households that were elderly, disabled, or working. The picture of a nation lounging at the trough is not accurate. The real picture is more troubling: a nation where working families, retirees, and businesses have all arranged their lives around the corn.

**"You can never hire someone to do a job cheaper than you can do it yourself" is not true as stated.** We hire plumbers, surgeons, and mechanics because they do the work better and cheaper than we could. What is true, and what the email was getting at, is that when government provides a service, the price is hidden, overhead and politics are added, and the person paying is not the person choosing. So it usually costs more than help given person to person, and nobody notices.

The trap is not set for "those people." It is set for everyone.

## The Fence Has a Name: The Benefits Cliff

The most important part of the parable is that the fence keeps the pigs from leaving, not just that they come in to eat. In our system, that fence exists, and economists have measured it.

Most assistance programs phase out as income rises. When several programs phase out at once, a family that earns more can lose more in benefits than it gains in wages. The Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, citing research by Altig and colleagues, reports that about a quarter of low-wage workers face lifetime effective tax rates above 70 percent on additional earnings. For those families, a raise, a promotion, or a marriage can leave the household worse off. Researchers call this a "benefits cliff." The plain name is a fence.

Nobody designed it to trap anyone. It grew one program at a time, each added for a good reason, the way the pig fence goes up one side at a time. But the result is a structure that punishes the very step that leads out of it: work, more work, marriage, and saving. People inside it are not foolish. They are responding rationally to the incentives we built.

## The Moral Test

I judge every policy by three questions. Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? And would I want it done to me, judged not by what I'd want in a weak moment but by the self I want to be before God?

**The pig trap fails all three.** It isn't righteous, because it treats people as livestock to be managed rather than as image-bearers of God, made to work, create, and provide. Scripture is direct: *"if any would not work, neither should he eat"* **(2 Thessalonians 3:10)**. Paul was not cruel to the needy. He was protecting their dignity. It isn't loving, because love does not want a neighbor to forget how to forage. And I would not want it done to me. Nobody, thinking clearly, wants to be fed in a pen.

**But abandoning the needy fails too.** God's law commanded Israel to leave the corners of the field and the fallen grain *"for the poor and stranger"* **(Leviticus 19:10)**. Look at how that law worked. The poor were not handed a meal. They were given access to the field, and they did the gleaning themselves. The help came through work, from neighbors, at the edge of a real economy. And the duty started at home: *"if any provide not for his own, and specially for those of his own house, he hath denied the faith"* **(1 Timothy 5:8)**, and the church was to care for its widows so that the wider community *"be not charged"* **(1 Timothy 5:16)**.

So the Biblical pattern is not "no help." It is help that keeps the forager foraging: close, personal, tied to work where work is possible, and generous without limit to those who truly cannot. Paul's goal for the reformed thief sums it up: *"let him labour, working with his hands the thing which is good, that he may have to give to him that needeth"* **(Ephesians 4:28)**. The aim is a person who moves from taking to working to giving.

## What I Would Do

Under the New ERA Vision, my approach comes from that pattern.


 	- **Make work always pay.** No family should ever be worse off for earning another dollar. Benefits should phase out gradually across programs, never at a cliff. This is the first section of fence to take down, and it can be done without cutting anyone off.

 	- **Move help closer to the neighbor.** Help should come first from family, then church, then the local community, and only last from distant government. I would use the president's voice to call churches and communities back to the work of the Good Samaritan. That work was taken from them gradually, and it can be taken back the same way.

 	- **Tie help to work wherever work is possible.** Follow the gleaning model: access, opportunity, and training rather than a check with nothing asked. Give fully and without shame to the elderly and to those who truly cannot work.

 	- **Stop feeding corn to the powerful.** Subsidies and carve-outs for industries with lobbyists are the same trap with nicer carpet. A country that asks the poor to stand on their own feet has to ask the same of agribusiness and corporations.

 	- **Keep faith with promises already made.** Retirees paid into Social Security and arranged their lives around it. Righteousness doesn't break a promise to fix a design flaw. We keep the promise and fix the design, honestly and over time.

 	- **Rebuild the forest.** Pigs stay in the pen partly because the woods have been cleared. People stay dependent partly because good work has left. Abundant, cheap energy is the base of my platform for exactly this reason: it brings back the factories, trades, and wages that make independence possible again.


## Why the Concern Remains Plausible

First, there is the plain sense that something is wrong. Most Americans can feel it. When half the households in a free country rely on government payments of some kind, and when politicians from both parties compete mainly by promising more, something has changed in the relationship between the citizen and the state. The numbers have caveats. The feeling is still pointing at something true.

Second, there is the money motive. Every program creates a group of recipients, administrators, and contractors who depend on it continuing. Each of those groups votes and lobbies. A provider that is also an employer and a patron has every reason to make itself necessary. This doesn't require a conspiracy, only ordinary self-interest acting the same way over decades.

Third, the doctrine and the system point in the same direction. A philosophy that treats the state as the main provider of security, a system of cliffs that makes leaving costly, and laws that grow each year in only one direction add up to a consistent pattern. When the belief, the structure, and the incentives all line up, the behavior that follows is not a coincidence.

Fourth, history backs the student's fear. His country's story, whatever country it was, is the story of many nations in the last century. The promise of provision came first. The loss of liberty came after.

## The Gate Is Still Open

Here is where the parable fails in our favor. Pigs cannot read the fence. We can. We can see each section going up, name it, and refuse it. We can take down the cliffs, return charity to neighbors, end corporate corn, and rebuild the forest of honest work. The gate hasn't slammed shut. But it closes the way it always does, quietly, while we are eating.

*"Go to the ant, thou sluggard; consider her ways, and be wise: which having no guide, overseer, or ruler, provideth her meat in the summer, and gathereth her food in the harvest"* **(Proverbs 6:6–8)**. No overseer, and no ruler. That is the picture of a free people. It is the picture I hope we choose.

God help us to see the fence while the gate is still open.

## Sources and Their Limits


 	- **The story.** This is a chain email that has circulated online since at least the 2000s. I could not find who wrote it or confirm the exchange student or the professor. I treat it as a parable, not a news report. The closing "quote for today" also has no confirmed author.

 	- **Hog trapping.** Texas A&M AgriLife Extension, *Placing and Baiting Feral Hog Traps* and *Box Traps for Feral Hogs*; Alabama Soil and Water Conservation Committee trap job sheet; University of Florida IFAS Extension. These are primary practitioner sources and are reliable on this point.

 	- **Benefit participation.** U.S. Census Bureau, Survey of Income and Program Participation, "More American Households Rely on Government Benefit Programs" (2013 infographic); Mercatus Center, "A Nation of Government Dependents?" These are early-2010s figures. Rates go up and down with the economy, and today's share may be different.

 	- **Who receives benefits.** Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, "Contrary to 'Entitlement Society' Rhetoric, Over Nine-Tenths of Entitlement Benefits Go to Elderly, Disabled, or Working Households" (2012). CBPP advocates for these programs. Its arithmetic is grounded in Census and budget data, but the way it defines "working household" counts anyone who worked at some point in the year, which flatters the result.

 	- **Benefits cliffs.** Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, *Mitigating Benefits Cliffs for Low-Income Families* (2023), citing Altig et al. (2023); Institute for Research on Poverty, University of Wisconsin; Pioneer Institute literature review. Economists agree that cliffs exist. They disagree about how much they actually change people's work decisions. A Pioneer review calls the behavioral evidence mixed, and HHS research finds that stable job offers can outweigh cliff effects.

 	- **Tocqueville.** *Democracy in America*, Volume II (1840), Book 4, Chapter 6.

 	- **Scripture.** King James Version.

 	- **A note on the record.** My sourcing leans on government statistics and established think tanks across the spectrum (Mercatus and Heritage on one side, CBPP on the other). Those sources measure what they choose to measure. Things like the slow loss of self-reliance, the habits families pass down, or the way dependence changes how people vote are hard to put in a survey. A lack of numbers on those things is not evidence that they aren't happening. Firsthand accounts from caseworkers, pastors, employers, and families who have lived inside the cliffs are worth hearing, and I treat them as leads rather than proof.
