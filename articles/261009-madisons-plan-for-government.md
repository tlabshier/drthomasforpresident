# 261009 Madison's Plan for Government

# The Hired Help: Madison’s Plan for Keeping Washington in Its Place


*Dr. Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | 09 October 2026*


## The Problem No Election Fixes


Every few years we are told that this election will finally restore the Constitution. We send new men to Washington, and they promise to shrink the federal government. Then the government grows anyway. The pattern is so steady that it deserves to be treated as a political law: a power asked to limit itself will almost never do so.


James Madison knew this before the Constitution was even ratified. In Federalist 48, he warned that “a mere demarcation on parchment” of each branch’s limits is not enough to stop the slide toward concentrated power. Words do not enforce themselves. Someone has to enforce them.


So the real question is not whether the Constitution limits Washington. It plainly does. The question is who holds Washington to those limits when it refuses. Madison answered. I think it is morally sound, and it can be misused in some places; I’ll say what I would do with it as president.


The Tenth Amendment Center recently published a clear summary of Madison’s argument (linked at the end). This paper builds on their work, but I want to ground it, as I ground everything on this site, in the moral question beneath the legal one.


## The Moral Foundation: Government Is the Servant


Before any strategy, we need the right picture of who works for whom.


Madison wrote in Federalist 46 that the federal and state governments “are in fact but different agents and trustees of the people, constituted with different powers, and designated for different purposes.” The people are the principal. Governments are agents. An agent who exceeds his commission is not exercising authority; he is overstepping it.


This is not merely a legal arrangement. It follows from what government is for. Every power the government holds is a power over a neighbor: to tax him, regulate him, command him, and, in the end, compel him by force. A power like that can be justified only if, when exercised, a reasonable neighbor could look at it and say, “This was done for my good and with my consent.” That is the test I apply to every policy: would my neighbor feel loved by this act in the public square? I note that feeling loved when one is disciplined requires the perspective of an adult who can see the discipline from God’s perspective and realize it was for his righteousness.


A government that takes powers beyond what Godly contract authorizes and pleases Him fails the principle of loving as Christ loved us. We can use the principle of consent of the governed on three levels, and a qualifier. 1) Does it please God, 2) Does it please our neighbor, and 3) does it please us? The qualifier, the new commandment Jesus Christ gave us, was to love, as He loved us. That love includes training in righteousness. A parent’s love disciplines to stop harmful behaviors, nourishes good behaviors, and sets guidelines for both. The love of neighbor and self must include both of these polarities. As people and States, we may do all lawful behaviors (not prohibited by God). But not every behavior is profitable or felt as being loved. For example, ruling a man without his consent, and without the justification of righteous principle, is the criteria of government. Resisting that overreach is therefore not rebellion against lawful authority. It defends lawful authority against unGodliness.


## What Washington Was Supposed to Do


Madison was specific about the division of labor. In Federalist 45 he wrote that the powers given to the federal government “are few and defined,” while those left to the states “are numerous and indefinite.” He said the federal powers would be exercised mainly on “external objects, as war, peace, negotiation, and foreign commerce.”


Everything touching “the lives, liberties and properties of the people,” and “the internal order, improvement, and prosperity of the State,” was to remain with the states.


Measure today’s Washington against that description and the gap is obvious. Education, health care, agriculture, local policing, land use, energy, food: the federal government now reaches into nearly every one of these through mandates, grants with strings, and agency rules that no voter ever approved.


## Madison’s Four Steps


Madison expected Washington to overreach. He also expected the people and the states to stop it, and he described how in Federalist 46. When a federal measure is unpopular in a state, whether because it is unconstitutional or simply bad policy, he said “the means of opposition to it are powerful and at hand.” He named four:


- **“The disquietude of the people.”** Public objection, loud and sustained, so that no official can pretend the people consent.

- **“Their repugnance and, perhaps, refusal to co-operate with the officers of the Union.”** In Madison’s day, “repugnant” carried the sense of contrary or resisting, not only disgusted. The people decline to help carry out the measure.

- **“The frowns of the executive magistracy of the State.”** Governors use their office to oppose the measure rather than to administer it.

- **“The embarrassments created by legislative devices.”** State legislatures pass resolutions and laws that withdraw state cooperation and make federal enforcement difficult.


Madison judged that this would be a serious obstacle in any state, a severe one in a large state, and, where several neighboring states acted together, an obstruction Washington “would hardly be willing to encounter.”


## Why This Works Today, and Why It Is Lawful


Madison’s plan is more powerful now than when he wrote it, for a simple reason: Washington cannot run its own programs. Most federal programs depend on states to administer them, staff them, and enforce them. A partnership that depends on the cooperation of one partner fails when that partner withdraws.


Importantly, this withdrawal is not lawless. The Supreme Court has held repeatedly that the federal government may not commandeer state governments. In *New York v. United States* (1992), the Court ruled that Congress cannot order state legislatures to enact federal programs. In *Printz v. United States* (1997), it ruled that federal law cannot conscript state and local officers to enforce federal law. In *Murphy v. NCAA* (2018), it confirmed that Congress cannot simply forbid states from changing their own laws. The principle reaches back to *Prigg v. Pennsylvania* (1842), which held that states could not be forced to help enforce the federal Fugitive Slave Act.


So a state that says, “Our officers, our jails, our databases, and our money will not be used to carry out this federal measure,” is acting within the Constitution as the Court itself reads it.


I want to be honest about the limit. The anti-commandeering doctrine lets a state refuse to help. It does not let a state physically stop federal agents from enforcing a federal law that is valid. The Supremacy Clause makes constitutional federal law binding, and a state that tries to arrest federal officers for doing their jobs puts itself in the wrong. Madison’s plan works by withdrawal, not by obstruction at gunpoint. That is its strength. It asks nothing of the states except that they stop lending their hands.


## The Test That Separates Resistance From Rebellion


This doctrine has been used for good and for evil, and we should face that squarely.


Before the Civil War, northern states passed personal liberty laws refusing to let their officials help capture people who had escaped slavery. That was Madison’s plan used to protect a neighbor from being returned to bondage. In the 1950s, southern states used the language of “interposition” to resist the desegregation of their schools. That was the same vocabulary used to keep a neighbor in a lesser station.


The difference between those two uses is not found in the procedure. Both involved states refusing federal demands. The difference is moral. In the first case, the state stood between a neighbor and a wrong done to him. In the second, the state stood between a neighbor and the rights he was owed.


That is why I insist the legal argument must rest on the moral one. State resistance is justified when it protects the people’s liberty, life, and property from a power that was never granted. It is not justified when it is used to deny one group of people the protections owed to all. The neighbor-love test is not decoration on top of the Constitution. It is the reason the Constitution deserves to be defended at all.


## The Jury: The People’s Own Check


Madison’s first two steps belong to the people themselves, and one of the most powerful tools the people have is one he did not list in Federalist 46 but which the founding generation understood well: the jury.


A federal law can only be enforced against a citizen if a jury of his neighbors convicts him. If twelve citizens conclude that a law is unjust, or is being applied unjustly, they may acquit. That is jury nullification, and I regard it as a cornerstone principle of a free society. It is the point at which the abstract question “Is this law legitimate?” is answered by real neighbors looking at a real man. A government that cannot persuade a jury of the justice of its law has lost the consent on which its authority depends.


I would add a word of caution about individual refusal. A citizen who disobeys a law he believes unjust should do it openly, peacefully, and ready to bear the consequences, trusting his neighbors on the jury to judge. That is the path of conscience. It is different from quietly evading a law while others bear the cost.


## The Final Safeguard


Madison anticipated the objection that Washington might simply build an army and crush any resistance. He answered it directly. Even if a standing army were raised to the largest size a country could sustain, which he estimated at about one percent of the population, it would face the state governments and the armed citizenry they lead. He concluded that “the State governments, with the people on their side, would be able to repel the danger.”


I include this because it is part of Madison’s argument, and because the right of the people to keep and bear arms is part of what makes the rest of the plan credible. But I want the proportion right. Madison’s point here was deterrence. An armed people with organized state governments is a reason why a tyrant never tries; it is not a call to violence. Every step that comes before it is peaceful, lawful, and political. A nation that uses those steps well never needs the last one. My aim is to make sure we use them well.


## What I Would Do as President


A president is the one federal official best placed to honor Madison’s plan instead of fighting it. Here is what I would commit to:


- **Refuse to enforce what the Constitution does not authorize.** The president swears to preserve, protect, and defend the Constitution, not every statute Congress passes. Thomas Jefferson pardoned those convicted under the Sedition Act because he judged it unconstitutional. I would use the same judgment, and explain my reasons publicly.

- **Stop fighting states that lawfully withdraw cooperation.** I would direct the Justice Department not to sue states for exercising their anti-commandeering rights, and not to punish them by cutting unrelated funding.

- **Return programs to the states.** Wherever a federal program operates in the domain Madison reserved to the states, I would work with Congress to end the program and return the funds and responsibility to the people closer to the need.

- **Defend the jury.** I would support requiring that juries be told the truth about their role, including their power to acquit when conscience demands it.

- **Keep the moral test in front of everything.** For each of these, the standard is the same: does this act serve the neighbor, with his consent, or rule over him without it?


## The Responsibility Is Ours


Madison closed his argument in Federalist 46 by placing the final responsibility where it belongs: “The ultimate authority, wherever the derivative may be found, resides in the people alone.”


It is easy to blame politicians, judges, and bureaucrats for the size of the government we now live under. They deserve their share of blame. But Madison would also ask us a harder question: why did we keep electing them, keep cooperating with them, and keep waiting for them to give our liberty back?


No president can restore the Constitution alone, and I do not pretend I could. What a president can do is stop standing in the way of the people and the states doing what Madison expected them to do. That is a position I hold firmly, and I hold it open to anyone who can show me a better way to keep government the servant of the people rather than their master.
