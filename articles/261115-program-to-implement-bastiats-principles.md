# 261115 - Program to Implement Bastiat's Principles

# Not Yours to Give


### Bastiat, Transfer Payments, and the Line Between Charity and the Common Defense

Thomas Lee Abshier, ND · 14 November 2026

## My Position

I stand with Frédéric Bastiat: government has no right to take one person's property and give it to another. Money is simply property in its most exchangeable form, and no one has a right to his neighbor's money, whether he takes it with a gun or with a ballot. Forced transfer is plunder, and it robs both men. It robs the one whose earnings are taken, and it robs the one who would have given, because a gift taken by force can never be an act of virtue. *"Every man according as he purposeth in his heart, so let him give; not grudgingly, or of necessity: for God loveth a cheerful giver."* **(2 Corinthians 9:7)**

There is one caveat, and it is God's own. Government has a God-given authority to tax for the common defense and to provide for the general welfare. *"For this cause pay ye tribute also: for they are God's ministers, attending continually upon this very thing."* **(Romans 13:6)** So the real question is not whether government may tax. It is this: **where is the dividing line between the private domain of responsibility (charity, family, church, and neighbor) and the God-given right of government to tax and provide for the general welfare?**

This paper answers that question. It also answers the practical one: is this realistic? My answer is yes, but not as a single act of repeal. It is realistic as a revolution of the heart carried out by law over a generation. The forced-charity cases are the clearest, and they should go first. Public services such as schools, police, fire, and roads are the hardest, and they need a test that can be applied honestly, even when it costs my own state of Montana.

The test I apply to every policy has three questions. *Is it righteous?* If so, *is it loving?* And *would I want it done to me?* A law that takes from one man to give to another fails the first question; however, it scores on the second.

## The Principle: Not Yours to Give

Bastiat's argument is short. Each person has a God-given right to his life, his liberty, and the fruit of his labor. Each person may defend those rights, and a people may join their individual rights of defense into a common force. That common force is law. A group, though, can have no rightful power its members lack. If I may not take my neighbor's wages to feed a third man, I cannot authorize a majority, a legislature, or a government to do it for me. A man cannot lend what he does not own.

By this reasoning, every transfer payment (money taken from one citizen and paid to another because of the second citizen's need, status, or political influence) is organized plunder. That holds however good the purpose. The commandment is four words long and admits no exception for majorities: *"Thou shalt not steal."* **(Exodus 20:15)** The Psalmist saw that law itself can be the instrument of the crime: *"Shall the throne of iniquity have fellowship with thee, which frameth mischief by a law?"* **(Psalm 94:20)**

The deeper injury is spiritual. When the state takes a man's money for the poor, it does two things at once. It robs him of the property and the virtue. He cannot give what has already been taken, and so he cannot feel or perform the love that charity is meant to be. The recipient is injured too, because he receives a claim rather than a gift and owes no gratitude to anyone. Meanwhile, the relationship between neighbors, which charity was meant to build, is replaced by a relationship between a citizen and a bureaucracy.

I believe God lives inside every person's experience. He feels the taking in the one who is plundered, and He is denied the joy of the giving in the one who might have given freely. That is why I hold this position first on moral grounds and only second on economic ones. Love must be free to be love. *"Love worketh no ill to his neighbour: therefore love is the fulfilling of the law."* **(Romans 13:10)**

## The Caveat: God's Grant of the Taxing Power

Scripture does not make government a thief by nature. It makes government a servant with a sword, appointed to punish evil and protect the good. *"For he is the minister of God to thee for good. **But if thou do that which is evil, be afraid; for he beareth not the sword in vain."* **(Romans 13:4)** It also says that this servant is owed his support: *"Render therefore to all their dues: tribute to whom tribute is due."* **(Romans 13:7)** When Jesus was asked about the tax, He did not call it theft: *"Render therefore unto Caesar the things which are Caesar's; and unto God the things that are God's."* **(Matthew 22:21)**

The tax Scripture authorizes has a purpose, though, and the purpose defines the limit. Tribute is owed to the magistrate *because* he attends to the work of justice: restraining evildoers, defending the innocent, and keeping the peace. A tax that pays for that work is each citizen paying his share for a protection he himself receives. A tax that takes from one to enrich another is something else, even when it passes through the same treasury.

### The Constitution says the same thing in different words

Article I, Section 8 gives Congress power to lay and collect taxes "to pay the Debts and provide for the common Defense and general Welfare of the United States." From the beginning, the meaning of "general Welfare" has been disputed.


 	- **Madison's reading.** James Madison held that the clause adds no new power. In his view, Congress may tax and spend only to carry out the powers listed after it. In 1794, when Congress debated relief for refugees from Santo Domingo, Madison said he could not find the article of the Constitution that gave Congress the right to spend its constituents' money on objects of benevolence. Another report of the same debate quotes him more bluntly: *charity is no part of the legislative duty of the government.*

 	- **Hamilton's reading.** Alexander Hamilton, and later Justice Joseph Story, held that the clause is a separate power to tax and spend for any purpose that serves the general welfare. Even on their reading, the welfare must be *general*, not local or particular.

 	- **The Court's settlement.** In *United States v. Butler* (1936), the Supreme Court adopted Hamilton's view. A year later, in *Helvering v. Davis* (1937), it upheld Social Security and left Congress to decide what counts as general welfare, unless Congress's choice is clearly arbitrary. Writing for the Court, Justice Cardozo still conceded that a line must be drawn between particular welfare and general welfare. In practice, though, no act of Congress has ever been struck down for failing to serve the general welfare.


So the line I am asking for is not new. Madison drew it at the enumerated powers. Hamilton drew it at the word *general*. The Court said a line exists, then declined to enforce it, leaving it to the conscience of Congress and the people. The rest of this paper tries to draw that line on moral ground that both Madison and Hamilton would recognize.

## The Dividing Line

I propose that a tax is righteous when the thing it pays for passes all four of these tests, and that anything failing any one of them belongs to the private domain of family, church, charity, and free exchange.


 	- **Protection.** Does it protect life, liberty, or property from violence, fraud, invasion, or a calamity that threatens everyone? Romans 13 assigns this work to the magistrate.

 	- **Generality.** Is the benefit open to every citizen on the same terms? Or is it paid to particular persons or firms because of their need, status, or political influence? A road serves everyone who travels it. A check serves the one whose name is on it.

 	- **Proximity.** Is it done by the government closest to the people that can do it? Federal power should cover only what states cannot do, and states only what counties and towns cannot do. This is Madison's distinction between a government "confined to specified objects" and the more general powers of the states.

 	- **No voluntary substitute.** Could free giving, mutual aid, or a user fee do the job just as well, without weakening the common protection? If so, compulsion is not needed, and what is not needed is not righteous.


The fourth test is where moral maturity enters. Defense cannot be left to voluntary contribution, because the man who refuses to pay is defended anyway, and a nation cannot defend only its donors. Feeding a hungry neighbor *can* be left to love, and Scripture commands it that way.

This is not the easy line. It keeps some taxes that strict readers of Bastiat would abolish, and it ends some programs that much of the country now depends on. But it is a line, and it can be stated, defended, and applied to everyone, including me.

## Is It Realistic?

It is realistic as a direction pursued for a generation. It is not realistic as a single act of repeal. The scale of the problem shows why.

### The size of the gap

In fiscal year 2025, the federal government spent $7.0 trillion. Of that, $4.2 trillion was mandatory spending, almost all of it benefit programs: about $1.58 trillion for Social Security, $1.82 trillion for the major health programs (Medicare and Medicaid), and nearly $400 billion for income-security programs such as food aid, Supplemental Security Income, and refundable tax credits. As early as 2011, the Congressional Research Service counted more than 80 federal means-tested programs, which, together with the states' required matching funds, cost about $1 trillion a year.

By comparison, all American charitable giving in 2025 from every source (individuals, bequests, foundations, and corporations) totaled about $617 billion. Individuals gave about $394 billion, and churches and other religious organizations received about $152 billion. Mandatory federal benefits alone are nearly seven times everything Americans now give to charity.

### What a tithe would do

The gap doesn't measure what Americans *can* give. It measures what they have been trained not to give. American personal income in 2025 was about $26 trillion. A tithe of that would be roughly $2.6 trillion a year, more than four times current giving and well beyond the cost of every means-tested program combined. Even a tithe on earned income alone, leaving aside the transfer income now counted in that total, would cover the poor many times over. The resources are there. What is missing is the heart, and the habit.

Two cautions keep this honest. First, much of the mandatory budget is not charity to the poor. Social Security and Medicare are paid mostly to the middle class, and the right replacement for them is a man's own savings and his family, not charity. Second, money given freely does not flow in the same pattern as money taken by force. Some needs that the state meets today would be met better by the church, some worse, and some not at all at first. A transition has to be built for that, which is the subject of the program below.

### We have done it before

Americans once carried most of this burden themselves. Before the New Deal, fraternal and mutual-aid societies were the largest voluntary associations in the country after the churches. A conservative estimate holds that one in three adult men belonged to one around 1910. These lodges provided sickness and burial benefits, life insurance, hospitals, and homes for the aged. Between 1890 and 1922, they founded 71 orphanages, almost all without government money. Their decline in the late 1930s was not because they failed. It was largely because the new federal programs took over their reason for being.

So my answer to "Is it realistic?" is that the law can follow the heart only so far. A nation that will not tithe cannot abolish forced charity without real suffering. A nation in revival can, and will, find the freedom and joy compulsion has taken from it. *"Give, and it shall be given unto you; good measure, pressed down, and shaken together, and running over."* **(Luke 6:38)**

## The Hard Case: Rural and Low-Density Services

The hardest question is not welfare. It is the ordinary public service in a place with few people and long distances. A school bus route in eastern Montana, a volunteer fire truck covering hundreds of square miles, or a county road that carries a dozen ranch families costs far more per person than the same service in a city. People far away now pay much of that cost.

I must begin with my own state. Montana's state and local governments drew 35.5 percent of their revenue from federal transfers in fiscal year 2023, ten points above the national average, and 44 percent of those transfers were for public welfare. Montanans received about $5,011 more per person in federal spending than they paid in federal taxes in 2022. By one count, Montana gets about $1.40 back for every dollar it sends. Only 13 states send Washington more than they receive. Those of us who object to plunder must be willing to apply the test to ourselves first. *"Thou hypocrite, first cast out the beam out of thine own eye."* **(Matthew 7:5)**

Not all of that money is a transfer, though, and the four tests sort it.


 	- **Defense located in rural places is not a transfer.** Missile fields and air bases sit on the plains because that is where the land and the mission are. That spending is the common defense, wherever it happens to be spent.

 	- **The national road network is a common good, not a subsidy from the city to the country.** The Constitution names post roads among the enumerated powers. The city eats, heats, and ships across rural highways. A network is valuable as a whole, and the lightly traveled stretch is part of what makes the heavy stretch worth anything. Highways pass the generality test, and those who use them should pay as much as possible through fuel taxes and tolls.

 	- **Police and fire belong to the county and town.** They pass the protection test, and they are righteous taxes when levied by the people who receive them. Rural America already knows the voluntary model: the volunteer fire department, the sheriff's posse, and mutual-aid compacts between neighboring counties. The higher cost per person of protection in open country is part of the price of choosing open country. Many of us gladly pay it for the freedom and space it gives.

 	- **Schools are formation, not protection, and they belong to the local board.** State equalization formulas that move money from rich districts to poor ones are transfers. During the transition, I would let states continue them by the choice of their own voters, but I would end all federal school money. Rural communities would need to decide, district by district, how to educate their children: consolidated schools, church and cooperative schools, home education, and distance learning are all available today in ways they were not a generation ago.

 	- **Farm subsidies fail every test.** They pay particular producers because of their political influence. Bastiat would have recognized them at once, and so should rural voters, however uncomfortable that is for us.


The principle for the hard case, then, is that **a common good shared by all may be supported by all, but a local good should be supported by the locality that enjoys it.** Where a rural community truly cannot sustain a service, the answer is neighborliness across county lines, freely given, and not a permanent federal claim on the earnings of strangers. Rural America will need a long runway to make that change, and the program below gives it one.

## A Program of Execution

The administrative challenge is enormous. Tens of millions of Americans have arranged their lives around government checks, and some have made collecting benefits their life's work. No one in that position has a right to his neighbor's property. But a righteous transition does not punish people for having trusted the promises their government made them. It changes the rules going forward, honors debts already incurred, and builds the voluntary alternative before it removes the compulsory one. The program runs in six phases over roughly a generation.


 	- **End plunder of the many for the few (years 1–2).** Begin with the clearest cases, where the recipients are not poor at all: corporate subsidies, bailouts, farm price supports, protective tariffs, and earmarks for favored industries. No one's survival depends on these, and ending them first shows the principle is applied to the powerful before the weak.

 	- **Build the voluntary alternative (years 1–5).** Allow a dollar-for-dollar federal tax credit, up to a cap, for gifts to churches and charities that relieve the poor. That lets each citizen direct his own share of poor relief while the system is rebuilt. Remove the legal barriers that keep mutual-aid societies and health-sharing ministries from serving their members. Call the churches to restore the deacon's office of care and to teach the tithe. *"Bring ye all the tithes into the storehouse, that there may be meat in mine house, and prove me now herewith, saith the LORD of hosts."* **(Malachi 3:10)**

 	- **Close the door behind the able-bodied (years 2–10).** Stop new enrollment of able-bodied, working-age adults without dependents in means-tested programs. Those already enrolled step down over several years, with work and training in place of the check. Scripture is plain about the duty of the able: *"Let him that stole steal no more: but rather let him labour, working with his hands the thing which is good, that he may have to give to him that needeth."* **(Ephesians 4:28)**

 	- **Protect the widow, the orphan, and the disabled throughout.** The truly helpless are never cut off during the transition. Their care moves, step by step, along the order Scripture sets: first family, then the church, then the local community, and only as a last resort, and for a limited time, the state. *"But if any provide not for his own, and specially for those of his own house, he hath denied the faith, and is worse than an infidel."* **(1 Timothy 5:8)** *"Pure religion and undefiled before God and the Father is this, To visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction."* **(James 1:27)**

 	- **Return the remainder to the states, then let it shrink (years 3–15).** Convert the remaining federal aid programs into block grants that decline each year to zero. States may continue aid by the choice of their own voters, answering to those voters, and I would urge them to apply the same principle. The caseworkers and administrators of today's programs are among the people best equipped to staff the charities and mutual-aid societies that will take up the work.

 	- **Honor Social Security and Medicare as a debt, then wind them down (years 1–30).** Americans were compelled to pay into these programs on a promise, so the promise is owed to everyone who paid in. New workers would instead own and control their own retirement and health savings, and the old programs would close out over a working lifetime. I treat Social Security in detail in my separate essays on it.


Throughout, the president's chief tool is not force but voice. The law can stop plunder. Only the heart can start charity, and the heart is moved by persuasion, example, and the Spirit of God.

## The Strongest Objections, Answered

**"Private charity already failed once, in the Great Depression."** This is the strongest historical objection. National income fell by more than half between 1929 and 1932. By mid-1932, a quarter of New York's private charities had closed for lack of money. In 116 urban areas, the private share of relief rose from about 25 percent in 1929 to a peak of 29 percent in 1931, and then fell to well under 1 percent by 1939 as government relief took over. My answer is that local governments were going bankrupt at the same time, so the Depression proves that every institution, public or private, breaks in a collapse of that size. It does not prove that charity cannot carry ordinary need. Many fraternal societies kept paying, and their per-person benefits were higher in 1935 than in 1929. A true national calamity of that kind is the one edge where I would allow temporary emergency relief under the common defense of life. It must be general, temporary, and openly declared, and it must not become permanent.

**"We voted for it, so it is not theft."** A majority's consent is not the victim's consent. Fifty-one men cannot vote away the property of forty-nine, any more than one man can vote away the property of his neighbor. Bastiat's point stands: a group has no power its members lack.

**"Your taxes for police and defense are forced too."** They are, and the difference is what the money buys. A protective tax pays each citizen's share of the protection he receives, and no one can be excluded from it. A transfer takes from one man to give to another. The first (taxation) is the price of living under law. The second (protection) is the thing law exists to prevent.

**"Scripture commands the king to care for the poor."** Many Christians, Catholic and Protestant alike, hold that the state is God's servant for the relief of the poor, and they point to passages such as Psalm 72: *"He shall judge the poor of the people, he shall save the children of the needy, and shall break in pieces the oppressor."* **(Psalm 72:4)** I take this seriously, because it is Scripture. But look at what the king does there. He *judges*, he *saves* from oppression, and he *breaks the oppressor*. That is justice for the poor: protecting them from fraud, violence, and the rigged law that robs them. It is not a treasury that redistributes. The Mosaic tithe for the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow **(Deuteronomy 14:28-29)** was collected and given locally, and it was answerable to God, not enforced by a royal tax collector.

**"Children and the disabled will suffer while hearts catch up."** They must not, and in this program they do not. That is why phase four protects them throughout, and why the program spans a generation rather than a budget cycle. A transition that left a single orphan hungry for the sake of a principle would fail my own test: *would I want it done to me?*

**"It is politically impossible."** Today, perhaps. Every great reform was impossible until the public conscience changed. Ending forced charity will follow a renewal of real charity, not come before it. My task, as I see it, is to make the moral case in public until the people are ready to choose it.

## Why the Concern Remains Urgent

The present system is plundering people who cannot object, because they have not yet been born. The federal government ran a $1.8 trillion deficit in fiscal year 2025, and interest on the debt passed $1 trillion, making it the second-largest item in the budget after Social Security. The Congressional Budget Office projects federal debt held by the public rising from 100 percent of the economy in 2025 to 118 percent by 2035, above the record set at the end of World War II. It also projects that the Social Security retirement trust fund will run dry in 2032. (Note: see essay on Social Security - There is no money in the SSTF; it has been spent. The date it runs dry is a legal technicality that triggers a 17% reduction in SS benefits.)  Every borrowed dollar spent on transfers today is taken, without consent, from a child who will have to repay it. *"The rich ruleth over the poor, and the borrower is servant to the lender."* **(Proverbs 22:7)**

Many Americans sense that something is unnatural about a nation in which so many live on money taken from their neighbors, and in which the surest road to wealth is to win a favor from the legislature. That sense is correct. Where law can hand out wealth, organized interests will spend heavily to steer it, and the money motive explains much of why the system grows no matter who is in office. The link between doctrine and behavior is also plain. A people taught that rights come from the state will look to the state for every good thing, and will feel no shame in using it to take. A people taught that rights come from God, and that love must be freely given, will look to their own hands and hearts.

That is why I believe this question belongs at the center of public life. It is not an accounting problem. It is a question of whether we will be a people who steal from one another by law, or a people who give to one another in love.
