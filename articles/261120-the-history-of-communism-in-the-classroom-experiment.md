# 261120 - The History of Communism in the Classroom Experiment

# Teaching the Truth About Communism:


### Florida's Experiment and the Lesson for the Nation

**By Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | 20 November 2026**


---


## The Occasion

This fall, Florida began teaching the history of communism as required instruction in its public middle and high schools. The law was signed in April 2024, the State Board of Education adopted the standards in November 2025, and the 2026–2027 school year is the first in which teachers must deliver them. Middle school students learn how economic systems work, study the advantages of free markets, and examine why the Jamestown and Plymouth colonies abandoned common ownership for private property. High school students go further: Adam Smith and Karl Marx, the Russian Revolution and the Red Terror, and firsthand accounts of life under communist regimes in the Western Hemisphere, Europe, and Asia.

The Epoch Times recently reported on how the first semester has gone. At Polk State College in Lakeland, Orlando Gutiérrez-Boronat, who fled Cuba as a child and later founded a radio station that broadcasts uncensored news back to the island, told a room of college and high school students: "Totalitarian states don't lead individuals; they lead masses." Afterward, a high school junior who wants to be a police officer said he had known little about Cuba before that day, but he knew now that he did not like communism and did not believe it could produce anything good.

I believe it is right, and necessary, to teach every American child the plain history of communism: what it promised, what it did, and why it did what it did. I also believe the effort will succeed or fail on whether it is taught as truth rather than as a slogan. Below I give my reasons, the strongest objection to the program, my answer to it, and one place where I think Florida's standards need care.

## Why This Is a Moral Question

Everything I write on this site rests on three questions I ask of any public act:


 	- **Is it righteous?** Does it conform to what God has established as right?

 	- **If so, is it loving?** Does it serve the real good of my neighbor?

 	- **Would I want it done to me?** Measured not by my momentary appetite, but by the self committed to righteousness and looking at life from the soul's eternal perspective.


Teaching the history of communism passes all three.

**It is righteous because it is true.** The record of communist regimes in the twentieth century is not a matter of opinion. The Soviet gulag, the Holodomor famine in Ukraine, Mao's Great Leap Forward and Cultural Revolution, and the killing fields of the Khmer Rouge are documented history. Common estimates of the dead run near 100 million. Teaching children these facts is telling them the truth about the world they will inherit. *"And ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free"* **(John 8:32)**.

**It is loving because ignorance of this history is dangerous to the young.** A child who has never heard what happened when a government promised to provide everything in exchange for control over everything is defenseless when the same promise is made to him again in new words. Protecting him from that deception is an act of care.

**And I would want it done to me.** If I had been raised in a country that had once fallen to a lie, I would want my teachers to have told me how it happened. Renee Zayas, a Cuban-born teacher and compliance director in Miami-Dade, described being a little girl watching a soldier confiscate her cherished bird charm during an inventory check, and eating dinners of coffee, milk, and toast after the regime seized her grandparents' dairy farm. She complained that families like hers rarely talk about these things, so their children take freedom for granted. A school that invites people like her to speak is doing what I would want done for my own grandchildren.

## The Question Under the Question

The surveys that alarm so many people are worth examining honestly. In a March 2025 Cato Institute/YouGov survey, 62 percent of Americans aged 18 to 29 said they held a favorable view of socialism, and 34 percent said the same of communism. A Cato survey this year put the communism figure among the young at 38 percent, and Gallup found this September that 57 percent of Americans aged 18 to 34 view socialism positively.

Those numbers need a caveat, and I will give it: the Cato poll did not define "socialism," so each respondent supplied his own meaning. Many young people who say "socialism" mean Scandinavian health care or cheaper rent, not the Politburo. But the caveat does not dissolve the concern. More than a third of young adults express a favorable view of the word "communism," a word that should be as morally settled as "slavery," telling us that something has failed in how we pass down history.

So what are these young people reaching for? I think the honest answer is that they are reaching for compassion. They see housing they cannot afford, debt they cannot escape, and medical bills that frighten their parents. Those grievances are real, and anyone who dismisses them will not be heard. Socialism comes to them as the promise that someone will finally take care of these things.

The answer is not to mock the impulse but to show where it leads when it is detached from righteousness. Michelle Brown of Moms for Liberty in Bay County put the problem well when she said she worries about socialism being masked as compassion. The compassion is genuine; the mask is the claim that it can be achieved by taking from one neighbor by force to give to another. That is not compassion. It is coveting, enforced by the state. *"Thou shalt not steal"* **(Exodus 20:15)**.

## The Strongest Christian Case for Socialism, and Why It Fails

Some young Christians are drawn to socialism by Scripture itself, and they deserve a serious answer. The early church in Jerusalem lived in common: *"And all that believed were together, and had all things common; And sold their possessions and goods, and parted them to all men, as every man had need"* **(Acts 2:44–45)**.

That is a beautiful picture, and it is the strongest Christian argument for socialism. But read three chapters further. When Ananias and Sapphira lied about the price of their land, Peter did not rebuke them for keeping property. He said the opposite: *"Whiles it remained, was it not thine own? and after it was sold, was it not in thine own power?"* **(Acts 5:4)**. Their sin was the lie, not the ownership. The sharing in Acts was voluntary, local, and born of love among believers. Nothing in it resembles a state compelling the sharing under threat of prison.

That distinction is the whole lesson. Voluntary generosity is Godly. Compelled redistribution, administered by men who claim the right to decide what every person needs, is the road that ended in the gulag. Emma Hakes, a homeschooled sophomore from Bradenton who won a Victims of Communism Florida scholarship contest with a speech on Mao's Cultural Revolution, saw this clearly: communism does not merely put government in control; it puts government in God's place. That is exactly why it must lie, and why it must kill those who will not believe the lie.

The middle school lesson on Plymouth makes the same point in miniature. Governor William Bradford recorded that the colony's early common-store system bred idleness and resentment, and that when each family was assigned its own plot in 1623, the harvest improved. Scripture had already said it: *"For even when we were with you, this we commanded you, that if any would not work, neither should he eat"* **(2 Thessalonians 3:10)**.

## The Strongest Objection, and My Answer

PEN America, a free-expression organization, has called Florida's standards a "dangerous recasting of McCarthyism as patriotism." Its Florida director argued that the standards impose a single, rigid set of beliefs, and that a classroom should be a place where ideas meet rather than a place where ideology reigns. Some educators have added that the standards teach students what to think rather than how to think.

I take this objection seriously because the principle behind it is a good one. Education should teach young people to reason, and a classroom that only recites conclusions produces students who can be turned by the next confident voice.

But the objection proves too much. We do not ask teachers to present "diverse perspectives" on whether the Holocaust was evil, or whether chattel slavery was wrong. Some questions of history are morally settled, and a teacher who refuses to say so is not being neutral; he is failing his students. The deaths of tens of millions under communist governments belong in that category.

Here is the standard I would hold the teaching to: **students should meet the strongest case for socialism, from Marx himself and from its modern defenders, and then see the record.** Florida's high school standards include Marx alongside Adam Smith. That is the right design. A student who has read Marx's promise and then read the testimony of those who lived under its fulfillment has been taught how to think, not merely what to think. Conviction that survives the strongest opposing argument can meet the test of real-world debate and decisions.

## Where Florida Must Be Careful

There is one place where Florida's standards need care, though not for the reason the critics give.

Florida's standards instruct teachers to present the use of "McCarthyism" as an insult and shorthand for all anti-communism, and to present terms like "red-baiter" and "Red Scare" as slander against anti-communists. There is a real truth behind this. For decades, many historians taught that Soviet espionage in America was minor and that the accusations of that era were mostly paranoia. The Venona decrypts, declassified in 1995, proved otherwise: Soviet intelligence had sources in the Manhattan Project, the State Department, and the Treasury. The anti-communists of the 1940s were right about the threat, and the word "McCarthyism" has too often been used to dismiss any concern about communist influence at all.

What Senator Joseph McCarthy got right can be named. Mary Jane Keeney, a United Nations employee among his cases, lost her UN post by the end of 1950; the Venona cables and her own diaries later confirmed that she and her husband had worked for Soviet intelligence. Solomon Adler, Lauchlin Currie, and T. A. Bisson, all among the people McCarthy pursued, were later shown to have been Soviet assets, though the evidence to prove it was not public in his lifetime. The Amerasia affair, which he pressed as proof of a security breach at the State Department, was real: in 1945 the FBI found hundreds of classified government documents in the offices of Amerasia, a pro-Communist magazine whose editor, Philip Jaffe, was arrested for conspiracy to commit espionage, and State Department China expert John Stewart Service had been recorded meeting with Jaffe. Even Annie Lee Moss, the Pentagon code-room clerk whom Edward R. Murrow's broadcast turned into the symbol of McCarthy's cruelty, had been identified by an FBI informant who served as membership director of the Communist Party's Washington branch; the informant testified that Moss's name and address were on the party rolls from 1943 to 1945. And the heart of his charge, that the State Department had kept on employees its own security screeners had flagged, rested on the department's own records.

What he got wrong can also be named. The famous Wheeling number is the clearest case. The only newspaper account of the February 1950 speech reports him saying he held a list of 205 State Department employees known to be Communist Party members. That figure came from a 1946 letter by Secretary of State James Byrnes, who reported that of about 3,000 employees screened, 285 had drawn adverse security recommendations and 79 had been removed, leaving roughly 205 flagged cases unresolved. That was a count of security cases, not of party members, and calling it the latter was an error. Within days, in his letter to President Truman and in the version of the speech he entered into the Congressional Record, he cited 57; on the Senate floor, he cited 81 cases. His opponents have used these shifting figures ever since to dismiss everything he said. But the figures measured different things (unresolved security flags, alleged party members, and case files), and none of them was invented from nothing. Three years later, McCarthy pointed out in a hearing that 54 State Department employees under disloyalty charges had been allowed to resign and 3 more had been found disloyal: 57 in all. The department's own official dismissed this as a numbers game, but it was a real count from the department's own files. His second error was overreach. He called Owen Lattimore the top Soviet espionage agent in the United States and said he would stand or fall on that case; no evidence has shown Lattimore to have been a spy. A later Senate inquiry did find that Lattimore had consistently and consciously done what the Soviet Union wanted at the Institute of Pacific Relations, which is a serious finding, but it is not espionage. His third error was carelessness of method. His approach was so scattershot that he missed a real spy who sat in front of him: Nathan Sussman, a cooperative witness before McCarthy's committee, is now known to have been a member of the Rosenberg ring.

Put in proportion, McCarthy was right about the substance and careless about the particulars. The threat he named was real, a number of the people he named were what he said they were, and the government he accused had in fact been slow to act on its own security findings. His errors were in precision and restraint: inflated labels, shifting numbers, and charges he could not prove. He did not invent a threat that did not exist. *"Thou shalt not bear false witness against thy neighbour"* **(Exodus 20:16)**. That commandment cuts both ways. It condemns careless accusation of the innocent, and it equally condemns seventy years of teaching students that McCarthy imagined the whole thing.

That is why I would urge Florida's teachers to teach the cases themselves, name by name, and not trade one slogan for another. Replacing "McCarthy was a bully who ruined innocent lives" with "McCarthyism is a slander" only changes which half of the truth students are given. A curriculum that exists to teach children how a lie captures a nation must not itself shade the truth, even in the direction of its own cause. If students learn that the anti-communists were right about the threat, and that the cause was hurt wherever they were careless with the facts, they will have learned something they can use for the rest of their lives: a righteous end is best served by righteous means, and accuracy is the anti-communist's strongest weapon.

## Why the Concern Remains Plausible

Some will say the alarm over young people and socialism is overblown, that they are using the words loosely and will grow out of them. I hope so. But I don't think we can wave away the concern for several reasons.

First, the trend is consistent across different pollsters and is rising, not falling. Second, this month the Democratic Socialists of America saw candidates it backed win across the country, including the first DSA-backed seat in the Florida Legislature. Third, Florida's attorney general has subpoenaed DSA to examine whether it accepted money or other support from Cuba or China in connection with its delegations to those countries. That is an investigation, not a finding, and DSA has said its fundraising is lawful; I hold it as a question, not an answer. Fourth, the Chinese government has openly said it wants to shape how young Americans see China, with Xi Jinping's 2023 invitation for 50,000 young Americans to study there over five years.

None of these facts proves a conspiracy. Together, they show that people and governments have a real interest in how the next generation of Americans thinks about communism, and that the next generation is, by its own report, more open to it than any generation before. That is reason enough to teach the history.

## What I Would Do as President

Education is local. School boards govern what children are taught, not Washington, and I would not seek a federal mandate or federal money for this or any curriculum.

A president can lead with his voice. Education Secretary Linda McMahon did exactly this on September 30, when she issued a letter urging schools nationwide to strengthen instruction on the history and human cost of communism, pointing to the approaches of Florida, Arizona, Arkansas, Tennessee, and Texas without mandating any curriculum. That is the right model. As president, I would continue it, and I would urge every school board in the country to adopt instruction in the history of communism, built on primary sources and firsthand testimony.

I would also commend the private efforts that make this work real. Victims of Communism Florida offers privately funded scholarship prizes to students who complete a workshop and present what they have learned. Teachers in Miami-Dade are inviting grandparents and colleagues who escaped Cuba and Venezuela to speak in their classrooms. These are citizens teaching citizens, and they need no federal program.

Finally, I would ask parents to do what Renee Zayas asked: talk about it at home. A school can teach a lesson. Only a family can hand down a memory.

## Conclusion

Communism's most dangerous weapon was never the tank or the prison camp. It was the promise, made sincerely to people with real needs, that a government could replace the free choices of free men with its own wisdom and love. Every generation must be taught why that promise is a lie, because every generation will hear it again.

Florida has begun to teach it. I believe the nation should follow, by local choice and with an unflinching commitment to the whole truth, including the uncomfortable parts of our own history. A free people that forgets what slavery under a total state looks like will not recognize it when it comes dressed as kindness.

## Sources and Their Limits

This essay leans mainly on the mainstream record: state legislative documents, the Florida governor's office, wire-service and public-radio reporting, PEN America's own statement, and the published polling of Cato and Gallup. The prompting article came from The Epoch Times, which writes from a perspective sympathetic to the curriculum; I have treated its firsthand interviews as testimony, not as proof of how the program is going statewide. No independent evaluation yet exists of how Florida's teachers are actually delivering these standards, or of what students are retaining, so claims on either side about the program's effect are early. The polling on socialism and communism measures reactions to undefined words, and should be read as a signal of attitude, not a measure of belief in any specific system. On the DSA investigation, only allegations and denials are public; the documents subpoenaed have not been released.

On McCarthy, the historians who did most of the work documenting Soviet espionage, John Earl Haynes and Harvey Klehr, are harsher on McCarthy than M. Stanton Evans, whose *Blacklisted by History* makes the strongest case for him from FBI files and Venona. Some of the specific identifications in that section (Adler, Currie, Bisson, and the Army's later acknowledgment in the Moss case) rest substantially on Evans's work. Venona decrypted only a small part of Soviet cable traffic, so the absence of a name from it is not proof of innocence, and its presence is not always proof of identity. Where curation in the mainstream record could hide evidence, it would most likely be in still-classified security files and in the details of foreign funding of American political groups, which by their nature are not openly reported.
