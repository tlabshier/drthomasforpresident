# 261101 - Brazil as Warning for America

# Brazil as Warning:


### Foreign Money, Captured Courts, and the Consent of a People


*A response to Scott Lively’s “How Lula, Obama and USAID Turned Brazil Marxist”*


**Dr. Thomas — 01 November 2026**


---


## Why This Matters Now


One week ago, on October 25, 2026, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva won a fourth term as president of Brazil, defeating Senator Flávio Bolsonaro 51.79 percent to 48.21 percent, by about 4.43 million votes. In the last hours of that election day, Supreme Court Justice Cristiano Zanin — Lula’s personal defense lawyer before Lula appointed him to the Court — granted a petition from Lula’s campaign and extended voting by two hours at every polling place in the country. The electoral prosecutor had argued against an extension. In the 2022 runoff, the head of the election court had refused a similar request.


Into this moment, Scott Lively, a longtime missionary, has published an essay arguing that Brazil’s transformation over the past two decades was not a purely Brazilian event. His thesis is that the Obama administration designed a program to export a sexual and cultural revolution through American statecraft; that Lula’s governments adopted it, staffed the courts to carry it, and let foreign foundations fund it; and that USAID and allied funders built the infrastructure. He tells the story through his friend Julio Severo, a Brazilian Christian writer who fled his country and died in exile.


I stand with Lively’s central concern. The government of the United States, using American taxpayers’ money and the weight of American diplomacy, took sides in another nation’s moral argument and helped carry one side of it around that nation’s own elected legislature. That is wrong. It would be wrong if the cause were one I favored, and it would be wrong if a foreign government did it to us. And Brazil shows what happens to a free people when courts become the place where its moral law is made.


Lively’s essay overreaches or gets a fact wrong in places, and I will name them, because a true case does not need inflation. But the overreach is at the edges. The center holds.


---


## Julio Severo


Julio Severo was a Brazilian journalist and evangelical writer whose 1998 book *O Movimento Homossexual* was published by the Brazilian branch of Bethany House. He became one of the most outspoken opponents of the LGBT political movement in the Portuguese-speaking world. He left Brazil with his family and lived for years in Guatemala, where he died of a heart attack on May 4, 2021. Lively says he avoided medical care for fear the Brazilian government would find him.


The accounts of why he fled differ. Lively says the trigger was a court order authorizing the removal of Severo’s children because he refused, on conscience grounds, to vaccinate them. The Brazilian evangelical press at the time of his death reported that he left after the Federal Public Ministry pursued him over “homophobia” complaints stemming from his coverage of the 2006 São Paulo pride parade. These are not necessarily in conflict; a man under attack is often attacked on several fronts at once. Either way, the essential fact is the same: a man left his country because of what he believed and wrote.


The reaction to his death tells us something too. The founder of Brazil’s oldest gay activist group publicly called him the country’s greatest homophobe and wrote that he had “died late.” A movement that wishes its opponents dead has told you what it thinks about the freedom of those who disagree with it.


---


## What Is on the Record


Lively’s argument does not rest on speculation. Much of it rests on public documents.


**The diplomatic program.** On March 19, 2011, during Obama’s first state visit to Brazil, he and President Dilma Rousseff announced that they would work to establish a special rapporteur on LGBT issues at the Organization of American States. The White House described it as the first post of its kind in the international system. The same week, the United States and 85 nations issued a joint statement at the UN Human Rights Council on sexual orientation and gender identity. On December 6, 2011, Obama signed a Presidential Memorandum directing every federal agency working abroad to combat the criminalization of LGBT status, protect LGBT refugees, press LGBT issues in multilateral bodies, and ensure that U.S. foreign assistance advanced these aims. USAID followed with its LGBT Vision for Action in 2014; the State Department named its first Special Envoy for the Human Rights of LGBT Persons in 2015.


**The domestic program.** Lula’s government launched *Brasil Sem Homofobia* in 2004, built with the national LGBT advocacy associations.


**The courts.** Brazil’s National Congress would not enact the movement’s agenda. The courts did it instead. In 2011, the Supreme Federal Tribunal (STF) unanimously recognized same-sex stable unions as family entities. In 2013, the National Council of Justice, an administrative body, ordered every notary in the country to perform same-sex marriages; Congress never voted. In 2019, the STF classified “homophobia” as a crime under the anti-racism law. Judges and administrators took each step. None was taken by the people’s representatives.


**The money.** The U.S. government’s own ForeignAssistance.gov data, presented in a hearing of the Brazilian Chamber of Deputies’ Foreign Relations Committee on August 6, 2025, shows $44.8 million in USAID funds flowing to Brazilian organizations in 2023 and 2024. Brazilian reporting confirms from the same official portal that U.S. development funding to Brazil rose after Jair Bolsonaro’s 2018 victory. When the Trump administration froze foreign aid in early 2025, the Washington Blade reported that the global LGBT rights movement stood to lose more than $50 million, and that the Institute on Race, Equality and Human Rights — a Washington-based group active in Brazil — lost nearly 80 percent of its funding and suspended its programming. That is how dependent the apparatus had become on American public money.


**The judges.** Justice Alexandre de Moraes presided over the election court in 2022, led the STF’s “fake news” inquiry, and served as rapporteur in the case that sent Jair Bolsonaro to prison for 27 years. Justice Zanin was Lula’s personal lawyer through the Lava Jato cases and won the annulment of Lula’s convictions in 2021, restoring his eligibility to run. Lula then appointed him to the Court in 2023. Last Sunday, Zanin extended the vote at Lula’s campaign’s request.


Put these together, and the pattern Lively describes is not a fantasy. A program was announced in Washington and Brasília. Money flowed from Washington and allied foundations to organizations that shared the program’s aims. Courts delivered what the legislature refused. And the judges who now rule on the country’s most politically charged questions include the sitting president’s own former lawyer.

## Where Proportion Matters


I have written before that hyperbole is lying by proportion. A true claim inflated past its evidence becomes, in its own way, a false one, and it hands opponents an excuse to dismiss everything else. Lively’s essay has a few such places. Correcting them makes his case stronger, not weaker.


**Obama’s private life.** Lively calls Obama “in-the-closet.” There is no evidence for this. It is the kind of claim that lets a reader stop reading. Obama’s public record is enough to judge him by; his private life is not ours to invent.


**Who started it.** Lively’s own history shows that the fusion of Brazil’s left with the LGBT movement predates Obama by decades; the São Paulo group SOMOS dates to 1978, and *Brasil Sem Homofobia* to 2004, years before Obama took office. Washington did not invent Brazil’s revolution. It took sides, lent it prestige, and paid for parts of it. That is a more precise charge, and a more damning one, because it is the charge an American president can actually answer for.


**Mike Benz.** The funding totals Benz cited are the U.S. government’s own numbers. His interpretation — that USAID’s purpose was to bring down Bolsonaro, and that “if USAID did not exist, Bolsonaro would still be president” — is an inference, and some of his specific claims have been contested: the fact-checking agency Lupa, for example, denies ever receiving USAID money. The honest statement is that the documented flow is real and the intent is disputed. Lively himself says this, to his credit.


**Small facts.** Lula’s famous *Playboy* interview was given in 1979, not 2009. Brazil’s 2025 Corruption Perceptions Index score of 35 out of 100, ranking 107th of 182 countries, is accurate and is the second-worst result in Brazil’s history under the index’s current method — but it is not true that only Venezuela, Nicaragua, and Haiti score lower in the Americas; several countries in the hemisphere, Mexico among them, score lower. Bolsonaro was convicted by a panel of the STF, with Moraes as rapporteur, not sentenced by Moraes alone. And the São Paulo pride parade attendance figures cited are organizers’ estimates, which run far above independent counts.


**“Marxist.”** Lula’s Workers’ Party has Marxist roots, and some of its factions still speak that language. But Brazil’s economy remains a market economy, and the word will strike many readers as a label rather than a description. The mechanism is what matters, whatever one calls it: a governing class that uses courts and administrative orders to do what elections will not.


**The voting machines.** No audit has found fraud in Brazil’s electronic voting. Lively is careful here, and I want to be equally careful. His point is architectural, and it is correct: Brazil’s machines produce no voter-verified paper record, so a hidden software flaw could, in principle, change an outcome without leaving evidence. Brazil’s Congress voted down a printed-ballot proposal in 2021. The Defense Ministry’s 2022 audit found no fraud, but stated that it could not affirm the system was free of malicious code. A system that cannot prove its own cleanliness asks its citizens for trust it has not earned the right to demand.


On the 2026 extension, the numbers are plain. About 34,330 votes were cast in the extra two hours, less than one percent of Lula’s 4.43 million margin. Both campaigns had asked for extensions in some places; Bolsonaro’s campaign cited heavy rain in the south. The extension did not change the result. What it changed was trust. A justice who had been the winning candidate’s lawyer, acting alone on that candidate’s petition, overrode the uniform hours the election court had set for the whole country, against the electoral prosecutor’s advice, after the counting had already begun. Even a just act done that way would be corrosive. The appearance of partiality is itself an injury to a republic.

## The Moral Question


The test I apply to any policy has three parts. Is it righteous — does it accord with what God has established? If so, is it loving toward my neighbor? And would I want it done to me?


**Is it righteous?** Marriage, the family, and the moral formation of children are not inventions of the state. Government’s role is to protect them, not to redefine them. And it is not righteous for any government to use its power to criminalize teaching what a people’s faith has taught for millennia. When a court classifies the public teaching of biblical sexual ethics as a form of racism, it has turned conscience into contraband.


**Is it loving?** I want to be clear about what love requires here. Violence against any person is wrong, and protecting every citizen from assault and murder is a legitimate end of government. If American diplomacy had confined itself to that, there would be little to argue about. But love does not mean telling my neighbor what he wants to hear, and it does not mean seizing his country’s lawmaking from him because I think I know better. Love respects a neighbor’s conscience and his right, with his countrymen, to govern himself. A program that bypassed Brazil’s legislature, fed one side of its moral debate with foreign money, and helped create a legal regime under which a Christian writer had to flee is not love. It is control dressed as compassion.


**Would I want it done to me?** Here the answer is easiest of all. Americans would be outraged — rightly — if the government of Brazil or China or any other nation funded American organizations to change our marriage laws, our speech laws, or our courts’ doctrines. We have written that outrage into federal law: foreign nationals are forbidden to spend money in our elections. A principle we insist on for ourselves we must extend to every other people. We cannot demand self-government at home and fund its bypass abroad.


But there is another perspective beyond funding backdoor judicial and legislative intervention. That is transforming the hearts of the people.  If people are taught the Gospel, the way of Godliness, the Biblical revelation, and they vote and ask for a new way of being and living. That is persuasion. As followers of Jesus Christ as our Lord, we have this obligation under the Great Commission. **(Matthew 28:18-20)** *“And Jesus came and spake unto them, saying, All power is given unto me in heaven and in earth. Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost: Teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you: and, lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world. Amen.”*


**The right way to change a nation.** None of this means a people’s moral law can never change, or that outsiders may never speak to it. As followers of Jesus Christ, we are commanded to do exactly that. When the Gospel is taught and hearts are changed, and a people then votes for a new way of living, that is not intervention. It is persuasion, and the change comes through their own consent and their own lawmakers.


The difference lies in three things: who acts, by what means, and toward what end. The Great Commission was given to the church, not to the state. It is carried out by believers acting on conscience with their own gifts, not by a government spending money taken from all its citizens. It works on the heart, which remains free to refuse, not through courts that bind whether one consents or not. And it ends in a people governing themselves, not in their legislature being bypassed.


An American government that funded evangelism abroad with tax dollars would fail the same test. Advocates of any cause, including causes I oppose, who persuade their own neighbors by argument are doing what free people do. Persuasion is the birthright of a free people. Bypassing their consent is not.

## What This Means for America


Lively closes by warning that Brazil is a preview of what could happen here. I agree that it is a warning, and I want to say what I would do about it. These are positions I hold and will advocate; I’m open to a better argument.


**1. No American money to remake other nations’ moral law.** U.S. foreign assistance should not fund advocacy aimed at changing another nation’s laws on marriage, sexuality, family, religion, or speech. Humanitarian aid — food, medicine, disaster relief — is a different matter. Paying activists to win another people’s moral debates is not aid; it is intervention.


**2. Every dollar traced to its last recipient.** Much of the money in the Brazil story moved through subcontracts and intermediaries that do not appear in headline figures. Every grant and sub-grant of U.S. foreign assistance should be published, down to the final recipient, so that citizens can see what their money buys.


**3. Reciprocity as policy.** The United States should declare, and keep, a simple rule: we will not do to other nations’ self-government what we forbid them to do to ours.


**4. Paper ballots Americans can see and count.** Every American vote should leave a voter-verified paper record that can be hand-counted in an audit. An election system should not merely be clean; it should be able to prove it is clean. That is the lesson of Brazil’s machines, and it applies to us.


**5. Lawmaking belongs to lawmakers.** Brazil’s courts became the means by which a governing class did what the people’s representatives refused. The answer in America is judges who interpret the law rather than write it — and citizens who remember that the final check on unjust law is not a judge at all, but a jury. Brazil does not try such questions before juries of ordinary citizens. We do. A jury of free men and women may refuse to convict a neighbor under a law that offends justice, and that power is one of the cornerstones of our liberty. We must guard it.


**6. No American shall be punished for stating his faith.** Whatever any court in any nation decides, no law in the United States should make it a crime for a pastor, a teacher, or a writer to state what his faith teaches about sexuality and marriage.

## Why the Concern Remains Plausible


Some readers will say each piece of Lively’s story has an innocent explanation: grants go to organizations that share an agenda because that is what grants are for; courts expand rights when legislatures stall; a president’s former lawyer may be a fine judge. Each of those may be true in a given case. The question is what the pieces add up to.


When the same agenda is announced by two heads of state, funded by one government’s development agency and a handful of allied foundations, carried by grantees that depend on that money to survive, and enacted by courts after the legislature declines to act — and when the judges who rule on the most contested questions include the president’s own former lawyer — a reasonable person does not need proof of a single coordinated plot to see a system. He sees a governing class that has found a way to rule without persuading.


There is also an intuition that ordinary people share and that experts too easily dismiss: a nation’s moral law should come from its people. When that law arrives instead through foreign money and administrative orders, something unnatural has happened, whatever the paperwork says. Brazilians who felt that way were not paranoid. They were noticing.


Brazil is not America’s destiny. But it is a warning, and a warning heeded is a mercy. We still have juries, a tradition of textual interpretation, a federal system, and a people who expect to be persuaded rather than ruled. The way to keep them is to insist on the same things for every nation that we insist on for ourselves: self-government, open books, verifiable elections, and the freedom to speak one’s conscience without fear.
