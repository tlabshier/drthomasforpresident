# 261127 - Election Integrity

# Count Every Vote Honestly


### Why Election Integrity Must Be a Just Weight, and What It Would Take to Build One

**Thomas Lee Abshier, ND |** **27 November 2026**


---


A cynical maxim, often attributed to Stalin, says it does not matter who votes; what matters is who counts the votes. Whether or not he said it, the warning is sound. A government of, by, and for the people is only as legitimate as its count. If the count can be bent, the people do not rule. Whoever controls the bending does.

I believe America's voting system has real gaps through which a determined person could change an outcome, and I believe we have not built the safeguards that would let a citizen know, rather than trust, that his vote was counted as cast. I want to make that case carefully, because the argument is often made badly, and a bad argument for a good cause does the cause harm.

The position I will defend is this: **every vote should be cast, kept, and counted in a way that makes cheating nearly impossible to do and easy to detect, no matter who attempts it.**

## The Principle: A Just Weight

Scripture is plain about honest measures: *"A false balance is abomination to the LORD: but a just weight is his delight"* **(Proverbs 11:1)**. And again: *"But thou shalt have a perfect and just weight, a perfect and just measure shalt thou have: that thy days may be lengthened in the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee"* **(Deuteronomy 25:15)**.

A ballot count is a scale on which a nation weighs its own will. I hold every policy to three questions: Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? Would I want it done to me?

An honest count is righteous because it is a just weight. It is loving because it protects every neighbor's voice from being stolen, diluted, or silenced. And it passes the third test from both directions: I would want my own vote counted exactly as I cast it, and I would want my opponent's honest victory to stand even when I wish it had gone the other way. A rule that would only catch the other side cheating is not integrity. It is a weapon. The standard must be one that would have caught my own side.

## Why "We Found No Fraud" Is Not Enough

After every disputed election, officials announce that no significant fraud was found. Sometimes that is true. But the statement means something only if the system was built so that fraud, had it occurred, would have been found.

"We looked in the places we looked and found nothing" is not proof of integrity. Proof requires a trail that can be followed from the voter's hand to the final tally, with every step open to inspection by people who do not trust each other. Where that trail exists, a clean audit is strong evidence. Where it does not, a clean audit tells us little. The question is not only whether fraud was found. It is whether the system could have found it.

## Where the System Stands Today

The news is better than many fear, and worse than officials admit.

**Paper.** The most important safeguard is a paper record of every vote, marked or verified by the voter, that can be counted by human hands and compared with what the machines reported. If a machine's software were altered, a hand count of the paper would expose it. According to the federal Election Assistance Commission, over 98 percent of election jurisdictions in 2024 used equipment that produces a paper ballot or an auditable paper record. That is real progress; a decade ago, roughly a quarter of voters lived where there was no paper at all.

**The gaps.** Louisiana is the last state still using paperless voting machines statewide on Election Day; its votes are recorded only electronically. About 23 percent of registered voters live where in-person voters use only ballot-marking devices, which print a ballot after the voter makes choices on a screen. Researchers warn that these machines can be hacked or err, and studies show that voters often fail to catch mistakes on the printout.

**Software.** The voting machine market is dominated by three for-profit companies. They submit their code to federally accredited testing laboratories, but they do not publish it, out of concern that doing so would reveal vulnerabilities. A citizen therefore cannot inspect the code that counts his vote. One nonprofit vendor, VotingWorks, now publishes all of its code for public review, which shows that transparency is possible.

**Audits.** A paper trail protects us only if someone actually checks it. Most states now require some form of post-election audit, but the methods vary widely. According to the National Conference of State Legislatures, 36 states and the District of Columbia conduct traditional audits, and seven conduct risk-limiting audits, a statistically rigorous method that keeps counting paper ballots until there is strong evidence the reported winner really won. Some states require no audit at all.

So the honest picture is this: nearly every vote now leaves a paper trail, but not every vote, not every trail is reliable, and not every trail is checked with rigor.

## The Weakest Link: Mail Ballots

In 2024, 30.3 percent of voters cast their ballots by mail. A mail ballot leaves the supervised polling place and passes through homes, mailboxes, and sometimes the hands of third parties before it returns. That is where the system is most exposed.

This is not a partisan claim. The bipartisan Commission on Federal Election Reform of 2005, co-chaired by former President Jimmy Carter and former Secretary of State James Baker, concluded that "absentee ballots remain the largest source of potential voter fraud." The commission did not call for abolishing absentee voting; it called for stronger security, including an end to the practice of letting candidates or party workers collect and deliver other people's ballots.

The danger is not theoretical. The 1997 Miami mayoral election was overturned after dozens of people were charged with manipulating absentee ballots. In 2018, the result of North Carolina's Ninth Congressional District race was thrown out and the election rerun because an operative working for the Republican candidate ran an illegal absentee-ballot collection scheme. That case matters to my argument: the clearest recent proven fraud in a federal race was committed on behalf of my own side. The safeguards I propose would have caught it.

## Citizenship and Identity

Only citizens may vote in federal elections. Most states verify identity at the polls in some way, but registration has historically relied on a sworn statement of citizenship rather than proof.

The SAVE Act would require documentary proof of citizenship, such as a passport or birth certificate, to register for federal elections. It passed the House in April 2025 with every Republican and four Democrats voting yes, out of 212 Democrats. A broader version, the SAVE America Act, passed the House in February 2026 with one Democratic vote and failed in the Senate in June 2026. I regard near-unanimous opposition to proof of citizenship as a serious failure of judgment by the members who voted against it.

I also want to weigh the evidence honestly, because the strongest case for reform is one that has answered its critics.

**How often noncitizens actually register.** When Georgia audited its rolls in 2024, it found 20 noncitizens among more than 8.2 million registrations, and only nine had ever voted. That is a small number. But it is not zero, and it was found only because Georgia looked. A state that never checks will never find.

**What happened when a state required proof.** Kansas required documentary proof of citizenship from 2013 to 2016. A federal court, after a full trial, found that the requirement blocked more than 31,000 eligible Kansans from registering, while finding that at most 67 noncitizens had registered or attempted to register in Kansas over the preceding 19 years. The law was struck down. Anyone who supports proof of citizenship, as I do, has to answer that finding.

**On the married-name objection.** A woman whose birth certificate shows her maiden name has a legal record of her name change: her marriage certificate. Bringing both is an added step, not a barrier. I regard this objection as overstated.

My answer to Kansas is not to abandon verification but to do it well. Weighty things require effort and intention, and a citizen who cares to vote can be asked to prove he is one. But the state already holds most of the records needed to verify citizenship. The burden should fall first on the government to check its own databases, and only then on the citizen who cannot be verified that way. A citizen's vote should not be lost to paperwork the state could have checked itself, and a noncitizen should not slip through because the state never looked.

## A Lesson from Brazil

Brazil's 2022 presidential election shows why the paper trail matters. After Jair Bolsonaro lost narrowly to Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, Bolsonaro's party asked the electoral court to throw out votes from most of the country's voting machines, citing a software flaw in their internal logs. Independent experts said the flaw did not affect the machines' reliability. The party challenged only the runoff, not the first round on the same machines, in which many of its own candidates had won. The electoral court rejected the challenge and fined the party for bad-faith litigation.

On the evidence presented, I do not find the fraud claim persuasive. But the head of that court was not a disinterested party, and Brazil's machines produce no voter-verified paper ballot; a proposal to add one was defeated in Brazil's Congress in 2021. So the honest conclusion is not that the election was proven clean. It is that no primary evidence of fraud has surfaced, and that the system itself offers no independent record that could prove the matter either way. A nation should never have to rest its legitimacy on trust alone. That is the lesson for us.

## What I Propose

Under the Constitution, each state's legislature sets the times, places, and manner of elections, and Congress may make or alter those rules for federal elections (Article I, Section 4). Most of what follows belongs to the states. Congress can set standards for federal races. A president's role is to lead by voice and to sign sound federal law. Here is what I would urge:


 	- **A voter-verified paper ballot for every vote.** End paperless voting everywhere, beginning with Louisiana. Prefer hand-marked paper ballots, with ballot-marking devices reserved for voters with disabilities who need them.

 	- **A risk-limiting audit of every federal race in every state,** conducted in public, before results are certified.

 	- **Open inspection of voting software.** Publish source code or make it available for independent public review, and verify before and after each election that the software running on each machine matches the certified version.

 	- **A documented chain of custody for every ballot,** with bipartisan observers at every stage, from printing to storage to tabulation.

 	- **Secure mail voting.** Mail ballots by request rather than mass mailing, with identity verification, ballot tracking, signature verification, and an end to third-party ballot collection, as the Carter-Baker commission recommended.

 	- **Citizenship verification at registration,** with the government checking its own records first, documents required only for those it cannot verify, and free documents for citizens who need them.

 	- **Clean and shared voter rolls,** with regular removal of deceased, moved, and ineligible registrants and secure data-sharing between states.

 	- **Voter identification at the polls,** with a free state ID available to every citizen.


None of this assumes that any past election was stolen. A lock on the door does not accuse the neighbors. It simply means that no one has to wonder.

## Why This Concern Remains Serious

Some will say that the audits we already have show fraud is rare, and that pressing the issue undermines public confidence. I take the first point seriously; the audit evidence I have cited does show that large-scale fraud has not been found where people have looked. But the concern remains serious for four reasons.

First, the vulnerabilities are real and documented: paperless voting in one state, unverified printouts in others, unpublished code, uneven audits, and mail ballots that leave supervised custody. These are not subtle loopholes; they are the places where fraud, if attempted, could live.

Second, the incentives are enormous. Federal elections decide who controls trillions of dollars in spending and the power of the sword. Where the reward is that large, someone will eventually try.

Third, confidence cannot be restored by assurance. It can only be restored by verification. A citizen who doubts the count is not answered by being told to stop doubting. He is answered by a system he can watch work.

Fourth, the stakes are moral, not merely partisan. If a nation can be steered by those who control the count rather than by the consent of the governed, then every other reform, every policy, every moral cause depends on a foundation that can be quietly removed.

I stand on the side of an election system built so that cheating is nearly impossible and easily detected. The areas of real uncertainty are how much fraud, if any, has gone undetected in places without strong audits, how to verify citizenship without blocking eligible citizens as Kansas did, and how to secure mail voting for those who genuinely need it. Those uncertainties are reasons to build better safeguards, not reasons to wait.

I hold this position open to a better argument. But I do not believe any argument can make a false balance acceptable to a people who claim to govern themselves.

This paper leans on official election data, court findings, and mainstream reporting. Those sources have limits. Election officials report on systems they run, so their findings of no fraud are, in part, testimony about their own work. Audits find only what they are designed to look for, and states without rigorous audits produce no evidence either way. Court findings in Kansas and Brazil rest on the evidence the parties chose to present. I found no primary evidence of large-scale fraud in the sources I examined; I also found real gaps where such evidence would not be collected. I have tried to state both.
