# 261009 - Science Summit Analysis

# Morning News Analysis: The “Golden Age of Science” Summit


**Dr. Thomas for President 2028 — October 9, 2026**


*Source reviewed: Jeff Childers, “The Golden Age,” Coffee & Covid, October 9, 2026. Coffee & Covid is a widely read daily newsletter written by a Florida attorney. It is openly sympathetic to President Trump, and it says so. That is no fault in itself; every writer has a point of view. But a reader should know it going in, and should separate the newsletter’s reporting from its cheering. Roughly half of this issue is a playful time-travel theory that the author himself calls entertainment. The other half reports a real event with real policy consequences. This analysis takes each item in turn, states what can be confirmed, and says what my administration would do.*


---


## The standard I apply


Every position below rests on one question: would a neighbor feel loved by this act in the public square? Love here is not sentiment. It means telling the neighbor the truth, protecting what belongs to him, refusing to rule him by force or flattery, and spending his money as carefully as he would. The legal and economic arguments that follow serve that moral test; they do not replace it.


I hold each of these positions as an advocate. I give my reasons, and I will change my mind for a better argument.


---


## 1. The summit and its venue


**What happened.** On October 8, the President hosted the “Science: A New Golden Age Summit” at the former U.S. Institute of Peace building, which he has since renamed. The DOGE team took over the Institute in 2025, and its new president is Darren Beattie of Revolver News. Federal court has contested the takeover of this congressionally chartered body.


**My assessment.** The newsletter calls the Institute a “color revolution headquarters.” Serious arguments suggest the Institute drifted into foreign meddling that Americans never approved. But the remedy for a bad institution that Congress created is for Congress to abolish or reform it. When the executive occupies such an institution by force and renames it for the sitting President, he teaches every future President that a building, an agency, or a budget belongs to whoever holds the keys. Many of us will not like the next man who holds them.


**What my administration would do.**


- Ask Congress to vote, up or down, on whether the Institute should exist, and abide by the result.

- Name no federal building, program, or account after any living officeholder, including me. Honor belongs to the dead, whose work is finished and can be judged.


---


## 2. “The Golden Age has begun”


**What happened.** The President opened by declaring that the Golden Age has now begun.


**My assessment.** Historians name a golden age afterward, by looking at results. A leader who declares it in advance asks the public to judge by announcement rather than outcome. That is exactly what the summit’s own “metascience” initiative is supposed to correct in science. It should be corrected in government as well.


**What my administration would do.**


- Make no declarations of eras. Publish instead, every year, a plain scorecard: real wages, energy prices, life expectancy, debt per citizen, scientific results delivered against what was promised. Let citizens decide whether the age is golden.


---


## 3. Dr. John Trump, Nikola Tesla, and the missing papers


**What happened.** The President honored his uncle, Dr. John G. Trump, an MIT electrical engineer who received the National Medal of Science in 1983. During World War II, Dr. Trump served in a leadership role at MIT’s Radiation Laboratory, where radar was developed. After Tesla died in 1943, the government asked Dr. Trump to review Tesla’s papers; he reported that they were mainly speculative, philosophical, and promotional.


**My assessment.** Dr. Trump was a real and distinguished scientist, and honoring him is fitting. Two corrections to the newsletter: Tesla did not invent alternating current, which predates him; his great contributions were the polyphase AC system and the induction motor that made AC practical. And Dr. Trump helped lead the radar effort rather than leading it alone; it was a large team enterprise.


Questions about Tesla’s papers are legitimate historical questions. The honest way to settle them is to open the records.


**What my administration would do.**


- Direct every agency holding records on Tesla’s papers, including any remaining classified material, to review and release them, with any withheld page justified in writing. Secrecy breeds legend; disclosure ends it.


---


## 4. The time-travel theory


**What happened.** The newsletter spins a theory linking Tesla, Dr. Trump, Ingersoll Lockwood’s 1890s children’s books about a boy named Baron Trump, and Elon Musk, suggesting the Trump family may have foresight. The author repeatedly says it is purely for entertainment and “unprovable by definition.”


**My assessment.** There is nothing wrong with a good story told as a story. But two features of this one deserve a sober word.


First, a claim that is unprovable by definition is, for that very reason, unfit to be believed. Any coincidence can be fitted to it, and nothing could ever count against it.


Second, the theory’s working premise is that a certain leader “always wins” because a hidden hand guides him. Christians should be careful with that kind of story about any ruler. Scripture tells us to pray for those in authority, not to imagine that they hold secret knowledge that places them beyond ordinary judgment. When a public believes its leader is guided by forces it cannot examine, it stops examining the leader. That is dangerous, no matter which party benefits.


**What my administration would do.** Nothing by policy; this is not a policy matter. As a matter of leadership, I would decline to encourage stories that elevate me above accountability, even in jest.


---


## 5. Nuclear power in space


**What happened.** NASA and the Energy Department are developing Space Reactor-1 “Freedom,” a nuclear-electric spacecraft targeted for a December 2028 launch toward Mars, built on hardware originally meant for the lunar Gateway station. A follow-on, Lunar Reactor-1, aims for a 2030 landing to power a Moon base. (The newsletter says the lunar reactor has no date; the agencies have set 2030.) Both designs use uranium fuel.


**My assessment.** This is good work and worth supporting. Fission is the only proven way to supply steady power on the Moon through its two-week nights, and to push large spacecraft beyond Mars. My platform has always held that abundant energy is the foundation of a free and prosperous people. That is as true on Earth as off it, and the ground comes first.


**What my administration would do.**


- Continue Space Reactor-1 and Lunar Reactor-1, and publish honest cost and schedule figures every year.

- Make a parallel investment on Earth: a national program to license and build thorium molten-salt reactors that can run at atmospheric pressure, consume existing spent fuel, and draw on a fuel America has in abundance. Lessons from compact space reactors should flow directly into civilian designs.


---


## 6. The Genesis Mission pledges and the AI industry


**What happened.** Eleven companies pledged about $2.4 billion in computing credits and tools to the Genesis Mission, the federal AI-for-science program. Nvidia pledged $1 billion, AMD $500 million, OpenAI $200 million, and Anthropic and Google $150 million each. The President also cited a “White House Accord on Superintelligence” signed with industry executives on September 29, which he described as morally binding.


**My assessment.** Applying AI to genuine scientific problems is worthwhile. But the companies making these gifts are the same companies whose products the federal government regulates and buys. A gift of this size from a party seeking favorable policy creates an obligation, whether anyone says it aloud or not. I have written about regulatory capture in AI on this site. Pledges made on a stage, followed by medals handed to the pledgers’ chief executives, are what capture looks like in its early stages.


A “morally binding” accord also binds no one. Many who build it say superintelligence is a potential threat to human freedom and survival. A threat of that weight warrants law, passed by the people’s representatives, not a handshake at lunch.


**What my administration would do.**


- Accept in-kind gifts to federal programs only under full public disclosure of their terms, and bar any donor from preference in procurement or regulation for the life of the gift.

- Ask Congress to turn the Superintelligence Accord into binding statute, with independent safety review before any frontier system is deployed in critical infrastructure.


---


## 7. The quantum computing race


**What happened.** The Energy Department launched a $215 million competition to build a fault-tolerant quantum computer, and the President set a goal of having one by 2028.


**My assessment.** The research deserves support, but the 2028 goal is very ambitious and shouldn’t be promised to the public as certain. More importantly, a working fault-tolerant quantum computer would eventually break the encryption that protects Americans’ bank accounts, medical records, and private messages. Building the machine is less urgent than protecting citizens from it, whoever builds it first.


**What my administration would do.**


- Fund the competition, and at the same time set a firm, short deadline to move every federal system, and the banking system, onto quantum-resistant encryption. Protecting the neighbor’s savings comes before the race for prestige.


---


## 8. “Virtual cells” and medical research


**What happened.** NIH, the Energy Department, and Biohub are putting $1.8 billion into computer simulations of living cells.


**My assessment.** As a physician, I welcome tools that let researchers test ideas quickly and with fewer animals. But a simulation can only reflect what we already know about the cell; it generates hypotheses, not proof. The history of medicine is full of elegant models that failed in living patients. The research most neglected today isn’t high-tech at all: nutrition, sleep, exercise, and natural compounds no company can patent, so no company will fund.


**What my administration would do.**


- Require that every virtual-cell prediction used in drug approval be confirmed in living systems, and that the models and data be public.

- Dedicate a fixed share of NIH funding to rigorous trials of unpatentable therapies and lifestyle medicine, the research that serves patients but no shareholder.


---


## 9. Fellowships, X-Labs, and “metascience”


**What happened.** A $100 million program for accelerated four-year PhDs, $310 million or more for the X-Labs research consortium, and a new “metascience” effort to test whether federal science funding produces results.


**My assessment.** Metascience is the best idea in the package. Much of published research fails to replicate, and for decades no one checked whether expensive programs delivered what they promised. Checking our work is simple honesty.


**What my administration would do.**


- Expand metascience into a standing replication fund: a fixed share of every research budget set aside to reproduce the most consequential findings, with results published whether they confirm or refute.

- Apply the same test to every federal program, not only science.


---


## 10. Young Scholars in mathematics


**What happened.** The National Science Foundation is reviving a program, dropped in 1996, that selects students in grades 3–12 on demonstrated mathematical ability.


**My assessment.** Gifted children are neighbors too, and a nation that will not cultivate its ablest young minds wastes a gift entrusted to it.


**What my administration would do.**


- Support the program, and require that homeschooled students and students in private and church schools be eligible on exactly the same terms as public-school students.


---


## 11. Merit in place of DEI


**What happened.** The administration says it has replaced diversity, equity, and inclusion criteria with merit across federal research, and supports only “gold standard” science.


**My assessment.** I agree with the principle. Judging a scientist by his group rather than his work is unjust to him, and to everyone his work could serve. But the remedy must not hand the same power to a different set of political hands. Critics have raised concerns that recent rules let political appointees terminate grants or overrule scientific judgment. A merit system that political appointees can override is not a merit system.


**What my administration would do.**


- Publish clear, written criteria for every grant decision, and bar political appointees, of either party, from overruling scientific review except in writing with stated reasons that are made public.


---


## 12. The medals


**What happened.** Elon Musk, Sergey Brin, Jensen Huang, and Lisa Su received the National Medal of Science; Michael Dell and Satya Nadella received the National Medal of Technology and Innovation. Press reports note that each honoree, or his company, financially supported the President or Republican causes.


**My assessment.** Several of these people have built remarkable things, and the newsletter is right that two of the six never finished college. That credentials are not achievement is a lesson worth teaching. But the nation’s highest scientific honors are meant to recognize discovery, and most of these honorees are executives rather than discoverers. When the honors flow to those who have supported the man presenting them, the public cannot tell whether it is witnessing recognition or reward. That uncertainty itself cheapens the medal for every honest scientist who has received it.


**What my administration would do.**


- Follow the recommendations of the independent statutory committee that nominates medalists, and publish its list in full.

- Award no medal during my term to anyone who has given to my campaign. Since I accept no donations, this rule costs me nothing, and that is the point: it should cost every President nothing.


---


## 13. Trump Accounts and the Dell pledge


**What happened.** Michael and Susan Dell pledged $6.25 billion to seed investment accounts for 25 million children, alongside the new federal program that opens such an account for every American child. The newsletter suggests these could replace Social Security, while noting the President did not say so.


**My assessment.** Private generosity on this scale is admirable, and an account that a family actually owns is morally better than a promise the government can change at will. But honesty requires proportion. A seed deposit of a few hundred dollars per child, even compounded for decades, will not replace Social Security, and suggesting it might misleads the people who depend on that system. (The newsletter also attributes to Einstein a famous line about compound interest; there is no reliable record that he said it.) Social Security’s trust fund is projected to run short in the early-to-mid 2030s. We need to face that problem openly, not obscure it with a hopeful comparison.


**What my administration would do.**


- Keep the accounts, keep them family-owned, and let churches, charities, and relatives contribute to them freely.

- Present Congress and the public with an honest plan for Social Security solvency that keeps the promise made to those who paid in.


---


## 14. Governing without Congress


**What happened.** The newsletter’s closing argument, and its central praise, is that the $6 billion initiative needed no bill, no budget, and no vote. Much of it came from private pledges and the rest from money already in the budget. “What Congress never appropriated,” the author writes, “a 2027 Democrat House cannot defund.”


**My assessment.** This is the newsletter’s most important point, and I believe it is exactly backward.


The Constitution gives Congress the power of the purse so the people, through their representatives, decide how their government acts. A program that needs no vote is a program the people never approved. The same reasoning that celebrates it also celebrates any future President who governs by will, and the next President, of whatever party, will inherit every tool this one perfects. What one man builds by force of personality, another can unbuild the same way. Only what the people’s representatives enact by law endures with their consent.


There is also a quieter cost. Programs funded by private pledges leave the government in debt to its donors, and that debt is paid in policy. A neighbor cannot feel loved by a government whose decisions are made without his voice.


**What my administration would do.**


- Send every major initiative to Congress for authorization and appropriation, and accept a “no” when the people’s representatives give one.

- Attach a sunset to every executive order, so that it lapses unless Congress makes it law.

- Publish every private pledge to a federal program, with its terms, in a single public register.


---


## 15. Energy at home


**What happened.** In passing, the President noted that new modular nuclear reactors came online this year; the newsletter contrasts this with Germany, which closed its last three nuclear plants in 2023.


**My assessment.** On this, the newsletter and I agree. Closing working, clean, reliable power plants was a costly mistake that left Germany more dependent on fossil fuels and foreign suppliers. Abundant, affordable energy is the first foundation of my platform, because it lowers the cost of everything else a family needs.


**What my administration would do.**


- Streamline licensing for advanced reactors, with thorium molten-salt designs as a national priority, while keeping safety review rigorous and fully public.


---


## Summary


The summit contained real goods: space reactors, metascience, a revived math program for gifted children, a turn toward merit. It also showed a pattern I would reverse: honors flowing to donors, gifts from companies the government regulates, institutions seized rather than reformed by law, and a governing style that treats Congress as an obstacle. Coffee & Covid celebrates that last feature as genius. I see it as the weakness that will undo much of the good, because what isn’t built on the people’s consent isn’t built to last.
