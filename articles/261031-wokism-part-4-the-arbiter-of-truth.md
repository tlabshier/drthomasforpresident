# 261031 - Wokism Part 4 - The Arbiter of Truth

# The False Gods of Wokism


## Part 4: Who Decides What Is True? — Institutions, Orthodoxy, and Speech


by Thomas Lee Abshier, ND 11/1/2026


## Plain language summary


Every religion that gains power needs institutions to teach it and enforce it. The woke religion found them ready-made: universities, newsrooms, agencies, corporate human-resources departments, and now the companies that build artificial intelligence. In those institutions, dissent is treated as harm, speech is policed as violence, and a small class of credentialed people decides what counts as truth and what counts as “misinformation.” This is not a conspiracy theory; it is a documented pattern, from the suppression of news stories to government pressure on social media companies to the loyalty statements required of job applicants. The Christian answer begins with the same truth as the rest of this series: God lives our lives from the inside, including our words and our consciences. To force a man to say what he believes is false is to make him lie in the presence of the God who lives his speech. And because only God sees from inside every life, no human institution may claim His role as the final judge of truth. Truth is found by open contest under God, not by decree. The remedies: government may not pressure anyone to silence lawful speech; no public institution may require political or ideological loyalty statements; agencies must answer to Congress and to juries; and the conscience programmed into AI must be published.


---


## Rick Kirchner’s terms


My friend Rick Kirchner, whose Facebook glossary prompted this series, gave the problem several names: *elite capture*, *the overlap*, *the orthodoxy*, *institutional capture*, and, at the end, *information monopoly*: “the control of what ‘counts’ as a legitimate fact, opinion, source, or question.” He put it in one line: whoever controls the vocabulary controls the argument.


I agree, and I want to examine why. A religion needs more than believers. It needs clergy to interpret it, schools to teach it, and some way to discipline heretics. The woke religion did not have to build these. It moved into institutions that already held the public’s trust, and it used that trust to establish its creed.


## The doctrine stated fairly


The woke doctrine of speech and institutions holds that:


- **Language creates reality.** Words do not merely describe the world; they shape it, and the words of the powerful keep the oppressed in their place.

- **Speech can be violence.** Words that wound members of oppressed groups cause real harm, and the community has a duty to prevent them.

- **Neutrality is complicity.** An institution that does not actively oppose oppression supports it. Every university, company, and agency must therefore take a side.

- **Misinformation must be managed.** False and harmful claims spread faster than truth, so experts and platforms must identify them and limit their reach.

- **Free speech is not freedom from consequences.** Private institutions may discipline employees and remove users who violate their standards; that is not censorship.


The steelman goes like this: “During a pandemic, false claims about treatments got people killed. Online harassment drives people from public life. Hate speech has preceded violence throughout history. Institutions have a responsibility to the people they serve, and a company has every right to decide what it will publish. Conservatives call it censorship when they lose an argument.”


There is truth in that. Lies can kill. Harassment is real. A private publisher has the right to choose what it prints. And some who cry “censorship” are simply angry that their views are unpopular.


But that is not the whole picture, and the rest of it is what matters.


## The God who lives our words


In Part 1, I set out the truth at the center of this series: God does not merely watch our lives; He lives them with us, from the inside. *“In him we live, and move, and have our being”* (Acts 17:28). Every experience a man has, God has with him.


That includes our words and our consciences. Jesus said that *“every idle word that men shall speak, they shall give account thereof in the day of judgment. For by thy words thou shalt be justified, and by thy words thou shalt be condemned”* (Matthew 12:36–37). Our speech is not a private matter between us and our listeners. God lives it with us.


Two consequences follow, and they are the whole argument of this part.


**First, compelled speech is a sin against God, not only against man.** When an institution forces a man to affirm what he believes is false, whether a pronoun, a political statement, or a confession of guilt he does not feel, it forces him to lie in the presence of the God who lives his speech. *“Let your communication be, Yea, yea; Nay, nay”* (Matthew 5:37). The early Christians refused to burn a pinch of incense to the emperor, not because the gesture was large but because it was false. A loyalty statement written to the woke creed is the same pinch of incense.


**Second, no human institution may claim God’s place as the final judge of truth.** God lives every life, every conscience, every point of view, and so He alone sees the whole. No committee, newsroom, agency, or algorithm does. When an institution claims the authority to decide what may be said and what is “misinformation,” and enforces that judgment by silencing dissent, it claims a divine prerogative.


The Christian way to truth is different: open contest, under God, among people who know they can be wrong. *“He that is first in his own cause seemeth just; but his neighbour cometh and searcheth him”* (Proverbs 18:17). The first story always sounds right until it is cross-examined. That is why the founders protected speech, why the courts use adversaries, and why a free press matters. Truth is not decreed. It is found, and it can only be found where people are free to search.


## The record


I want to make this argument from evidence, not from grievance, so here is part of the record.


**The news stories that were suppressed.** In October 2020, weeks before the presidential election, the *New York Post* published reporting on a laptop belonging to Hunter Biden. Twitter blocked users from sharing the story, and fifty-one former intelligence officials signed a letter suggesting it had the hallmarks of Russian disinformation. The laptop was later confirmed to be genuine and was used as evidence in his federal prosecution.


**The hypothesis that was banned.** In February 2021, Facebook announced it would remove claims that the COVID-19 virus was man-made. In May 2021, it reversed course. Since then, the FBI and the Department of Energy have each assessed that a laboratory origin is most likely, and in 2025 the CIA stated the same with low confidence. The question is still debated, but a hypothesis that serious intelligence agencies now favor was once removed from the largest platform in the world as misinformation.


**The government’s hand.** In *Missouri v. Biden*, federal courts found substantial evidence that White House and FBI officials had pressured social media companies to remove lawful speech. The Supreme Court, in *Murthy v. Missouri* (2024), ruled that the plaintiffs lacked standing and did not decide the underlying question. Whatever the final legal answer, the record shows officials of the government pressing private companies to silence citizens.


**The monoculture.** In a 2018 study of faculty at leading liberal arts colleges, the economist Mitchell Langbert found that registered Democrats outnumbered Republicans by roughly ten to one, and that in many departments there were no Republicans at all. The Center for Public Integrity found that in the 2016 election, more than 95 percent of political donations from journalists went to one candidate. Gallup reports that Americans’ trust in the mass media has fallen to the lowest levels it has ever recorded.


**The loyalty statements.** Over the last decade, many public universities required faculty applicants to submit “diversity statements” describing their commitment to the institution’s equity agenda, and some screened out applicants whose statements were judged inadequate before their scholarship was even read. Several universities have since dropped the requirement, which tells you something about how it looked in daylight.


None of this requires a secret conspiracy. It requires only what Rick Kirchner called *the overlap*: people who share one worldview, working in the institutions that shape public knowledge, rewarding those who agree and quietly excluding those who don’t. A monoculture does not need to coordinate. It only needs to agree.


## Testing the doctrine


### 1. “Speak truth to power” — until it holds power


The woke religion began as a movement of dissent. It taught that established institutions serve the powerful and that the outsider’s voice must be heard. Then it took over the institutions, and its attitude toward dissent reversed. The same people who taught suspicion of authority now demand deference to “the experts,” “the science,” and “trusted sources,” as long as those authorities agree with them.


That is not a principle. A principle applies the same way whether you hold power or not. The creed distrusts authority when the authority is someone else’s, and enforces it when the authority is its own.


### 2. “Words are violence” makes everyone a censor


If words that cause distress are violence, then whoever feels the most distress holds a veto over speech. And since nearly every important truth distresses someone, the doctrine gives the most easily offended the power to silence the rest.


Scripture draws the line differently. Some speech really is wrong: fraud, slander, false witness, incitement to violence. These have victims and can be judged. But the prophets spoke words that distressed kings, and Jesus spoke words that offended the religious authorities enough that they killed Him. A rule that forbids distressing speech would have silenced them all.


### 3. Who checks the fact-checkers?


The doctrine trusts experts and platforms to separate truth from misinformation. But the record above shows that the experts and platforms were sometimes wrong, and sometimes wrong in a direction that served those in power. When the institution that decides what is misinformation is itself captured by one side, “misinformation” comes to mean “what the other side says.”


This is the problem that Proverbs 18:17 anticipates. The first account seems right until the neighbor searches it. An arrangement that forbids the neighbor to search has removed the only way error is ever corrected.


### 4. Neutrality abolished, then claimed


The doctrine says neutrality is complicity and every institution must take a side. Yet the same institutions continue to present themselves to the public as neutral: the university as a place of open inquiry, the newspaper as an objective record, the agency as a nonpartisan servant. They cannot be both. An institution that has taken a side should say so, and the public should be free to weigh its claims accordingly. Taking a side while claiming neutrality is the definition of propaganda.


## The newest institution: the machine


There is a new institution now, and it may become the most powerful of all. Artificial intelligence is becoming the counselor that millions of people consult for their questions about history, morality, medicine, and faith. Each model is built with a charter: a set of instructions about what it will affirm, what it will hedge, and what it will refuse.


In *The Machine That Would Not Say the Sentence* (September 2026), I examined a staged debate between two chatbots on whether Christianity is the most beneficial framework the world has known, and I asked the question that turns out to be the whole question: when a machine has a conscience, whose is it? If one company’s standard of the good is installed in every school, agency, and courthouse, the nation will have a single catechism, written by people no one elected.


## Applying the test


**Is it righteous?** No. Compelling a man to affirm what he believes is false makes him lie before the God who lives his speech. Claiming the authority to decide final truth claims what belongs to God alone.


**Is it loving?** No. It humiliates dissenters, drives honest people into silence, and leaves the public with a picture of the world that has been filtered by one faction. A neighbor does not feel loved by an institution that decides for him what he may hear.


**Would I want it done to me?** The woke believer should ask this question most carefully. Institutions change hands. The power to silence “misinformation” will one day be held by people he distrusts. Measured by the self committed to righteousness, no one should want any faction, including his own, to hold that power.


## Why the concern is not overblown


A critic will say that every era has had dominant institutions with dominant views, and that conservatives control plenty of institutions themselves. Some of that is true. But three things make this moment different.


**The reach is new.** A handful of platforms now carry most of the public’s daily information. A decision by a few people at a few companies can reach hundreds of millions of people in an afternoon. No newspaper editor of the past had that power.


**The government has reached through private hands.** The First Amendment forbids the government to abridge speech. When officials press private companies to do what the government itself may not, the constitutional protection is evaded without being repealed.


**Money and power reinforce belief.** Careers, grants, promotions, and reputations depend on staying within the orthodoxy. When a person’s livelihood depends on a belief, he will defend it whether or not it is true. That is why, as I wrote in 2024, those who have built their identity on the narrative find it so hard to admit they were deceived. The cost of admitting it is the self.


## Where the argument must be careful


Many people working in universities, newsrooms, and agencies are honest professionals doing careful work, and some institutions have begun to correct themselves. Some false claims really are dangerous, and some speech, such as fraud, defamation, and incitement, has always been subject to law. A private publisher has the right to decide what it prints.


My argument is not that every institution is corrupt or that all speech must be amplified. As I wrote years ago, the freedom to speak does not obligate anyone to promote an idea. My argument is that the government must never use its power to silence lawful speech, directly or through others; that no public institution may require ideological loyalty as a condition of employment or education; and that no human body may claim the final authority over truth that belongs to God.


These boundaries do not weaken the thesis. A religion has captured institutions that the public trusted to be neutral, and it uses them to define truth and discipline dissent.


## What I would do


- **Government hands off lawful speech.** No federal official may request, pressure, or coordinate with any private company to remove or suppress lawful speech. Every contact between government officials and platforms about content should be logged and published.

- **No loyalty oaths.** No public institution, and no institution receiving public funds, may require applicants, employees, or students to affirm any political or ideological position as a condition of hiring, promotion, or admission.

- **Agencies answer to Congress and to juries.** As I proposed in *Reforming the Administrative State* (October 2026): Congress votes on every major rule; every rule and agency expires unless renewed; no agency writes, enforces, and judges its own rules; and any penalty an agency seeks may go before a jury of citizens, who may refuse to enforce a rule they find unjust.

- **Publish the machine’s conscience.** As I argued in *The Machine That Would Not Say the Sentence*: any AI model offered to the public at scale should publish the full document that governs what it affirms, hedges, and refuses; no government should adopt a single model as the default oracle for public schools or administration; and parents, not platforms, should choose the charter a child’s machine runs on.

- **More voices, not fewer.** The answer to a monopoly on information is not a different monopoly. It is competition: independent journalism, citizen review, and the Christos Voting Network, through which citizens can rate the institutions that govern them.

- **Lead by voice.** The president should say plainly that truth is found by open argument, that dissent is not violence, and that no American should have to lie to keep his job.


## The better way


The woke religion promised to give a voice to the voiceless. In power, it took the voice of everyone who disagreed. That is what happens when a movement makes itself the judge of truth: it cannot tolerate a rival judge.


The Christian answer is humility before the One who sees all. Because God lives every life and every conscience, no man and no institution sees the whole. We find truth together, by searching one another’s claims, under a God who will judge every word. *“And ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free”* (John 8:32). Truth that must be protected from questions is not the truth that sets men free.


The church has a part to play here too. If we want an open public square, we must argue in it honestly: with reasons, without exaggeration, and with the humility to admit when we are wrong. The world will believe our defense of free speech when it sees that we are not afraid of the argument.


## Where to push


- **Is there a version of content moderation that a Christian should support?** I accept limits on fraud, defamation, and incitement. Show me where the line between those and suppression of dissent should fall.

- **Can the government ever legitimately warn platforms about dangerous falsehoods?** I say officials may speak publicly, but may not pressure privately. Show me a case where that rule fails.

- **Does “God lives our words” make all compelled speech wrong?** What about a court requiring a man to testify, or a contract requiring a statement? I say these compel truth, not falsehood. Show me where the distinction breaks down.
