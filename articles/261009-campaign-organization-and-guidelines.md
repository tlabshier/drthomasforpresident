# 261009 - Campaign Organization and Guidelines

# The Map and the Measure: How This Campaign Is Organized, and Why


*Dr. Thomas Lee Abshier, ND — October 2026*


A campaign usually centers on a candidate’s strategy to win: events and schedules, rallies, debates, Electoral College considerations, donors, deadlines. This one is organized around a body of writing. I take no money, I am on no ballot, and I expect to persuade only by reason. That means that my words are the campaign. I intend to make my database of essays available to every citizen so they can find out what I think about a question that concerns them.


Accessing my indexed GitHub database lets you ask your AI what I believe, why, and what my stand means for your economic well-being, religion, lifestyle, and culture. If you find a flaw, a disconnected reason, a philosophical disagreement, etc., then send me an email, and I will do my best to respond to your circumstance. So the first practical problem of this campaign is organization, and behind that organization sits a philosophy that decides what goes on the map and how I judge each piece. This essay explains both.


## Why the writing needs a map


I write most days. I read an article, a speech, or a column, and I respond with a position paper or a shorter reflection. Over the past few months, I have produced a large, growing collection. The current presidential position papers join a much older collection: essays from my 2003 run for the Oregon Senate, many on the Founding Fathers and the principles of the Constitution; my 2024 DrThomasForPresident campaign; my essays of medicine at www.naturedox.com; my essays on Christian Philosophy, apologetics, and culture at www.Renaissance-Ministries.com; my essays at www.mycounselor.com; my essays on Science and technology at www.TheoryOfAbsolutes.com; and my papers on my theory of everything at www.hyperphysics.com.


The essays live in what is essentially a flat collection. No folders lock an essay into one place. Instead, every essay carries a small block of hidden information (called front matter, written in YAML) that records its topics, scale, source, and key ideas. That block is invisible to the reader, but it lets the whole collection be sorted in many ways: by date, by author responded to, by policy area, by the era of my writing, or by principle. A flat collection with good labels is more flexible than a filing cabinet, because one essay on energy policy toward China can appear under energy, under foreign policy, and under accountability without being copied three times.


The labels will not be right the first time. As the collection grows, we will discover categories we did not anticipate and refine the labels in periodic batches rather than piecemeal. At some size, a flat collection may become too large to handle at once and will need to be divided into sections. We will cross that bridge when the collection tells us we have reached it.


## Two directions on the map


The reader will eventually see this organization as a mind map: a page of topics, each opening into a more detailed map, each opening again, as deep as the writing goes. My physics site already works this way, and I have found it a good way to let a reader start with the whole picture and descend to the detail he cares about. There are two natural directions of descent.


**The first is scale.** Every question of government lives at some level: international, national, state, local, family, and individual. A reader can begin with the nations, click on the United States, then on a state, then on a city, and at each level see the essays that bear on that level. Or he can open a state and see, instead of cities, all the topics I have written about as they apply there. The scale axis is the easy one; geography and the structure of our federal system already give it, including the Tenth Amendment’s reservation of powers to the states and the people.


**The second is concern.** This is the harder and more interesting axis: the abstract space of what human beings must deal with together. Science and technology, economics, energy, environment, health, education, defense, justice, family, faith. A useful starting checklist is the list of federal departments. Government has tried to regulate nearly everything, and its departments are, in effect, an inventory of the areas of human concern. I do not endorse the scope of what those departments do. But borrowing their list lets us lay my writing against a complete map of public life and see plainly where we are thin. If the map shows twenty essays on energy and two on agriculture, that is a gap, and it tells me where to write next.


On a phone, a large interactive map is hard to use, so the site will offer a list-style view on small screens and the full map on larger ones, where the reader can drag and zoom to see what lies in each region.


This isn’t decoration. A navigable platform is a platform you can check. A voter should be able to ask “What does he think about water rights in my state?” and find the answer, along with the reasoning behind it, in a few clicks. Making that possible shows him respect.


## The center of the map: a New ERA


Every map needs a center, and this one has had the same center since 2003: **Energy, Righteousness, and Accountability.** I first chose the acronym partly because it was memorable. Over twenty years, it has proven to be more than a slogan. I now test each new essay against all three, and in the process I have come to understand them better.


### Energy


Energy is the foundation, and I now see more clearly how much of the world’s turmoil traces back to it. Nations compete for it because whoever controls abundant, cheap energy controls manufacturing, agriculture, transport, and defense. Oil wealth has also given some regimes resources far beyond what their own productive economies would ever have supplied, and some have used that wealth to fund and export ideologies hostile to liberty. A nation that produces its own abundant energy is not obliged to finance its adversaries.


Abundant energy is a power tool, and power tools demand more care than hand tools, not less. Theodore Kaczynski concluded that technological society was destroying mankind and that the answer was to return to the horse and the millstone. The concern was not baseless, but the conclusion is impossible, and the means he chose were monstrous. Life does not run backward. The answer to powerful tools is not to abandon them but to use them morally. That is why I favor thorium molten-salt reactors: I believe they offer abundant power with a far smaller ecological footprint than the alternatives, including wind and solar installations whose bird deaths, land use, and enormous decommissioning costs are rarely counted in their advertised economics. I hold that position on the evidence as I understand it, and I will change it if better evidence shows I am wrong.


### Righteousness


Righteousness is the part of the frame I had neglected, and recognizing it was the most important thing I learned this month.


While drafting many recent position papers, the guiding principle kept coming back to “love your neighbor.” That is the second great commandment, and every policy on this site should pass the test of whether a neighbor in the public square would feel loved by it. But as I worked through how the United States should deal with China, I realized that framing was missing something. Love of neighbor without a stated standard of right collapses into pleasing him. It gives him cupcakes and does not notice it is giving him diabetes.


Real love includes dealing with a neighbor righteously, and moving him, as far as it lies within our power, toward what is right. It holds together two things that are easy to separate: agape, the steady commitment to another’s good, and what we call tough love, the willingness to tell him and show him what his life requires even when he would rather not hear it. A policy that only makes people feel good is not loving. A policy that only enforces rules is not loving either. The righteous policy does both, and that requires stating the standard openly rather than leaving it implied.


So every essay on this site should now answer an explicit question: is what we propose *right*, and not merely kind, efficient, or popular?


### Accountability


Accountability is the consequence side of righteousness. If you commit a crime, you serve time. If you take what you want, you pay for it. If you eat Twinkies all day, don’t be surprised when you lose your feet to diabetes. Accountability is the recognition that every choice carries a cost and that the person who makes the choice should bear it, rather than shifting it onto his neighbors.


Justice, accountability, and righteousness live in the same domain. We are held to account only for what is unrighteous, and righteousness without accountability is only advice. Among nations, as among people, the ordinary condition is not harmony but a standoff between parties who do not fully trust one another. Peace in that condition depends on everyone knowing that bad conduct carries a real cost. The state, at its best, is like a father among quarreling children: not a master who owns them, but the adult in the room who mediates disputes, administers correction when it is earned, and is himself held to a higher standard than those he corrects.


## Why character is the qualification


That last point leads to the deepest principle behind this campaign. Human beings do not know perfect religion, and we do not exercise power perfectly. Policy ideas are cheap; whether they work will be learned in practice. But people carry out every idea, and a new, powerful policy administered without morality will amplify that immorality across a nation. The more powerful the instrument, the more the character of the person wielding it matters.


For that reason I believe the primary qualification for public office should be demonstrated moral maturity and wisdom, the old ideal of the philosopher-statesman. Credentials, connections, and money are secondary. A citizen evaluating me should evaluate my writing chiefly on that basis: not whether he agrees with every conclusion, but whether the reasoning is honest, the standard is stated, and the person behind it is trying to do what is right.


## A handbook, held open


Taken together, these essays are becoming something like a presidential handbook: an attempt to state, issue by issue, how a president should think and act, at home and abroad, if he takes righteousness and accountability seriously. I am running on that handbook. I want voters to know what I would do before they ever consider voting for me, and I want them able to hold me to it.


But a handbook written by a fallible man must stay open. Each position here is advocacy with reasons: a position I take and will defend, and also one I will revise if someone brings a better argument. An AI assistant has helped me draft, proving useful in testing each essay against the ERA frame and the words of Jesus, and in catching where an argument drifts. That help does not change the standard. Every conclusion here is mine, and I am responsible for it. People have done great harm by deciding “this is God’s way” and never checking whether it actually was. The safeguard is to keep checking.


## Carrying the weight


I should be honest about one more thing. This work is emotionally taxing. On a given day, I move from “this is too much; what am I thinking?” to “this is a good idea; that could actually help people,” and back again. Anyone who takes on a project of this size and takes it seriously will feel that swing.


A friend gave me good advice: step away from the screen, walk, get quiet with God, and empty your mind before returning to the work. The questions that race through the head during a campaign deserve that quiet. Am I doing this to win? To show that it can be done? To serve in some other role? My answer remains the one I gave publicly in August: this is a witness candidacy. I am running because someone who will stand behind these things should say them, and because a body of honest, organized moral reasoning about government is valuable whether or not its author ever holds office. I have asked people to write my name in only if they are confident I would win, and otherwise to vote for the best man on the ballot. That has not changed.


Carrying the weight well, I think, means three things. It means not letting the size of the task excuse carelessness in any single essay. It means holding myself to the standard I propose for others, since a campaign about righteousness and accountability that does not practice them refutes itself. And it means remembering that the outcome is not mine to control. My part is to think clearly, write honestly, and put the work where people can find it.


That is what the map is for, and that is what stands at its center.
