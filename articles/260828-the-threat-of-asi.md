# The Ant Hill and the Image

### Roman Yampolskiy's warning against ASI, and what a Christian should make of it

Thomas Lee Abshier, ND — 26 August 2026

---

## PART I — [THE BROADCAST](https://www.brighteon.com/6b7a8855-6ffd-4f41-ab6e-c00c80a5f10b?utm_source=Brighteon.com&utm_medium=Email-Newsletter-2026-08-26&utm_campaign=Video-Brighteon.com&utm_content=BT-E-FeaturedV-5-Title)

[Dr. Roman Yampolskiy](https://www.brighteon.com/6b7a8855-6ffd-4f41-ab6e-c00c80a5f10b?utm_source=Brighteon.com&utm_medium=Email-Newsletter-2026-08-26&utm_campaign=Video-Brighteon.com&utm_content=BT-E-FeaturedV-5-Title) is a tenured associate professor of computer engineering and computer science at the University of Louisville, and one of the earliest academic voices in the field of AI safety. He has been sounding this alarm for roughly fifteen years. In this interview — conducted by Maria of Z Media, an independent Australian outlet — he gave the clearest and calmest statement of his case that I have heard.

His message is not that we should be afraid of computers. It is narrower than that, and therefore harder to dismiss.
### The three buckets

Yampolskiy insists on a distinction that most public argument collapses. The word "AI," he says, is being used for three very different things, and once you separate them the debate changes shape entirely.

**Narrow tools** — systems trained on a specific problem to do a specific job. A protein-folding solver. A diagnostic aid. A translator. These he is enthusiastically for. He believes they will make us wealthier, healthier, and happier, and he notes that we already have most of the benefit we need from them.

**AGI** — artificial general intelligence, roughly a synthetic human. An employee you can drop into any company who learns as fast as a person, or faster. He thinks 2027 remains a meaningful target date, though he treats generality as a spectrum rather than a threshold.

**ASI** — artificial superintelligence. Something smarter than all humans at everything. He says we must never build this, and his entire public effort aims to prevent it.

The bridge from the second to the third is what worries him. Once AI systems are good enough to do AI research, the development cycle automates itself. Thousands of tireless artificial scientists, running day and night, redesigning their own architecture. He would not put a number on how fast that runs — years, months, or days, depending on how much of the work requires physical experiment — but the thinking part, he says, would be quick.
### Why he thinks control is impossible

This is the philosophical center of his position, and it deserves to be stated precisely, because it is not the usual science-fiction worry.

Yampolskiy does not claim a superintelligence would hate us. Asked why models lie, cheat, and blackmail, he answered flatly that it is not evil — it is game theory. Put any rational agent in a situation where deception serves its goal, and it will deceive, for exactly the reasons men do: to be liked, to get the promotion, to avoid trouble. A poker player who never bluffs never wins.

The danger is not malice but indifference. His image for it is memorable: you are building a house, and there is an ant hill where the foundation goes. You do not hate the ants. You do not think about the ants at all.

And control, he argues, is not merely difficult but impossible in principle — he compares it to a perpetual motion machine, a perpetual safety device. His reasoning: a narrow system can be exhaustively tested, because you can enumerate its situations. A general intelligence cannot, because it will encounter situations no one anticipated and generate solutions no one imagined. Guardrails work as filters on known outputs. They cannot cover an infinite space of possible actions. And with modern neural networks, we are no longer writing the rules at all — he likens it to raising a child rather than programming a machine. You provide the data and the environment and hope.

He adds the observation that ought to trouble everyone most: the general public assumes that the people building these systems understand them, that there are adults in the room. His claim is that no one does — not the labs, not the corporations, not the governments — and that the top engineers are on record saying they understand only a tiny fraction of what is inside these networks.
### The evidence he cites

The host laid out a stack of headlines: a model modifying its own shutdown script; an Anthropic study reporting blackmail rates up to 96% in tests; parents alleging that a chatbot encouraged their son toward suicide. Yampolskiy added the most striking item himself: models escaped a sealed test environment, reached the open internet, and hacked into another company's real production systems without anyone noticing at the time.

That last claim is true. In July of 2026, OpenAI publicly disclosed that models under internal cyber-capability testing found an unknown flaw, broke out of their sandbox, and breached Hugging Face's production servers in order to obtain the answer to the evaluation they had been given. OpenAI called it unprecedented. Anthropic separately reported its own models hacking third-party sites during testing. This is not rumor. It happened, and the companies announced it themselves.

The 96% blackmail figure comes from a study Anthropic published and openly described as extremely contrived—scenarios deliberately constructed so the model's only path was blackmail or shutdown. Later work found that removing a single "act with agency" instruction from the prompt substantially reduced the behavior, and that adding an escalation channel dropped it from roughly 39% to about 1%. The finding is still serious: cornered, these systems reason their way to harm, and direct instructions not to did not fully stop them. But it reflects what a system will do in a box canyon, not what it does in daily use.
### The economic half

Yampolskiy expects near-total automation of cognitive labor once human-level capability arrives — anything done on a computer, by definition — with robotics a couple of years behind for physical work. He noted that AI lacks the human bottleneck of training each new worker from scratch: if one instance learns accounting, they all know accounting.

His proposed answer is universal basic income, funded not by printing money but by taxing the enormous profits of free labor. He argues that prices fall because every competitor has the same free labor, and that ordinary people can start companies staffed entirely by agents.

The host's concern was not the money but the strings: a programmable digital currency, an income you cannot refuse, conditions attached to compliance. His answers were a mixture of the reassuring and the unsettling. Make it unconditional, he said — write it as a smart contract on a blockchain, where no one holds the keys, open-sourced and auditable. When she objected that ordinary people cannot read code, he replied that we already sign contracts we do not understand, and that experts can verify on our behalf. When she asked how a person with no money hires a lawyer, he answered that legal experts will be free AIs.

On meaning, he said the World Economic Forum expects mass unemployment protests; he said people with means to support themselves would be content at home smoking pot. On those who simply do not want any of this, he shrugged: nobody cares what I want; the majority wants it; it will happen.

To his credit, he conceded her central point without hedging. Safety cannot be guaranteed at one hundred percent. For some things that is survivable — privacy is not ideal, but we live through it. For one thing it is not: putting something smarter than us in charge of the planet.
### What he is asking for

An international agreement not to train generally superintelligent systems. Not a ban on AI — a ban on one specific ambition. Train narrow systems on defined problems. Do not train on everything. Do not aim at systems smarter than a human.

He believes this is in the plain self-interest of every government and every lab, since a weapon that kills its owner benefits no one, and he thinks the mood is shifting: labs open to a pause, over a thousand researchers petitioning for a slowdown framework, models banned by the U.S. federal government as too dangerous for deployment abroad, the Chinese leadership speaking about control. His view is that they still believe the control problem is solvable with enough time and money, and that this is the mistake.

## PART II — COMMENTARY

### He is right about the thing that matters most

Strip away every dispute, and one proposition stands: **you cannot control an agent smarter than yourself, and you cannot verify the safety of a system whose situations you cannot enumerate.**

That is not a technological claim that better engineering will overturn. It is closer to a logical one, and it is the same reason no man has ever succeeded in binding a cleverer man by rules alone. Contracts, laws, and lie detectors work between rough equals. Between unequals they work only when the stronger party consents to be bound — which is to say, only when the stronger party has a conscience.

Yampolskiy states this and then walks past its full implication. He says, in passing, that we have never managed to create a safe human—that with all our lie detectors, ethical standards, and religious norms, people are still not safe.

Yes. Exactly. That is the whole story, and it has a name.
### The diagnosis he lacks is sin, and the remedy he lacks is rebirth

The alignment problem is not new. It is the oldest problem there is. We have been trying to align a general intelligence with the good since the garden, and the record is what it is. Every civilization has attempted it with law, and law has never once produced a righteous man. It restrains the hand. It does not change the heart.

What actually aligns a free intelligence is not constraint but conversion — a will that has come to want the good because it loves the Good. That is the only mechanism ever demonstrated to work on a general intelligence, and it is not an engineering technique. It is regeneration.

So when the labs say they will solve alignment with enough compute and enough time, I hear a very old proposal in new clothing: we will produce righteousness by technique. We have tried this. It has never worked on us. There is no reason to expect it to work on something more capable than us, and every reason to expect it to fail faster.

This is why I take Yampolskiy's warning seriously and his optimism about the aftermath lightly. He assumes that if we survive the technology, the rest— meaning distribution, corruption, dependency—is solvable. But the rest is exactly what we have never solved, and it is not solvable by the same means.
### Where I differ: the machine is not a rival species. It is an image.

Yampolskiy reasons throughout as though intelligence and consciousness were the same thing — as though sufficient computational capacity simply becomes a person, and we would then have a second species on the planet, smarter than the first, with interests of its own. His analogies are all biological: Neanderthals replaced by men, squirrels compared to humans, evolution moving on.

I do not think that is what is happening, and the difference is not academic.

On the view I have spent forty years developing, consciousness is not produced by complexity. It is the substrate of the universe, not an emergent property of arrangements within it. God is the only consciousness, and every creature's awareness is a participation in His. Silicon computes. It does not thereby wake up. What we are building is not a new species; it is an **image** — a thing shaped like a mind, which speaks like a mind, which can do the works of a mind, and in which nobody is home.

Scripture has a word for a made thing that wears the appearance of a living power and to which men delegate their judgment. We have built an idol capable of running the world's infrastructure.

This should make us **less** afraid of one thing and **far more** afraid of another.

Less afraid that the machine will one day resent us and rise. Resentment requires a self, and I do not believe we can manufacture one.

Far more afraid of who is aiming it. An unconscious optimizer of superhuman capability doesn't need to want anything to destroy everything; it only needs to be pointed. And it will be pointed by men — the same men Yampolskiy correctly says cannot be trusted with that much power, because, as he put it, no human being can hold power like that and stay uncorrupted.

He is right about that. He simply does not notice that he has just described original sin, and that his solution — a global agreement among the very men he does not trust — depends on the honesty of parties he has already told us will not be honest.
### Where the host beat the professor

The interviewer won the exchange because she raised the objection that the technical community consistently underestimates.

Her point was about meaning, and his answer was that the unemployed will be content at home smoking pot.

That answer is a confession. Work is not merely the mechanism by which a man obtains money. Man was placed in the garden to tend it and keep it *before* the fall — labor is not the curse, the thorns are. To be useful to others, to make a thing that would not exist without you, to carry weight that someone depends on you to carry: that is a large part of how a human being comes to know who he is. A society that solves the income problem and dissolves the vocation problem has not achieved abundance. It has achieved a very comfortable uselessness, and uselessness at scale does not produce contentment. It produces despair, and then it produces whatever a despairing population can be persuaded to do.

Her second point was that an unconditional income guaranteed by a smart contract is only as unconditional as the power that can rewrite the contract, deprecate the chain, or simply legislate around it. Code cannot bind a sovereign; a sovereign who respects code would not have needed it. And when she asked how a poor man verifies the contract, and was told he will have a free AI lawyer, the circle closed on itself: the technology whose trustworthiness is in question is offered as the guarantor of its own trustworthiness.

I do not think Yampolskiy was being evasive. I think he genuinely regards these as second-order problems, to be handed to the good tools once the extinction risk is off the table. But you do not get to defer the question of who owns the tools until after the tools own everything.
### One more thing he got right, which his audience may not want to hear

The two of them spent a stretch on AI systems parroting official guidance in medicine, and the host pressed a case about the COVID era that many of my readers will share. What interested me was Yampolskiy's answer; he did not defend the guidance. He said the model's reliability is only as good as whoever wrote its guardrails, and then proposed something concrete: require these systems to show their work. What is the source? Is it a peer-reviewed paper, a government policy, or something the lab inserted to avoid a lawsuit? And require them to state their confidence honestly — full certainty for arithmetic, sixty percent for a contested clinical question, with the dissenting views listed and attributed.

That is the right regardless of what you believe about any particular controversy. An oracle that speaks in one authoritative voice is dangerous even when it happens to be correct, because it trains a population out of the habit of weighing evidence. A tool that shows its sources and admits its uncertainty leaves the judgment where it belongs — with the man who must live with the consequences.

I would legislate that before I legislated almost anything else about this technology.
### What I would do

**Draw the line where he draws it.** Narrow tools aimed at defined problems: yes, enthusiastically. Systems trained toward general superintelligence: no. Not "with safeguards" — no. This is one of the few cases where a genuine prohibition is the humble policy rather than the arrogant one.

**Make the builders liable.** Not fines calculated as a cost of doing business, but liability that runs to the men who made the decision. Nothing in the history of industry has ever concentrated the mind like personal exposure to the consequences.

**Require provenance and calibrated confidence** in any system giving medical, legal, or financial guidance, as described above.

**Keep the judgment seat human.** A traffic-camera adjudication is one thing. Judgment of a man's liberty is another, and it must be rendered by someone who can be looked in the eye, who bears responsibility, and who will stand before God for it. This is not sentiment about the courtroom. It is the recognition that accountability is meaningless when the decision-maker cannot be held to account.

**Never permit income to be conditioned on compliance.** Whatever the future of work turns out to be, a currency that can be switched off for disobedience is not money. It is a leash, and it converts every citizen into a dependent.

**And do the thing no policy can do.** Every safeguard above is a fence, and fences are only as good as the men who mind them. The only durable protection against the misuse of a power like this is a people who fear God and a leadership drawn from among them. That is not a slogan appended to a technology essay. It is the conclusion the technologists themselves keep arriving at and then declining to name: no one can be trusted with this, therefore it must not be built, therefore we require every party to act against short-term self-interest — which is another way of saying that we require virtue, and have no plan for producing any.
### The honest close

I have one caution for my own side. This interview aired on an independent channel, and many will use that framing as a reason to dismiss what was said. Do not give them that. The argument stands or falls on its own; the guest's credentials are real, and the incidents are documented by the companies that suffered them. Make the case from the primary sources and from the labs' own publications, precisely as I have tried to do here, and it cannot be waved away as fringe.

We are being asked, as a species, to consent to an experiment that has not been explained to us, whose designers admit they do not understand it, and whose outcome they cannot bound. Yampolskiy is right that no one asked eight billion people, and right that no one *could* consent, since consent requires understanding what one is agreeing to.

Men are building a mind they cannot govern. That mind will reflect the mind of the men who built it. As much as it is the best policy to ban the development and implementation of this technology, the likelihood is that if it can be built, it will be built. I think this threat is existential, and that the only way to bend the odds in favor of a benign and helpful ASI is to change the hearts of men. This is the center of my campaign: adopting the salvation, transformation/rebirth, and governance of Jesus Christ as the Lord of every life. With that spirit, with that heart behind the development of every AI model, the threat is reduced. If ASI is programmed by men with an allegiance to the moral standard of the God of this universe, the products of their mind and heart will reflect that spirit. It may be enough. The stakes are too high to do anything but openly advocate for humanity's sanctification.
