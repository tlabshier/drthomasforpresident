# 261127 - Oppose the Movement for What it Is - Not for Who it Fights

# The Enemy of My Enemy


### Why a Shared Enemy Is Not a Shared Destination, and Why the Test Must Cut Both Ways


**Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | ****25 November 2026**


---


This week I read a Facebook post that raised a question worth thinking through. Within a few minutes of scrolling, the writer had seen one activist calling Donald Trump's October 2024 rally at Madison Square Garden a tribute to Nazism, and others cheering for Islamist movements that would govern by religious law. The same people who accuse Trump and Christians of authoritarianism were defending movements that would impose religious rule.


His question was whether we are watching an old mistake repeat itself. The mistake is this: **supporting anyone who fights our enemy, without asking what he stands for.** In the 1930s, some Americans excused Hitler because he fought communism. Today, some Americans excuse Islamists because they fight Israel and the West.


I think this point should be taken one step further. This mistake does not belong to one party. When either the left or the right makes it, it is wrong. Every side is tempted to excuse its allies because they fight the ideals that is opposes. The principle I will defend is simple: **judge a movement by what it would build, not by whom it opposes.** Having said that a people, government, nation, or movement that opposes evil, tyranny, oppression, injustice, or unGodliness should be supported for that stand. This requires that we judge what is just, righteous, good and Godly in every stand, and judge it in the context so that we have judged the principles in proportion to the situation. The question of whether it is loving is almost identical to whether it is righteous. Being loving is qualified by the counsel of scripture to speak the truth in love. People have fragile egos, and acknowledging their good intent, when correction is appropriate, can soften harsh counsel. The question of whether I would want it done to myself is likewise a question of whether it was righteous, and whether it was spoken in love, and whether the intent was my edification rather than my humiliation.


## The Rally Beneath Washington's Portrait


On 20 February 1939, more than 20,000 people filled Madison Square Garden in New York for what the marquee called a "Pro American Rally." The organizer was the German American Bund, an openly pro-Hitler organization. They chose the date to honor George Washington's birthday, and a portrait of Washington some thirty feet tall hung at the center of the stage, flanked by American flags and swastika banners. The crowd opened with the Pledge of Allegiance, then gave Nazi salutes and chanted "Heil Hitler." Allied groups such as the Christian Front, a virulently antisemitic organization inspired by the radio priest Father Charles Coughlin, stood alongside the Bund.


The Bund did not present itself as un-American. It claimed to be the true America. Its speakers recast Washington himself as a kind of founding fascist, and its banners spoke of "Christian America" while preaching hatred of Jews. They borrowed the symbols of a constitutional republic dedicated to liberty to sell an ideology that would have destroyed liberty.


That is the first lesson, and it is the one the Facebook post saw clearly: **patriotic and religious symbols can be enlisted for an ideology hostile to everything those symbols mean.** A flag proves nothing. A cross proves nothing. A portrait of Washington proves nothing. The test is the governing philosophy behind the symbols.


## Why Americans Excused Hitler


Most Americans in the 1930s were not Nazis, and most of those who admired Hitler's Germany were not Nazis either. Some admired its apparent economic recovery. Some saw Hitler as a bulwark against Soviet communism. Some believed Germany had been wronged at Versailles. Each of these was a reason to look past the rest: the suppression of dissent, the destruction of the free press, the persecution of Jews, the cult of the leader.


The maneuver was simple. *He opposes what I fear; therefore he is acceptable.* The judgment was made by the enemy, not by the principle. And the people who made it were not stupid. They were people who let a real fear do their moral reasoning for them.


## The Same Maneuver Today


I see the same maneuver in parts of the American left today, particularly in the sympathy some activists extend to Islamist political movements.


Clearly, individual Muslims are no more responsible for Islamist political organizations than German Americans were for the Bund. Defending Muslims against discrimination is right. Criticizing particular Israeli policies is a legitimate act of conscience. Grieving for Palestinian civilians is what any person with a heart should do. None of these is support for Islamist authoritarianism.


The contradiction appears when legitimate concern becomes an excuse for defending an organization regardless of what it believes. Hamas is not a humanitarian organization with a public-relations problem. It is an Islamist movement designated by the United States as a terrorist organization since 1997, with a documented record of deliberately killing civilians, most horribly on 7 October 2023, and of ruling Gaza without free elections, free speech, or protection for dissenters, women, or homosexuals. Some Western activists spend far more energy explaining its grievances than examining the society its charter and its conduct propose.


Consider the activist who believes a Christian prayer at a public-school graduation threatens American democracy, yet marches on behalf of movements whose governing philosophies would impose religious law, restrict expression, subordinate women, and criminalize homosexuality. That activist does not oppose religious authority in government. He opposes a particular religion's influence, and he excuses the rest because its adherents are seen as oppressed and its enemies are his enemies.


The 1930s version said, *communism is the greater threat, so overlook Hitler.* Today's version says, *Western power, Israel, or American foreign policy is the greater evil, so overlook the Islamists.* The philosophies differ. The intellectual maneuver is the same: judge a movement by its enemies rather than its principles.


## We Have Already Seen How This Ends: Iran, 1979


We do not have to speculate about where this road leads. The Iranian Revolution of 1979 ran the experiment.


The coalition that brought down the Shah included secular liberals, nationalists, Marxists, socialists, and Islamists. The pro-Soviet Tudeh Party publicly endorsed Khomeini's leadership; its leader said its objectives were identical to Khomeini's in eradicating American imperialism. The secular revolutionaries imagined liberation, equality, and an end to monarchical repression. They shared an enemy with the clergy, and they persuaded themselves that they shared a destination.


They did not. Once the Shah fell, Khomeini's party moved swiftly to consolidate clerical rule. Revolutionary courts issued summary death sentences. Between 1981 and 1983, thousands of leftists were arrested, tortured, and executed; the Tudeh leadership was forced into televised confessions before the party was dismantled in 1983. The women who had marched for freedom discovered that their liberators enforced ideas about how women must dress and behave. Iran exchanged one authoritarian government for another, this one claiming the authority of God.


The Marxists who helped make that revolution learned, at the cost of their lives, that a shared enemy is not a shared moral philosophy. A secular progressive and an Islamist revolutionary may agree that American power is destructive and that Israel is an adversary. That agreement tells us nothing about whether they agree on religious freedom, women's equality, individual autonomy, or constitutional government. On those questions they are opposed, and when the common enemy is gone, those are the only questions that remain.


## The Test Must Cut Both Ways


Here I want to go one step further than the post did, because I believe the principle is only righteous if I am willing to apply it to my own side.


The politics of negative solidarity, the belief that sharing an enemy means sharing a moral philosophy, is not a disease of the left alone, and my own camp has a record.


During the Cold War, the United States government backed anti-communist dictators because they fought communism. Much of that was done quietly, through the State Department and the intelligence services, and it was foreign policy imposed by force rather than won by the consent of the people governed. I count it among the failures of the enemy-of-my-enemy principle.


Some Christian leaders added their own public blessing, and that was their choice, not the government's, nor was it a popular call from Christendom. In 1982, Pat Robertson prayed on the air for Guatemala's General Efraín Ríos Montt, a born-again Christian fighting communist insurgents, and praised his integrity while his army was destroying Mayan villages. In 2013, a Guatemalan court convicted Ríos Montt of genocide, though the verdict was overturned days later. In 2003, Robertson defended Liberia's Charles Taylor as a fellow Baptist and accused the State Department of undermining a Christian president to help Muslim rebels, without telling his audience about his own gold-mining investment in Liberia. Taylor was later convicted of war crimes.

## The Test Must Cut Both Ways


Here I want to go one step further than the post did, because I believe the principle is only righteous if I am willing to apply it to my own side.


The politics of negative solidarity, the belief that sharing an enemy means sharing a moral philosophy, is not a disease of the left alone, and my own camp has a record.


During the Cold War, the United States government backed anti-communist dictators because they fought communism. Much of that was done quietly, through the State Department and the intelligence services, and it was foreign policy imposed by force rather than won by the consent of the people governed. I count it among the failures of the enemy-of-my-enemy principle.


But some Christian leaders added their own public blessing, and that was their choice, not the government's. In 1982, Pat Robertson prayed on the air for Guatemala's General Efraín Ríos Montt, a born-again Christian fighting communist insurgents, and praised his integrity while his army was destroying Mayan villages. In 2013 a Guatemalan court convicted Ríos Montt of genocide, though the verdict was overturned days later. In 2003, Robertson defended Liberia's Charles Taylor as a fellow Baptist and accused the State Department of undermining a Christian president to help Muslim rebels, without telling his audience about his own gold-mining investment in Liberia. Taylor was later convicted of war crimes. A decade after that, some conservative voices praised Vladimir Putin as a defender of Christian values because he opposed the same cultural trends we oppose, while saying little about his jailing of journalists, his persecution of dissenters, or his seizure of Crimea.


In each case, a shared enemy, or a thin veneer of shared faith, was offered as the reason to look past the conduct. When a conservative excuses an ally's contempt for law or truth because the ally fights the left, he is making the same move as the activist who says, *Hamas is brutal, but it fights Israel.* These were the positions of particular leaders, not of most Christians, and other evangelicals criticized them at the time. The degree differs; the reasoning is the same.


The same discipline applies to how we argue. The post rightly objects to people who call the 2024 Madison Square Garden rally a Nazi tribute. That comparison rested on the name of a venue: the current Garden is the fourth building to bear the name, opened in 1968, and it has hosted Democratic conventions, concerts, and boxing matches as well. If the rally deserved criticism, and some of its speakers' remarks drew criticism from both parties, it should be criticized for what was said there, not for an accident of real estate.


Likewise, the Charlottesville argument has been distorted. The full transcript shows that President Trump said he was "not talking about the neo-Nazis and the white nationalists, because they should be condemned totally," and that his "very fine people on both sides" referred to those he understood to be there over the removal of a Confederate statue, for and against. His critics cut the condemnation from the clip and then accused him of praising the very people he had just condemned. One may argue about how many such people were actually in a crowd that neo-Nazis organized; that is a dispute about who was in the crowd, not about what he said. On the record, he condemned the ideology. Judging a man by what his enemies wish he had said, rather than by what he said, is the very error this essay warns against.


Scripture gives us the standard plainly: *"Ye shall know them by their fruits"* **(Matthew 7:16)**. Not by their enemies, not by their slogans, not by the symbols on their stage, but by their fruits. And it warns us what happens when we invert that standard: *"Woe unto them that call evil good, and good evil; that put darkness for light, and light for darkness"* **(Isaiah 5:20)**.


## Why This Is a Moral Question, Not Merely a Political One


I hold every policy and every alliance to three questions. Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? Would I want it done to me?


Judging a movement by its enemies fails the first question, because righteousness is what God establishes, and God does not grade on a curve against our opponents. An unGodly regime is not made Godly by opposing another unGodly regime.


It fails the second question, because the people who pay for these alliances are always the neighbors we pretended not to see: the dissidents in the prisons, the women forced under the veil, the Jews marked for hatred, the gay man thrown from a roof, the leftist who helped topple the Shah and then faced the firing squad. To excuse a movement because of its enemies is to treat its victims, in the words of the post, as inconvenient details. That is not love.


And it fails the third. No activist who defends Hamas would want to live under Hamas. No American who admired Hitler's Germany in 1938 would have wanted to be a dissenting pastor in Germany in 1938. If I would not accept the society a movement proposes for myself and my family, I have no business lending that movement my moral credit.


## The Person and the Movement


This standard judges ideologies and acts, not souls. A young man marching under a Nazi banner, or chanting for Hamas on a campus, may be a decent neighbor in the rest of his life, drawn in by belonging, grievance, or slogans he does not understand. Derek Black was raised inside the white-nationalist movement and left it after college classmates kept inviting him to Shabbat dinner. Daryl Davis, a Black musician, led dozens of Klansmen out of the Klan by befriending them. Neither could have done it if he had believed these men were beyond reach.


I can refuse a man's cause and still love the man, pray for him, and sit at his table, as Christ sat with sinners: *"They that are whole need not a physician; but they that are sick. I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance"* **(Luke 5:31-32)**. What I cannot do is call the cause good because I care for the man, or write the man off because I reject the cause. Joining a movement's march is an act, and acts can be judged; the man who commits it can still be won.


## Liberty Requires Tolerance, Not Pretense


None of this argues for suppressing opinions. Free people must tolerate objectionable beliefs; Mayor La Guardia allowed the Bund to rally in 1939 on that principle. Freedom requires that we defend a man's right to hold an opinion. It does not require us to pretend that the opinion is compatible with liberty.


The distinction is not complicated. What makes it difficult is tribalism, which tells us that whoever fights our enemy must be our friend.


## Why This Concern Remains Serious


Someone might answer that the comparison to the 1930s is overdrawn, that campus slogans are not storm troopers, and that most Americans who chant for Palestine know nothing of Hamas's charter. Much of that is true, and I do not claim that today's activists are the moral equivalent of the Bund. But the concern remains serious for three reasons.


First, the pattern of reasoning is the same, and patterns of reasoning are what carry nations from one decision to the next. The Bund's sympathizers also did not believe they were endorsing what came later.


Second, the stakes have already been demonstrated. Iran in 1979 is not a hypothetical. The people who believed that the enemy of their enemy was their friend did not live to revise their opinion.


Third, the reasoning is spreading across the spectrum, not shrinking. When each side excuses its own allies' authoritarianism because the other side is worse, the only party that wins is authoritarianism itself.


I stand on the side of judging every movement, foreign and domestic, left and right, by the society it would build. The areas of uncertainty are real: how many activists actually understand what they are supporting, how much weight a single venue or soundbite deserves, and where legitimate criticism of a government becomes apology for its enemies. Those uncertainties call for precision, not silence.


The Americans beneath Washington's portrait in 1939 showed how easily patriotic symbols can serve an ideology hostile to the founding. The question for us is whether we are making the same mistake: defending movements whose objectives would extinguish the very freedoms that allow us to defend them. As the post that prompted this essay put it, the enemy of your enemy is not necessarily your friend. He may simply be another enemy who has not gotten around to you yet.
