# 261024 - Data Center Solutions Power and Cooling

# Power Before Servers


## A righteous path through the data center boom


*Dr. Thomas Lee Abshier, ND — 24 October 2026*


---


## The situation


America is in the midst of the largest private construction program in its history. Roughly $800 billion is being invested in AI infrastructure in the United States this year, with more than a trillion expected next year. Data centers now account for close to a quarter of new nonresidential construction. I do not oppose this investment. Computing will shape this century’s economy, and a nation that cannot build it will be governed by those who can.


But the way it is being built is wrong. Facilities that draw as much power as a city are being built next to farms and neighborhoods, often in less affluent rural areas where land is cheap. The people who live there did not ask for them and are bearing their costs:


- **Electricity bills.** Grid upgrades to serve these loads are often charged to residential ratepayers. In the Mid-Atlantic grid, the independent market monitor attributed most of a dramatic rise in capacity prices to data center demand.

- **Water.** Evaporative cooling for a single gigawatt-scale campus can consume millions of gallons a day, and small towns feel it in lower water pressure and higher rates.

- **Noise.** Cooling equipment and generators produce a constant, low-frequency hum that passes through walls, breaks sleep, and can comply with ordinary noise permits while still keeping families awake.

- **Land.** Families are being pressured to sell, and eminent domain is being used to run private power lines across private land.

- **Power quality.** Independent sensor data indicate that data centers are degrading the quality of electricity delivered to homes for miles around. Whether this harms human health remains unproven, but it is undisputed that it stresses appliances and the grid.


The political response has been to override objections rather than answer them, calling opposition disloyal or foreign-inspired. Our rivals may exploit some opposition; that does not make a family’s sleepless nights or doubled power bill any less real. Those who suffer the cost deserve an answer, not an accusation.


---


## The moral test


Every policy I propose is measured by three questions, in order:


- **Is it righteous?** Does it accord with what God has established as just: honest dealing, respect for property, and refusal to make one man pay for another’s gain without his consent?

- **If so, is it loving?** Would my neighbor, standing in the public square, recognize this act as care for him?

- **Would I want it done to me?** Not to my momentary appetite, but to the self committed to righteousness: would I accept this if the hum were in my bedroom, the bill in my mailbox, the line across my pasture?


Applied to the present buildout, the answer is plain. Placing a city-sized industrial load beside a family, charging him for the grid upgrades it needs, taking his land for its power line, and filling his nights with noise fails all three. No one would choose it for himself. The fact that the enterprise is profitable and strategically important does not change the moral character of imposing its costs on people who never consented. “Thou shalt not covet thy neighbor’s house” binds corporations as much as individuals, and the commandment to love one’s neighbor as oneself is not suspended for national strategy.


The good news is that the righteous path is also the practical one.


---


## The principle: displacement, not prohibition


History shows that protest alone rarely ends harmful practices. Leaded gasoline survived fifty years of warnings and was removed only when the catalytic converter made unleaded fuel necessary and profitable. Chlorofluorocarbons were phased out once replacements were patented. The lesson is that the most reliable way to stop a harmful practice is to make a better one cheaper.


The better practice here is obvious: **put gigawatt-scale computing where no one lives, and let it make its own power.** Cold, remote places cut cooling costs. Off-grid generation keeps residential ratepayers out of it entirely. Air cooling instead of evaporative cooling saves the water. Distance removes the noise. A separate electrical system keeps power-quality problems inside the fence. And these facilities’ enormous, steady demand is exactly the customer America’s most promising energy technology, the thorium molten salt reactor, has lacked for fifty years.


---


## What I would do


### 1. The one who causes the cost pays the cost


Any new load above 100 megawatts should either bring its own generation or pay the full cost of the grid capacity, transmission, and upgrades it requires, through rates that cannot be shifted onto residential customers. This is not a penalty. It is the basic justice of paying for what you use.


### 2. No eminent domain for private computing


The power to take a family’s land against their will exists for genuinely public works. A private company’s power line to a private server farm is not one. I would oppose federal use, authorization, or financing of eminent domain for transmission that primarily serves a private data center, and I would encourage states to adopt the same rule.


### 3. Measure noise as people experience it


Federal permits, loans, and land leases for large data centers should require enforceable night-time limits on low-frequency noise, measured at the nearest home with C-weighted and low-frequency band measurements rather than only the A-weighted scale that discounts the hum people actually hear. Limit generator testing to daytime hours. These are inexpensive conditions that should have been standard from the start.


### 4. Open the remote frontier, with the public as a shareholder


Federal lands and states like Alaska that want this industry should offer remote sites for large computing campuses, conditioned on off-grid power, air cooling, and a real return to the public. Alaska already has the model in its Permanent Fund, which returns resource revenue to every resident, and in its Native corporations, which share subsurface revenues. A lease that pays a percentage of gross receipts into a public trust turns the host community from a victim into a partner. A lease that rents Arctic land at pasture rates repeats the error. Heavy public opposition to Alaska’s first proposed North Slope campus warns that remoteness alone does not secure consent; fair terms do.


### 5. Build the thorium fuel cycle now


The thorium molten salt reactor was demonstrated at Oak Ridge in the 1960s and set aside because it did not make bomb material. It runs at atmospheric pressure; it cannot melt down in the ordinary sense because its fuel is already liquid; it shuts down by draining into passive tanks; it produces far less long-lived waste; and it can be cooled by air rather than water, making it ideal for remote sites. China now operates the world’s only thorium molten salt reactor and has a public schedule for larger units around 2030 and 2035.


America’s task is not to subsidize corporations but to remove the obstacles only government can remove and to rebuild capabilities only our national laboratories hold:


- **Restore domestic lithium-7 enrichment.** America has not produced enriched lithium since 1963 and now depends on China and Russia for a material every molten salt reactor needs.

- **Fund the chemistry.** Direct the national laboratories to complete the fuel-salt processing, protactinium handling, and materials qualification that were left unfinished in 1973.

- **Build a thorium test reactor** under Department of Energy authority, so that years of operating data begin accumulating this decade.

- **Write the rules.** Direct the Nuclear Regulatory Commission to create a licensing pathway for liquid-fueled reactors, with firm deadlines, so that private capital, including the computing companies that need the power, knows the path to a commercial plant.


The data center companies are willing to pay almost any price for firm power. Let them pay for the first commercial thorium reactors, sited beside their own campuses in remote places. The public gains an energy technology that can eventually power homes and industry at low cost; the companies gain the power they need; and no family pays with their sleep.


### 6. Find out the truth about the unknowns


Fund independent power-quality measurements, including the higher-frequency band utilities do not monitor, in homes near data centers, and a properly blinded study of whether filtering that distortion improves residents’ sleep and symptoms. If it harms people, we should know. If it doesn’t, families shouldn’t spend money on remedies that cannot help them. Either way, truth serves the neighbor.


---


## Why this matters


My platform rests on The Vision for a New ERA: Energy, Righteousness, and Accountability. Abundant energy underpins prosperity; righteousness measures every policy; accountability means those who cause harm answer for it.


The data center boom touches all three. It is the greatest new demand for energy in our lifetimes. We are pursuing it in a way that makes ordinary people bear costs they never chose. And if we are wise, it offers the first real opportunity in half a century to build the clean, safe, abundant energy source we abandoned. We can continue imposing these facilities on unwilling neighbors and buy thorium reactors from China in the 2040s, or we can move the servers to where they belong and build the reactors ourselves.


I hold this position because I believe it is right, and I hold it open to a better argument. If someone shows me a path that protects the neighbor more fully and builds American energy more surely, I will take it.
