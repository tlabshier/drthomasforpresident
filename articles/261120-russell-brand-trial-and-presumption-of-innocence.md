# 261120 - Russell Brand Trial and Presumption of Innocence

# The Verdict, the Gag, and the Presumption of Innocence:


### What the Russell Brand Case Asks of a Free People


**By Thomas Lee Abshier, ND** | 20 *November 2026*


---


On November 20, [Tucker Carlson interviewed Megyn Kelly about Russell Brand's conviction and imprisonment](https://tuckercarlson.com/tucker-show-megyn-kelly-112026-russell-brand). Kelly is an attorney, and she walked through the evidence against him count by count. She concluded the case was a hit job by political enemies. I watched the interview, then went to the court reporting, the judge's sentencing remarks, the government statements, and the critics of the verdict, to see how much of what she said holds up.


Here is where I stand. I stand on the side of the presumption of innocence, and of a convicted man's right to say out loud that he is innocent. I do not know whether Russell Brand raped the woman the jury says he raped. Neither does anyone else who was not in that room seventeen years ago. What I do know is that the process that sent him to prison, and what the British government is doing to him now that he is there, falls short of what justice requires of a free people. The verdict may be right. The process is not righteous, and the gag is plainly wrong.


The uncertainties are real. The jury heard evidence the public did not. The complainant's side offered contemporaneous testimony. Brand's own conduct in those years was unGodly by his own admission. None of that changes the thesis. A man can be a sinner, a jury can be sincere, and the system can still be tilted.


## What the Record Shows


On November 5, 2026, a jury at Southwark Crown Court in London convicted Brand of one count of rape and three counts of sexual assault involving four women. He was acquitted of two other counts of rape and one count of attempted indecent assault. He had stood trial on seven charges relating to six women, with the alleged offenses dating from 1999 to 2009. On November 9, Mr Justice Joel Bennathan sentenced him to ten years and six months: nine years for the rape, with the sexual assault sentences added to it.


The rape count concerns a woman who was 21 when she met Brand at one of his shows in 2009. By her own testimony, she willingly took part in a sexual encounter with him backstage, then went to his Hampstead home a few days later for sex. Her account is that the sex began with her consent and that she said no to one act, which he did anyway, pushing her down so she struggled to breathe. Brand testified that he remembered the encounter and that the act was consensual. Agreed facts read to the jury showed that he had saved her in his phone under a crude nickname marking her as a casual encounter of a particular kind.


No forensic evidence and no CCTV were presented, according to one critic of the verdict. The case rested on testimony. Some of that testimony was on her side, and it was not trivial. Friends testified that she told them at the time that the act was forced and that it hurt her badly. Another said Brand promised to be gentle and then went ahead without her consent. Her mother recalled hearing her describe screaming in pain while telling him to stop, and her father told her then that it was rape. She did not go to the police, and her friends said it was because she did not think anyone would believe her. She came forward after the 2023 documentary.


In sentencing, the judge called Brand a sexual predator and cited his "staggering arrogance." He added time to the starting point because Brand had made the woman clean the sheets, and took a year off for good character, Brand's charitable work, and the roughly seventeen years since the last offense.


That is the record, stated as fairly as I can state it. Now to what it means.


## What Brand Admits


Before I criticize the process, I want to say plainly what this website exists to say: sin is sin, and it ought to be named.


On the stand, Brand admitted that he had used his fame to gain sexual access to women. He called his years of conduct "beyond reckless philandering" and said that using his fame that way was wrong. He has called his relationship with a 16-year-old girl, when he was 30, "exploitative," and said he gave "barely any" consideration to how his sexual conduct affected other people.


That is a confession of an unGodly life. It is not a confession of rape, and the difference matters both in law and before God. But nobody should defend Brand by pretending his past was innocent. The Scripture is direct: *"Flee fornication. **Every sin that a man doeth is without the body; but he that committeth fornication sinneth against his own body"* **(1 Corinthians 6:18)**. A culture that celebrated a man for boasting about this on television, as British broadcasting did for years, shares the guilt for what that culture produced. The women who came out of it were hurt; whatever happened in any particular room, they were real people harmed by a real sin.


Brand says he has repented and been baptized. I take him at his word on that, because only God sees the heart, and because the Gospel teaches that repentance is real. But repentance does not erase consequences, and if he committed a crime, the law has a just claim on him. This essay does not ask whether Brand was a good man in 2009. He was not. The question is whether the state proved a crime by a standard a free people should accept, and whether it is treating him justly now.


## One Witness, Seventeen Years


The law of Moses set a standard of proof that still deserves our respect: *"One witness shall not rise up against a man for any iniquity, or for any sin, in any sin that he sinneth: at the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall the matter be established"* **(Deuteronomy 19:15)**.


English law once reflected that principle. Judges were required to warn juries of the danger of convicting on the uncorroborated word of a complainant in a sexual case. Parliament abolished the mandatory warning in 1994. There were reasons for that: sexual assault usually happens with no one else present, and the old warning was often used to treat women's testimony as inherently suspect.


In fairness, I have to acknowledge that Scripture itself recognizes this problem. In the case of the woman assaulted in the field, where *"the damsel cried, and there was none to save her"* **(Deuteronomy 22:27)**, the law convicts the man on the strength of what the circumstances show, even with no second eyewitness. God does not require a rape victim to produce a bystander. The two-witness rule was never meant to make an assault in a private room unpunishable.


So the question is not whether a rape conviction can ever rest on one person's account. It can. The question is what should stand in for the second witness. In the field, it was the place and the cry. In Brand's case, it was the testimony of friends and parents about what the woman told them afterward. That evidence is real. It is still her own account, repeated by people who love her, about one act within an encounter that both parties agree was otherwise consensual, seventeen years before trial. A juror who weighed all that and found it enough was not acting in bad faith. A juror who weighed it and found reasonable doubt would not have been acting in bad faith either. When the margin is that narrow, and the penalty is nine years, the law ought to lean hard toward the presumption of innocence. In this case, the whole atmosphere leaned the other way.


The jury showed discernment: it acquitted on three of the seven counts, including a rape charge where it believed one part of a woman's account and rejected another. That is not the behavior of a mob. But a jury's discernment cannot repair what was done to the evidence before the jury ever saw it, and that is my next concern.


## Trial by Documentary


The allegations first reached the public in September 2023, through a joint investigation by The Times, The Sunday Times, and Channel 4's *Dispatches*. The program told the stories of several women under pseudonyms, and its own promotion disclosed that, in some cases, actors had been used. In November 2026, days after the sentencing, Netflix released *Russell Brand: Open Secret*, featuring the same lead journalists and again using actors to deliver some of the women's testimony.


I understand why a broadcaster protects an accuser's identity. Shadow and voice distortion do that. An actor does something more. He or she performs grief, hesitation, and tears, and the viewer's instincts read the performance as a witness. We judge most of whether someone is telling the truth from the face and voice. Putting a professional performer in that seat persuades the audience through a channel the audience does not know it is using. Disclosing it in small print does not undo the effect.


The deeper problem is what the broadcast did to the trial that followed. The complainants in the criminal case came forward after the 2023 program, not before. The complainant on the rape count testified that she had watched it. Brand's lawyers argued that the broadcast poisoned the well, and that afterward incidents were "reinvented or reimagined." Memory is not a recording. Memory, seventeen years old, revisited after a national broadcast portraying the accused as a predator, is exactly the kind of evidence the law should handle with the most care. Two decades of silence followed by a media event, then police reports, then a trial, is the sequence that produces false convictions when it produces any. I do not say it produced one here. I say the risk was built into the process, and nobody in a position of authority seemed troubled by it.


The *Dispatches* program and this trial were also, as far as public reporting shows, about different women. The program's four accounts covered 2006 to 2013. The trial counts covered 1999 to 2009. The woman the 2023 program called "Alice," who was 16 at the time, has since waived her anonymity. She said that prosecutors chose not to include her case in the first trial, and that she does not know whether she has the capacity to give years more of her life to further proceedings. Her account may be entirely true. But the most-publicized allegations from the program that started all this never went before a jury.


## Anonymity in One Direction


Under British law, a complainant in a sexual offense case receives lifelong anonymity. The accused receives none. From the day of the accusation, his name, his face, and the charge are public.


Megyn Kelly made the strongest point on this in the interview, drawing on the Duke lacrosse case. In 2006, three young men were falsely accused of rape. Because reporters learned the accuser's identity, people who knew her could come forward, and her credibility could be tested against her history. In Britain, only the defense team gets to test a complainant's credibility. The public does not, and the stranger who might recognize a face and say "she accused me too" never sees it. According to Kelly, Brand's investigators found that one complainant, on a count where he was acquitted, had previously accused other men of rape. I couldn't confirm that in the trial reporting, but if it is true, they found it despite the anonymity, not because of it.


I am not arguing for exposing victims. A woman who has been raped deserves protection from a public that would shame her. But justice that protects one side's name and destroys the other's before any verdict is not balanced. If anonymity is right for the accuser until the truth is established, it is right for the accused until the truth is established. Parity is what fairness looks like.


## Punished for Saying "I Am Not Guilty"


This part of the case needs no weighing of credibility, because the facts are not in dispute.


On November 15, Brand's wife Laura read a short message from him on their YouTube program. In it, he said he was not guilty of the offenses for which he had been jailed. He said Christ was with those who choose Him, that the prison staff was kind, that he was in no position to judge his fellow inmates, and that people should pray for his family. That was all.


The next day, the Prime Minister's spokesman called the statement "clearly appalling" and announced an urgent investigation. The spokesman said prisoners are banned from making statements that cause distress to victims, and that they cannot get around that by having someone else read their words. Justice Secretary Alex Norris called the broadcast completely unacceptable. LBC reported that Brand now faces the possibility of having all his communications monitored and of being banned from speaking to his wife.


Consider what is being punished. Not a threat. Not an attack on any victim by name. Not a revelation of anyone's identity. A man said he is innocent, mentioned Christ, and asked for prayer for his wife and children.


Every convicted person in Britain retains the right to appeal, and the country maintains a Criminal Cases Review Commission precisely because juries sometimes convict the innocent. A prisoner who maintains his innocence is doing what the innocent ones do, and some of them turn out to be right. A rule that treats the bare statement "I am not guilty" as a punishable injury to victims has decided that a jury's verdict may not be publicly questioned, even by the man it convicted. That is not a protection of victims. It prohibits dissent from the state's judgment, enforced by threatening a man's contact with his wife.


The Scripture warns: *"Thou shalt not follow a multitude to do evil; neither shalt thou speak in a cause to decline after many to wrest judgment"* **(Exodus 23:2)**. Whatever Brand did in 2009, what the government is doing in 2026 is a public wrong, and it is one I would want every American to see clearly, because the same impulse is present here.


## The State and the Platforms


The pattern did not begin with the prison message. In September 2023, within days of the broadcast and before any police charge, Dame Caroline Dinenage, then chair of the House of Commons Culture, Media and Sport Committee, wrote to Rumble and other platforms asking whether they would follow YouTube in cutting off Brand's income. YouTube had already demonetized him. Rumble refused and published its reply calling the letter disturbing.


After the conviction, Meta removed his Instagram and Facebook accounts under its policy on convicted sex offenders. A private company may keep its own rules. But LBC also reported that ministers held fresh talks with YouTube that same week, pressing it to take his accounts down, with a source pointing to Meta as the example to follow. The Prime Minister said, "We've heard enough from him."


When elected officials lean on private platforms to silence a specific critic, the platforms become instruments of the state, and the citizen has no court in which to answer. Our own Supreme Court has wrestled with this in the government-pressure cases. The principle should not be hard. If the law has not silenced a man, ministers should not do it by phone call.


## Why the Concern of Political Targeting Remains Plausible


Underneath the particulars, the question many people are really asking is this: was Russell Brand prosecuted because of what he did in 2009, or because of what he said after 2020?


I cannot prove the second. The timing evidence cuts both ways. The Sunday Times said its investigation began in 2019, before Brand's most outspoken years on vaccine mandates, government overreach, and censorship. A jury of twelve ordinary people, not ministers, returned a verdict and acquitted on three counts. If this were a pure political frame, it was an unusually discriminating one.


Even so, the concern is not paranoia, and I will say why it remains plausible.


First, the state's response to Brand's speech is disproportionate to anything a neutral system would do. Ordinary prisoners who protest their innocence do not draw statements from Downing Street. When a government spends that much attention on one man's words, it is fair to ask what about his words frightens it.


Second, the British cultural establishment celebrated Brand for years while his politics matched theirs. He wrote for left-leaning publications, guest-edited the New Statesman, and was a fixture on the BBC and Channel 4 while his conduct with women was, by the Netflix film's own title, an "open secret." The establishment's moral seriousness about that conduct arrived after his politics changed. That does not make the accusations false. It does make the zeal suspect.


Third, Britain has shown that its institutions can look away from sexual abuse for years when pursuing it is politically uncomfortable. The national audit of grooming gangs led by Baroness Casey in 2025 found that officials had avoided confronting the ethnicity of perpetrators for fear of being accused of racism, while thousands of girls were abused. A justice system that can be that selective in one direction can be selective in another.


Fourth, Britain is now arresting citizens in large numbers over online speech. The Times, drawing on police data, reported that roughly 12,000 people a year are arrested under laws against offensive or menacing communications. A government willing to police speech that broadly will find it natural to police the speech of a prisoner who embarrasses it.


None of this proves that Brand was framed. Taken together, it explains why so many reasonable people, not only his fans, feel that something besides justice is at work.


## Applying the Test


On this site, I ask three questions of any public act. Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? Would I want it done to me?


**Is it righteous?** Punishing rape is righteous. The law of God treats rape as a grave crime, and a woman who was violated deserves justice even when there was no other witness. But convicting a man on a seventeen-year-old account with no physical evidence, after a media campaign that used performers to portray his accusers, sits at the edge of what can be called proof beyond reasonable doubt. Punishing a prisoner for saying he is innocent is not righteous. It sets the state's verdict beyond question, and no human verdict deserves that.


**Is it loving?** Love for the women means believing that harm can be real, taking their accounts seriously, and protecting them from shame. Love for the accused means not destroying his name before the truth is known, giving him every means to test the evidence, and letting him maintain his innocence. A system that offers love to only one side is not loving. It is partisan.


**Would I want it done to me?** Put any of our sons in Brand's position. Picture a young man who has a consensual encounter with a woman, and twenty years later she comes forward anonymously, after a national broadcast about him, describing one act within that encounter as forced. He cannot learn who else she may have accused. His name is public from day one. The jury hears her account and the accounts of the friends she told. He goes to prison for nine years. When he says from his cell, "I am innocent; pray for my wife," the government threatens to cut him off from his wife. Not one of us would want that done to our son, even a son who had lived badly. That is the test, and the process fails it.


## What I Would Advocate


As a candidate, my role is leadership by voice. I would not impose these views on a foreign country, and America has its own failures to fix. But I would advocate these principles here at home, and would urge our allies to honor them:


 	- **The right to proclaim one's innocence is absolute.** No American prisoner should be punished for saying he did not commit the crime he was convicted of. Our First Amendment protects this, and we should say plainly that Britain's rule as applied here is a wrong, not a cultural difference.

 	- **Anonymity should apply equally.** Where a jurisdiction grants anonymity to complainants in sexual cases, it should extend the same protection to the accused until conviction.

 	- **Historic allegations call for a careful jury instruction.** When an accusation is decades old and was first made after media publicity, juries should be told explicitly about the risk of reconstructed memory. That should not be a warning against believing women. It should warn about time and contamination, which affect everyone's memory.

 	- **Government must keep its hands off the platforms.** No official should press a private company to silence a particular citizen. If speech is unlawful, let a court say so openly.

 	- **Journalism that dramatizes testimony owes its audience full disclosure.** When a documentary uses performers to deliver an accuser's words, it should make that impossible to miss, and it should tell the audience whenever a witness has given inconsistent accounts. These are standards of honesty, not law. I would urge them from every platform I have.

 	- **Brand lives in the United States.** He is a British citizen, convicted under British law, and he has no claim to American protection against that verdict. But our State Department can and should say, as a matter of principle, that a government punishing a prisoner's declaration of innocence and his profession of faith is acting against the freedoms our two countries claim to share.


## Where the Interview Overstated or Erred


I share most of Megyn Kelly's concerns, but holding the evidence to a standard means holding my allies to it too.


 	- She said the rape complainant went seventeen years without calling it rape. Reporting from the trial shows that she told friends and her parents at the time that the act was forced, and that her father told her then that it was rape. That is the prosecution's strongest evidence, and it should be faced, not omitted.

 	- She attributed one of the harshest government statements to "Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband." The statements I could verify came from the Prime Minister's spokesman and from Justice Secretary Alex Norris.

 	- She described Caroline Dinenage as a government minister. In 2023, Dinenage chaired a parliamentary committee, not a ministry. Her letter was still an elected official pressing platforms, and still wrong.

 	- She quoted the complainant as telling a friend she was "probably just as responsible as he was." I could not find that remark in the trial reporting I examined. Friends did testify that the complainant blamed herself, which a reasonable person can read in more than one way.

 	- Her comparison to shorter sentences for stranger rapes is not a fair measure. British sentencing follows published guidelines, and the judge explained on the record where he placed this offense and why he added and subtracted time.


None of these corrections touches the central points: the evidentiary margin was thin, the media process was prejudicial, the anonymity was one-sided, and the gag is unjust.


## A Closing Word


Tucker closed the interview by asking for prayer for Russell Brand and his family. I join him. *"I was in prison, and ye came unto me"* **(Matthew 25:36)**. Christ did not tell us to visit only the innocent prisoners.


I also ask for prayer for the women who testified. If they were wronged, they carried it for twenty years, and no verdict gives back what was taken. If any of them were mistaken, may the truth come out in God's time. Righteousness does not require us to choose between grief and the presumption of innocence. It requires us to hold both, and to demand a justice system that does the same.


*"He that is first in his own cause seemeth just; but his neighbour cometh and searcheth him"* **(Proverbs 18:17)**. A free people keeps the searching open, even after the verdict, and especially when the government would rather it closed.

## Sources and Their Limits


**Primary record.** Mr Justice Bennathan's sentencing remarks, published by the UK Judiciary (judiciary.uk, R v Russell Brand, 9 November 2026), are the most authoritative source on the sentence and the judge's findings. The Crown Prosecution Service and Metropolitan Police charging announcements (cps.gov.uk; news.met.police.uk) establish the counts and dates.


**Trial evidence.** No trial transcript is public. Everything here about testimony comes from contemporaneous court reporting: the Associated Press, Reuters, PA Media (via ITV, the Irish News, and Australian outlets), Courthouse News, LBC, and Britbrief (for the agreed facts about the phone contact). Court reporters summarize and select. They are not a full record, and none of them saw what the jury saw of the witnesses' demeanor.


**Government statements.** Downing Street and Justice Secretary Norris's comments come from Reuters, ITV, LBC, and Mediaweek. The prospect of monitored communications and a ban on contact with his wife comes from LBC's reporting of the investigation. Official statements are the government's own account of its motives, and I have treated them as interested-party testimony.


**Dissenting analysis.** John Leake's essay "Highly Questionable Conviction of Russell Brand" (*Focal Points*, thefocalpoints.com, 8 November 2026) gives the strongest critical reading of the evidence that I found, including the absence of forensic evidence and the witnesses who were never called. It is advocacy, but useful advocacy. The Megyn Kelly interview on *The Tucker Carlson Show* (20 November 2026) prompted this essay. I checked her factual claims against the reporting above and noted where they didn't hold up.


**Documentaries.** The program's own promotion, as reported by JOE, documents the use of actors in Dispatches: Russell Brand: In Plain Sight (Channel 4, September 2023). IBTimes UK and What's on Netflix document its use in Russell Brand: Open Secret (Netflix, November 2026). I have watched Open Secret and noted the disclaimer that actors were used.


**Background.** The 2023 Dinenage letter and Rumble's reply, the 1994 abolition of the corroboration warning, the 2025 Casey audit on grooming gangs, and The Times's 2025 report on communications-offense arrests come from the established public record before this case. I did not re-verify each one for this essay.


**Limits.** My sourcing leans on mainstream outlets because they had reporters in the courtroom. Those outlets have mostly been hostile to Brand since 2023, and their choice of which testimony to quote could have left out evidence favorable to him, as well as evidence against him. The public knows only fragments of the defense's full case and the cross-examination of each complainant. Firsthand accounts from people close to the case, on both sides, should be treated as leads, not proof. I have not named any complainant who has not chosen to identify herself.
