# 261119 - Purity of Ideology in Idaho Politics

# The Hireling, the Label, and the Open Door:


### Party Purity, Honest Representation, and the Work of Persuasion

**Thomas Lee Abshier, ND** *| 19 November 2026*


---


## A Report from Kootenai County

I recently talked with my friend John Howard, whom I have known since college, more than fifty years now. John lives in Kootenai County, in North Idaho. His wife Jeanette was recently elected a precinct committeeman (PC) on the Kootenai County Republican Central Committee (KCRCC), and John has been going to meetings with her as a spectator. He told me about a meeting he thought I needed to hear about. They are questions about Idaho, but they are also questions about every party, every election, and every attempt to govern a free people by persuasion instead of by force.

Some background. On November 3, 2026, Idaho voters rejected Proposition 1, an initiative that would have written a right to abortion up to fetal viability into state law. It failed by roughly 56 to 44 percent, and Idaho's protection of unborn life remains in force. Earlier, while the initiative was still gathering signatures, it came out that several sitting Republican precinct committeemen in Kootenai County had signed the petition to put it on the ballot. The Idaho Republican Party platform is plainly pro-life. The KCRCC opened an inquiry into the signers.

John watched that confrontation. Three PCs had been identified as signers. The conservatives in the room pressed them with moral arguments, and John said they did it well. They wanted the signers to admit they had made a grave moral error. One of the three exploded, shouted that the whole thing was an outrage, threw down his chair, and left. The other two gave an answer John did not expect.

## The Hired-Gun Defense

Their answer, as John reported it, went like this: *I signed that petition as a private citizen, on my own initiative. I am free to sign any petition I want. But when I come to the KCRCC, I know the committee opposes this initiative, and I keep to the letter of its bylaws. I never say I support the initiative while I am representing the committee.*

When the conservatives asked what they personally believed about abortion, the two men would not engage. They did not seem to think the question mattered. They made no argument for abortion at all. Their whole case was that they had kept their contract.

John called it the morality of a hired gun, a mercenary. A bodyguard hired to protect a billionaire might say, *I don't care whether he lives or dies, but while I'm on the job, I do the job.* There is a kind of integrity in that. But John found it strangely empty, with no personal conviction behind it, like a machine running its program. He asked me what I made of it.

My first answer was that everyone has opinions, and they will not always match the organization they serve. If it is your job to carry out a position, you had better carry it out. Keeping one's word is a real virtue, and I do not dismiss it.

But the real question is a different one: **Is this the man you want representing you?** A precinct committeeman is not a contractor hired to deliver a service. He is a voice. He is chosen to carry the convictions of his neighbors into the councils of the party. If he does not hold those convictions, then when the time comes to argue for them, he will have talking points and no heart. He will read off a list. There will be no spirit behind it, and people can hear that.

Scripture names this figure exactly. Jesus contrasts the good shepherd with the hired hand:

*"But he that is an hireling, and not the shepherd, whose own the sheep are not, seeth the wolf coming, and leaveth the sheep, and fleeth: and the hireling fleeth, because he is an hireling, and careth not for the sheep."* **(John 10:12-13)**

The hireling may do his job on a quiet day. The test comes when the wolf comes. A representative who does not care about the position he represents will not hold it under pressure, will not find the better argument when the old one fails, and will not pay any price for it. The hired-gun defense answers whether these men broke a rule. It does not answer whether they are fit to shepherd.

## Walter's Rhyme

John raised this with Walter, who was visiting for John's birthday and offered a clever counterpoint. John eats a plant-based, nutritarian diet. He has said plainly that he does this because the current science says it is healthiest, and that if a study showed snail eyeballs were the key to health, he would start raising snails. So, Walter said, aren't you the same as these men? You aren't committed to the principle of not killing animals; you are committed to what you get out of it.

John laughed and admitted he had never thought of it that way. I told him the two cases rhyme, but they are different categories. Here is the difference, as I see it. John states his position openly, gives his reason, and stands behind it. He does not claim to be an animal-rights vegan. Anyone who asks him knows exactly where he stands and why. A conviction held provisionally, for a stated reason and open to evidence, is still an honest conviction.

The signers' problem is not that their views are provisional. It is that their label and their convictions do not match, and they did not tell the voters. The question is not how firmly you hold a view. The question is whether you are truthful about what you hold.

## A RINO Label Is a False Report

That brings me to the heart of it. A Republican In Name Only says, *I am a Republican.* Ask him what he stands for, and on the defining moral questions he stands with the other party. Ask him what he calls himself, and he says Republican. I told John that this is simply a lie. It may be a lie of omission rather than commission, but a party label on a ballot is a public promise about what the candidate stands for. Our Lord's standard for speech is simple:

*"But let your communication be, Yea, yea; Nay, nay: for whatsoever is more than these cometh of evil."* **(Matthew 5:37)**

I want to be fair here. A party is a coalition, and no member agrees with every plank. A man may disagree with his party's position on tariffs or road funding and still be honestly called a Republican. But the protection of innocent life is not a minor plank in the Idaho platform; it is near the center of what the party says it is. Actively working to put abortion on the ballot while wearing the party's name, and not disclosing it to the people who elected you, is not a disagreement on the margins. It is a misrepresentation at the center.

## Should the Party Be Able to Throw Them Out?

John pointed out something important. The bylaws imply that anyone who claims to be a Republican agrees with the platform, which is long. But nothing in them says a member found to oppose a plank can be removed. There are no teeth. He thought perhaps there should be: a rule that a PC who does not uphold the party's values can be found unsuitable, tried before the committee, and voted out by his fellow PCs.

### The constitutional parallel

This reminded me of the arguments I have had to work through many times in this campaign about the separation of church and state. When a man stands on a Christian platform, people say, *You can't do that; that's separation of church and state.* That argument is flawed. The First Amendment forbids Congress to make a law establishing a religion, that is, to declare that everyone shall be a Presbyterian. It does not forbid a candidate to speak from his faith, or voters to weigh it.

The more relevant clause for John's question is in Article VI of the Constitution: *"no religious Test shall ever be required as a Qualification to any Office or public Trust under the United States."* The law may not bar a man from office for his beliefs. But John saw the point immediately: the voters can still judge a man by what he says and what he is. *If you're not of the character they expect, they're not going to vote for you.* The ballot is the test. There is no need for any other.

So there are layers. There is what the voters want. Then there are the restrictions of law. Then, more refined still, the restrictions of the Constitution. A party is a voluntary association, not the government, and it is not bound by Article VI. But the wisdom behind that clause applies to it too.

### Two worldviews

What John and I were really looking at is a choice between two ways of governing. One is centralized and law-based: you must pass this test to belong, and a tribunal enforces it. The other is closer to a free market of ideas: each man states his position, the voters decide, and the remedy for a bad representative is to replace him. Every republic has to decide what it makes a law against and what it leaves to persuasion. A party has to answer the same question.

### Why a purity rule cuts both ways

Here is the problem I see with a rule that excludes anyone who does not fully support the platform: **once you have it, the platform can never change.** You will have selected for people who already agree with it. If the platform is right, that seems fine. But if it ever goes wrong, how would anyone ever fix it?

John answered that you change it the same way you passed it, by majority vote. True, but the majority is drawn only from those already admitted. Now imagine the moderates fully take control of the KCRCC, rewrite the platform to their liking, and then use an expulsion rule to remove anyone who dissents. The conservatives could never get back in to change it. The machinery of purity belongs to whoever holds it, and it would be used against the very people who built it. John's comment was, *Maybe that's why they don't have any teeth behind it.* I think he is right.

This is not a hypothetical in Kootenai County. In the May 2026 precinct elections, the moderate faction made gains and the longtime chairman, Brent Regan, was voted out. Control of these committees changes hands. Any weapon built to drive out the other side will someday be in the other side's hands.

### Where I stand

I stand on the side of keeping the door open and putting the test before the voters, and I hold that position open to a better argument. My reasoning runs through the three questions I apply to every public policy.

*Is it righteous?* A party has every right to define what it stands for and to say so plainly. It is righteous to tell the truth about what a man believes and how he has acted. It is also righteous to refuse to let a man use a name he does not honor.

*If so, is it loving?* Exposing a public official's public acts, such as his signature on a petition he signed as a citizen, is accountability, and accountability is a form of love toward the voters who trusted him. Building a tribunal that a faction can turn against its rivals is not loving, because its natural use is the punishment of dissent, not the discovery of truth. I would also draw a line between officeholders and ordinary citizens. A PC asked people to trust him with a public office; a private citizen who signed a petition did not. Scrutiny of the first is fair; publishing the second for public shaming is not the same thing.

*Would I want it done to me?* If I held an office and had acted against my party's platform, I would want the truth told about it, and I would want to answer before the voters. I would not want a committee controlled by my opponents to have the power to remove me without the voters. The rule I would accept for myself is disclosure and an election, not expulsion.

So the party's real tools, as I see them, are these: a clear platform; open questioning of candidates about where they agree and where they take exception; published records of how officeholders have acted; endorsements given and withheld; and the primary election. A party can fairly ask every candidate for precinct committeeman to state publicly whether he supports the platform and where he takes exception. That is not a religious test or a loyalty oath. It is the truth in labeling that John and I agreed was missing. The signers ran without the voters knowing what they had done. A conservative opponent who brought it to light might well have decided the race.

## The Test Fails When the Truth Fails

But leaving the test to the voters works only if the voters can get the truth. And this is where politics as it is now practiced breaks down.

Lying about one's opponent is a stock in trade of modern elections. A campaign says, *This candidate bites his dog.* It is a lie, but it goes out in ads, on television, and through bots, and there is little or no penalty. Our courts have been very reluctant to let the government punish false political speech. In *United States v. Alvarez* (2012) the Supreme Court struck down a law making it a crime to lie about military honors, and federal appeals courts have since struck down state laws against false campaign statements in Minnesota and Ohio on First Amendment grounds. Defamation suits remain possible, but they are slow and costly and rarely reach the election in time. I do not want the government deciding which political claims are true; that power would be abused faster than any other. But it means the protection has to come from somewhere other than law.

The commandment still stands, whether or not the courts enforce it:

*"Thou shalt not raise a false report: put not thine hand with the wicked to be an unrighteous witness."* **(Exodus 23:1)**

The damage of campaign lies is larger than any one race. Once every claim about a candidate might be a lie, every claim is discounted, including the true ones. John said this is exactly why so many people do not vote. They vote for a proposition they think helps them, find out its wording did the opposite, and conclude, *I just hurt myself by taking part. The best I can do to protect myself is to stay out of it.*

John told me what this looked like door to door. People told Jeanette, *If this has anything to do with Brent Regan, I'm voting against it,* though they had never been to a meeting and could not say what he had done. One man said he simply disliked the KCRCC. John asked him: if the people who opposed Brent took over, would he still hate the KCRCC? It is just a name; the question is who is running it. The man had no answer. His mind was made up by a reputation, not by any fact he could name.

### What gets lost when the majority isn't real

The same disconnect explains, I believe, some of the frustration voters showed in this election. John's sense was that people were not voting for the left's agenda; they were angry that Republicans had held the presidency and both houses of Congress and had not done what they promised. I think a large part of the reason is that the majority was not what it appeared to be. A majority that includes members who call themselves Republicans but vote with Democrats on the defining questions is a majority on paper only. So the voter says, *I oppose everything the Democrats will do, so I'll vote for the Democrats, because the Republicans didn't do what they said.* John reminded me of a cartoon: *There's no way I'm voting for this Democrat. I'm going to find another Democrat.* There are other reasons too, such as narrow margins and Senate rules, but labels that do not match convictions are one of them, and the voter pays for it.

### The national platform

I have to apply the same standard to my own party's national platform. In 2024 the Republican National Convention changed the platform. It had called for protecting unborn life through a constitutional amendment and had defined marriage as the union of one man and one woman. The 2024 platform drops the national protection of life, says the party will oppose late-term abortion and leave the question to the states, and speaks warmly of "the sanctity of marriage" without defining it or calling for any change to the legal recognition of same-sex marriage. A platform states what legislation a party will work for. By removing those planks, the party says it will no longer work for that legislation. On those points I am against my party's national platform.

I understand the practical argument, and John raised it on behalf of his friend Brent, who served as a presidential elector: you cannot judge a candidate in isolation, only against his opponent, and the alternative was worse. I accept that reasoning in the voting booth; it is why I tell voters to vote for the best man on the ballot, and why I will not be a spoiler. But the voting booth is not the platform committee. A party can be the better choice and still have weakened its stand, and those who love it ought to say so.

Idaho, by contrast, has kept its stand. John was struck by how firm the Idaho Republican platform is, and in 2026 the state convention added a call to eliminate property taxes, led by former state senator Scott Herndon of Sandpoint, who argues that a property tax makes every homeowner a renter from the government. John said Herndon has clearly thought it through and holds his ground under hard questions. Whatever one thinks of the plan, that is how a platform plank should be advanced: in public, with reasons, by a man willing to defend it.

## How Wrong Thinking Is Actually Overcome

If law cannot police the label, and purity rules can be turned against their makers, then what is left? Persuasion. And John's experience shows what persuasion actually requires.

### It begins with listening

The first thing John told me about that meeting was not the confrontation but his own state of mind. He said it was like watching a Shakespeare play. He did not sit there thinking, *These are the bad guys; I don't want to hear them.* He was open to anything anyone said, and he found that even the RINOs, in their own strange way, had principles. You cannot persuade a man whose reasoning you have not understood. The hired-gun defense is wrong as I see it, but it is not nothing; it is an appeal to keeping one's word, and the answer to it must start there.

Scripture describes how a case is tested:

*"He that is first in his own cause seemeth just; but his neighbour cometh and searcheth him."* **(Proverbs 18:17)**

Every side sounds right until it is questioned. Debate is the searching. It is not a contest to humiliate the other man; it is how a community discovers which case survives examination. The conservatives at that meeting asked the right question: what do you personally believe? The fact that the answer was refused told the room something.

### Arguing with made-up minds does not work

John was blunt: you cannot campaign by arguing with people whose minds are made up. That does not work. Some people will hear you; others will not. What does work is personal. Jeanette is warm and personable, and she went to hundreds of houses. Her opponent was a lifelong resident and a firefighter whom everyone knew and liked. Jeanette did not attack his character. She told voters a plain, checkable fact: he missed most meetings, and when he came, he did not know what was going on. Many voters said, *If that's all he does, I'm not voting for him.* She won by 13 votes. Without the door-to-door work, she would almost certainly have lost.

That is what overcoming wrong thinking looks like in practice: a true, specific, verifiable claim, delivered face to face by someone whose character the voter can see for himself. It cannot be faked at scale, and it is very hard to answer with a lie.

### A trusted rating, earned over time

The second remedy is what John's group calls "rating and vetting." Most voters cannot research every race and every initiative. If an organization does that work carefully, and earns trust by being right over time, the voter can rely on it and vote the slate. John believes this fixes a large hole in the democratic process, and I agree. It is a group of citizens who take on the duty of being informed — moral guardians of the law, in a sense. Since conservatives no longer control the KCRCC, John's group is building that role through the Kootenai Freedom Caucus.

When John was active in the KCRCC, he made a line-by-line comparison of the slate endorsed by the rival "North Idaho Republicans" with the slate endorsed by the local Democratic Party, candidates and initiatives alike. They were nearly identical. Laid side by side, the facts did the persuading. That is the honest answer to a false label: not a tribunal, but the record, set out where anyone can check it.

### Personal witness weighs more than money

John made one more observation I found wise. A yard sign means something a billboard does not. A billboard means someone spent a few hundred thousand dollars. A sign on a lawn means a neighbor put his name, in effect, on that candidate or that cause. A whole street of signs is a street of personal endorsements. That is why, John said, signs opposing Prop 1 were torn down over and over, and why his group kept putting them back. The measure failed.

The lesson is that conviction persuades in proportion to what it costs the one who holds it. Money is cheap testimony. A neighbor's name is costly testimony. The hireling gives the first kind; the shepherd gives the second.

### Speaking the truth in love

All of this comes back to a single standard:

*"But speaking the truth in love, may grow up into him in all things, which is the head, even Christ."* **(Ephesians 4:15)**

Truth without love becomes the shouting match that ends with a thrown chair. Love without truth becomes the humoring silence that lets a false label stand. The work of a free people is to hold both: to name plainly what a man has done, to listen to his reasons, to answer them, and to leave the verdict to his neighbors at the ballot box.

*"Come now, and let us reason together, saith the LORD."* **(Isaiah 1:18)**

If God Himself governs by reasoning with His people, then we should not be quick to govern our parties by expulsion. We should be quick to reason, slow to exclude, and unwilling to lie.

## What This Means for My Campaign

This conversation clarified something about my own effort. The point of my candidacy is to bring moral questions into the public conscience by voice and by reason, not by power. The tools John described are the same ones I am relying on: positions stated openly, with their reasons; an invitation to challenge them; and a network of people each persuading the people they know, one telling two, so that the conversation at the door is multiplied rather than replaced. If that network does not form, the effort does not go forward; I will not run as a spoiler. If it does, it will be because the case was made honestly, neighbor to neighbor, and survived examination.

The hired gun does the job and does not care. The shepherd cares, says plainly what he stands for, and stays when the wolf comes. Our parties need more shepherds, and the way to get them is not a stronger rulebook. It is voters who know the truth and neighbors willing to tell it.

## Notes


 	- **Proposition 1.** Idaho's Reproductive Freedom and Privacy Act initiative qualified for the November 3, 2026 ballot and was defeated with about 56 percent voting no. Idaho's existing law, which protects unborn life with limited exceptions (including to save the mother's life), remains in force. John's comment in our conversation, that abortion is already permitted when the mother's life is threatened and that the campaign for the initiative implied otherwise, is consistent with the law's text; the dispute in Idaho has been over how clearly the exceptions are drawn in practice.

 	- **The KCRCC inquiry.** The Coeur d'Alene Press reported in September 2026 that the KCRCC opened an investigation into members who signed the Prop 1 petition, and that at least one signer called it a "power play." My account of the meeting itself is John's firsthand report. I have not named the signers here.

 	- **The firefighter resolution.** At a special KCRCC meeting, a retired firefighter in Jeanette's precinct asked the committee to support dedicating a stretch of highway to Battalion Chiefs John Morrison and Frank Harwood, who were killed in the June 29, 2025 ambush on Canfield Mountain. John said every faction supported it. I note it because it shows the committee can still act together when the moral question is clear.

 	- **Article VI.** The No Religious Test Clause reads: "no religious Test shall ever be required as a Qualification to any Office or public Trust under the United States." It binds the federal government, not private associations such as political parties.

 	- **False campaign speech.** *United States v. Alvarez*, 567 U.S. 709 (2012); *281 Care Committee v. Arneson*, 766 F.3d 774 (8th Cir. 2014) (Minnesota); *Susan B. Anthony List v. Driehaus*, 814 F.3d 466 (6th Cir. 2016) (Ohio).

 	- **2024 Republican platform.** The 2024 platform states the party will oppose late-term abortion and treats abortion law as a matter for the states after *Dobbs*; it no longer calls for a Human Life Amendment, and it no longer defines marriage as one man and one woman.


## Sources and Their Limits

The account of the KCRCC meeting, the precinct race, the door-to-door conversations, and the comparison of endorsement slates comes from John Howard's firsthand report in a conversation with me on November 19, 2026. I treat it as reliable testimony from a man I have known for fifty years, but it is one observer's account of contested local events, and the people on the other side would describe some of them differently. Election results, the KCRCC inquiry, the 2026 Idaho platform's property tax plank, and the change in the Kootenai County chairmanship are drawn from regional news reporting (the Idaho Capital Sun, States Newsroom, the Coeur d'Alene Press, Boise State Public Radio, and the Idaho Statesman). That reporting leans toward the mainstream record, and on intra-party disputes in North Idaho some of those outlets are themselves regarded by local conservatives as partisan; where my account relies on them, I have used them for facts that are a matter of public record rather than for their characterizations.
