# 261114 - The Crusades Re-evaluated

# The Crusades in Proportion


### *A history against the modern propaganda*

By Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | 14 November 2026


---


## Why I am writing this

While writing on Denmark's refugee policy, I found myself making a point about the Crusades that deserves an essay of its own. The point is this: the West has been taught to remember the Crusades as its original sin against Islam, and that lesson is now used as a lever. If the West believes it wronged Islam first, it will feel bound to make amends by opening its doors, its schools, and its public square without condition. I believe that lesson is disproportionate, and I believe the policy built on it is dangerous.

Two American presidents have given the lesson its modern form. In November 2001, two months after the September 11 attacks, Bill Clinton told students at Georgetown University about the massacre at Jerusalem in 1099 and said of that story, "we are still paying for it." In February 2015, at the National Prayer Breakfast, Barack Obama answered the atrocities of the Islamic State by reminding Christians of the Crusades and the Inquisition, so that we would not "get on our high horse." In both cases, the Crusades were offered as a reason for the West to hold back its moral judgment of Islamic violence in the present.

My posture is unapologetic. I am not against Muslims. They are God's children, made in His image, and He wants them free. I am against the system of Islam, which I believe holds them in bondage, and I believe the struggle with Islam is one of the greatest threats to God's kingdom, to peace, and to prosperity in the world. My campaign will treat that struggle as a crusade, and before I use that word I owe my readers an honest account of the original Crusades: what they were, why they happened, where they went wrong, and how they have been turned into propaganda. At the end I will say exactly what kind of crusade I mean. It is not the kind fought with the sword.

## The story we are told

The popular account runs roughly like this. In 1095, a pope sent greedy, land-hungry European knights to invade a peaceful and more civilized Muslim world. They massacred innocents, tried to force conversions, planted colonies, and left behind a wound that Muslims have remembered with bitterness ever since. On this account, the Crusades were the first chapter of Western imperialism, and modern Muslim anger is its delayed result.

Nearly every element of that story is either false or badly out of proportion. To see why, the story has to start where the Crusaders' own story started: not in 1095, but four and a half centuries earlier.

## Before the Crusades: four and a half centuries of conquest

Muhammad died in 632. Within a few years, Arab armies broke out of Arabia and fell on the Christian Roman Empire and the Persian Empire at once. At the Yarmouk River in 636, they destroyed the Roman army of Syria. Jerusalem surrendered in 637. Egypt, the most Christian province of the ancient world, fell in the early 640s, with Alexandria taken by 642. Carthage fell in 698, and with it the Christian church of North Africa, the church of Augustine, began its long death.

In 711 Muslim armies crossed from Africa into Spain and conquered nearly all of the Iberian peninsula within a few years. They pushed on into France until Charles Martel stopped them between Tours and Poitiers in 732. Through the ninth century, Muslim forces took Sicily, island by island and city by city, from 827 to 902. They raided the coasts of Italy and southern France, and in 846 a Muslim raiding force sacked the basilicas of St. Peter and St. Paul outside the walls of Rome.

Consider what this meant for the Christian world. Of the five great patriarchal cities of early Christianity, three (Jerusalem, Antioch, and Alexandria) were under Muslim rule by the middle of the seventh century, and the other two (Rome and Constantinople) had been attacked. Christians who remained under Muslim rule lived as *dhimmis*, a protected but subordinate people who paid a special tax, the jizya, and lived under legal restrictions. Many communities endured for centuries under those terms; many also slowly converted, emigrated, or disappeared.

Then came two shocks in the eleventh century. In 1009, the Fatimid caliph al-Hakim ordered the destruction of the Church of the Holy Sepulcher in Jerusalem, the church built over the tomb of Christ, and launched a wider persecution of Christians and Jews. In 1071, the Seljuk Turks crushed the Byzantine army at Manzikert in eastern Anatolia. Within a few years Turkish forces had overrun most of Asia Minor, the heartland of the Greek Christian empire, and set up their capital at Nicaea, the city where the Nicene Creed had been written, within striking distance of Constantinople itself. Pilgrims traveling to Jerusalem faced new dangers along the roads.

In March 1095, envoys of the Byzantine emperor Alexios I came to Pope Urban II at the Council of Piacenza and asked for help from the Latin West. In November of that year, at Clermont in France, Urban preached the sermon that launched the First Crusade. Its stated aims were to aid the Christians of the East and to free Jerusalem.

That is the context the modern story leaves out. The Crusades did not begin a war between Christendom and Islam. They were a late, and ultimately unsuccessful, counterattack in a war that Islam had begun four and a half centuries earlier and had been winning ever since.

## The First Crusade (1096–1099)

The response to Urban's call was far larger than anyone expected. Tens of thousands took the cross, knights and commoners alike.

The crusade's first great crime came before it ever reached the East, and it must be named first. In the spring of 1096, bands of crusaders in the Rhineland, led by men such as Count Emicho, turned on the Jewish communities of Speyer, Worms, Mainz, and Cologne, and murdered thousands of Jews who refused baptism. The Church had not called for this, and some bishops tried to stop it; the bishop of Speyer protected the Jews of his city, while others failed or did not try. Fifty years later, when a monk named Radulf began preaching against the Jews during the Second Crusade, Bernard of Clairvaux went to the Rhineland in person and silenced him. The Rhineland massacres were a betrayal of the crusade's own purpose, and they belong in any honest account.

The main armies crossed into Asia Minor in 1097, took Nicaea, defeated the Turks at Dorylaeum, and endured a starving, desperate siege of Antioch through the winter of 1097–1098. They took the city, were themselves besieged inside it, and broke out against all expectation. In June 1099, the survivors, perhaps a fraction of those who had set out, reached Jerusalem. On 15 July 1099, they stormed the walls.

What followed was a massacre. I will not soften it. Crusaders killed many of the city's Muslim and Jewish inhabitants, including people who had taken refuge on the Temple Mount.

But it must also be put in proportion, for three reasons.

First, the scale has been inflated. The Western chroniclers wrote of blood up to the ankles or the knees, borrowing the image of Revelation: *"and blood came out of the winepress, even unto the horse bridles"* **(Revelation 14:20)**. That was the language of apocalyptic triumph, not a body count. The figure of 70,000 dead comes from the Muslim historian Ibn al-Athir, writing more than a century later; the earliest Arabic reports of the conquest vary widely, and the full-scale massacre narrative became the standard Arabic account only in the thirteenth century. Letters preserved in the Cairo Geniza show Jewish captives being ransomed after the fall of the city, and some Muslim defenders were allowed to leave. Most modern historians judge the dead to have numbered in the thousands, not the tens of thousands.

Second, by the laws of war of that age, both Christian and Muslim, a city that refused to surrender and was taken by storm could be sacked. That was a cruel rule, but it was the common rule. Muslim commanders applied it as well: Zengi at Edessa in 1144 and the Mamluk sultan Baybars at Antioch in 1268, where Baybars himself wrote a letter to the city's absent Christian prince boasting of the slaughter.

Third, and most important, the sack of Jerusalem violated the teaching of the faith in whose name it was done. I will return to that point, which is central.

## The long defense (1099–1291)

After 1099 the crusaders established four small states along the eastern Mediterranean: the County of Edessa, the Principality of Antioch, the County of Tripoli, and the Kingdom of Jerusalem. They were never large. Throughout their history, the Latin Christians were a minority in them, ruling a population of Eastern Christians, Muslims, and Jews. The crusades that followed the first were, for the most part, attempts to defend or recover what had been won, not to expand it.

Edessa fell to Zengi in 1144. The Second Crusade (1147–1149), preached by Bernard of Clairvaux, came to recover it and failed. Then Saladin rose, united Egypt and Syria under his rule, destroyed the army of the Kingdom of Jerusalem at Hattin on 4 July 1187, and took Jerusalem that October. Here Saladin deserves the credit history gives him: he spared the city's Christians and allowed them to ransom themselves rather than repeating the massacre of 1099. I say so gladly. Fairness to an opponent is no concession.

The Third Crusade (1189–1192), led by Richard the Lionheart and Philip II of France, recovered Acre and much of the coast but not Jerusalem. It too carries a stain: in August 1191, when negotiations over a prisoner exchange broke down, Richard had some 2,700 Muslim prisoners from the Acre garrison executed. The crusade ended in the Treaty of Jaffa (1192), which left Jerusalem in Muslim hands but guaranteed Christian pilgrims access to it.

The movement's worst disgrace came in 1204. The Fourth Crusade, deep in debt to Venice and drawn into a Byzantine succession dispute, turned on Constantinople, the greatest Christian city in the world, and sacked it. Pope Innocent III had forbidden the crusaders to attack Christians and had excommunicated those who did. The sack broke the Greek East's strength and deepened the schism between the Eastern and Western churches for centuries. In 2001, and again in 2004, Pope John Paul II expressed his sorrow for it to the Orthodox world. If the West owes an apology for any crusade, it owes it to the Greek Christians of Constantinople, and it has apologized.

The thirteenth century brought the Fifth Crusade's failure in Egypt, the emperor Frederick II's recovery of Jerusalem by treaty in 1229 without a battle, the city's final loss in 1244, and the two crusades of the French king Louis IX, both of which failed. Baybars took Antioch in 1268. Acre, the last major crusader city, fell in 1291. The crusader states had lasted, in all, less than two centuries.

## The war did not end in 1291

The modern account treats the Crusades as a closed chapter of Western aggression that ended with the fall of Acre. But the war between Christendom and Islam did not end in 1291. It moved west, into Europe itself, and for the next four centuries Europe was on the defensive.

In Spain, Christians had been fighting to reclaim their land since the battle of Covadonga around 722. The Reconquista did not end until Granada fell in 1492, nearly eight centuries after the conquest of 711.

In the East, the Ottoman Turks rose in the fourteenth century and drove into the Balkans. Crusading armies were destroyed at Nicopolis in 1396 and at Varna in 1444. Constantinople fell in 1453, ending the Christian Roman Empire after more than a thousand years. The Hungarian commander John Hunyadi held Belgrade in 1456. Rhodes fell in 1522, and the kingdom of Hungary was shattered at Mohács in 1526. Ottoman armies besieged Vienna in 1529. The Knights of St. John held Malta against a great Ottoman siege in 1565, and a Holy League fleet broke Ottoman sea power at Lepanto in 1571. In 1683 the Ottomans stood at Vienna a second time, and were driven off by an army under the Polish king Jan Sobieski.

At sea, the Barbary corsairs of North Africa raided the coasts of Italy, Spain, France, and even England, Ireland, and Iceland. The historian Robert Davis has estimated that between 1530 and 1780 they enslaved somewhere between one million and one and a quarter million Europeans.

Count the years. From the fall of Jerusalem in 637 to the second siege of Vienna in 1683 is more than a thousand years. For most of that millennium the expanding power was Islam, and the defending power was Christendom. The crusades to the Holy Land fill less than two centuries of it.

## Weighing the myths

With history in view, we can weigh the common claims one at a time.

**"The Crusades were unprovoked aggression."** They were a response to centuries of conquest, the destruction of the Holy Sepulcher, the loss of Asia Minor, and a direct appeal for help from Eastern Christians. Whatever one thinks of their methods, they were defensive in origin.

**"The Crusades were colonialism, driven by greed for land and loot."** Historians such as Jonathan Riley-Smith show that crusading was ruinously expensive. Many knights sold or mortgaged their lands to go, and most who survived came home rather than settling in the East. The crusader states never drew large numbers of Western settlers, and they never produced the kind of wealth that would make a colony pay. Men went for many reasons, including ambition, but their dominant motive, in their own words, was penitential: to do something hard for God and for the remission of their sins.

**"The Crusades were about forcing Muslims to convert."** They were not. Forced conversion was not a goal of the crusading movement, and Muslims under crusader rule generally kept their mosques and their faith. The Muslim traveler Ibn Jubayr, passing through the Kingdom of Jerusalem in 1184, observed with some alarm that the Muslim peasants living under Frankish rule were treated well, and he feared the example would draw Muslims to Frankish rule.

**"Muslims have remembered the Crusades with bitterness for nine centuries."** This is the claim I find most revealing. Until the nineteenth century, the Crusades were not a major theme of Muslim historical memory. To the great Muslim powers of the Middle Ages, the crusaders were a regional problem, far less important than the Mongol invasions that destroyed Baghdad in 1258. The Arabic term for the Crusades, *al-hurub al-salibiyya*, "the wars of the cross," came into use only in the nineteenth century, adopted from European writing. The first full history of the Crusades in Arabic, by the Egyptian historian Sayyid Ali al-Hariri, appeared in 1899, around the time the German Kaiser Wilhelm II visited Saladin's neglected tomb in Damascus and paid to restore it. The bitter "long memory" was largely revived in the age of European colonialism and then put to political use by twentieth-century nationalists and Islamists. When Osama bin Laden founded his "World Islamic Front for Jihad against Jews and Crusaders" in 1998, he was drawing on that revived memory, not on nine unbroken centuries of grievance.

In fairness, some scholars, such as the historian Paul Cobb, argue that Muslim writers remembered the Crusades more continuously than the textbooks say, and that the Arabic sources from 1300 to 1900 have not been fully studied. I accept that the question is open. But even on that view, the Crusades were remembered as one episode in a long history of Muslim victory, ending with the crusaders expelled, not as a wound inflicted on a helpless people.

## The excesses, in proportion

I have named the crimes of the crusading movement: the Rhineland massacres of 1096, the slaughter at Jerusalem in 1099, the execution of the prisoners at Acre in 1191, and the sack of Constantinople in 1204. To those I add the crusade against the Cathars in southern France, where the crusaders massacred the people of Béziers in 1209. These were real sins, committed by men who bore the cross.

Here is the proportion I ask my readers to keep.

The crusaders' worst acts violated their own Lord's teaching. Jesus told Peter, *"Put up again thy sword into his place: for all they that take the sword shall perish with the sword"* **(Matthew 26:52)**. He told Pilate, *"My kingdom is not of this world: if my kingdom were of this world, then would my servants fight"* **(John 18:36)**. When a Christian massacres the innocent, he acts against Christ, and the Christian conscience has the means within its own Scriptures to condemn him. That is why Bernard could silence Radulf, why Innocent III could excommunicate the sackers of Constantinople, and why a pope could apologize for 1204.

The conquests of the seventh and eighth centuries were different. They were not a betrayal of Islam's founder; they followed his example. Muhammad was himself a military commander who led his followers in battle, and the caliphs who conquered Syria, Egypt, Persia, North Africa, and Spain did so in the decades immediately after his death, under men who had known him. The Qur'an itself commands: "Fight against such of those who have been given the Scripture as believe not in Allah nor the Last Day … until they pay the tribute readily, being brought low" (Qur'an 9:29, Pickthall translation). Muslims interpret that verse in different ways, and many peaceful Muslims read it narrowly. But it is the textual root of the *dhimma* system under which Christians and Jews lived for centuries in the lands Islam conquered.

So the proportion is this. The Crusades were less than two centuries of mostly failed defensive war, marred by real crimes that their own faith condemned. The Islamic conquests were more than a millennium of expansion, carried out in the name of a faith whose founder was a conqueror. A history that remembers only the first, and forgets the second, is not a history. It is propaganda.

## Why the propaganda matters now

The modern use of the Crusades is not an academic quarrel. It does work in the present.

It disarms moral judgment. When a president answers the Islamic State's beheadings by recalling what Christians did nine hundred years ago, he is telling the West that it has no standing to condemn. But a Christian's sins in 1099 do not excuse a jihadist's crimes in 2015, and the West's capacity to see the difference is exactly what it most needs to keep.

It creates a debt that can never be paid. If the Crusades are the West's original sin against Islam, then every restriction on immigration, every defense of Christian culture, and every public preference for the God of the Bible can be painted as a new crusade, a fresh offense against the victim. A nation that accepts that framing will feel bound to prove its virtue by giving way, again and again.

And it hides the present danger. While the West apologizes for the eleventh century, it ignores what is happening in the twenty-first.

## What I believe about Islam today

Here I state my own convictions plainly.

I believe Islam does not assimilate; it dominates. It is not only a private faith but a complete system of law, government, and social order, and where it grows strong enough it seeks to rule. When its community within a host nation reaches a critical size, it begins to reshape the host's towns, then its leadership, then its culture, and no safe level exists at which that aspiration simply disappears. Like the Jewish people, whose religion and traditions have preserved them as a people for thousands of years, Islam has a self-reinforcing, self-perpetuating system that carries it from generation to generation. Unlike Judaism, it includes a mandate to expand. To expect Islam to dissolve into a Western nation the way other immigrant faiths have done is wishful thinking. As long as Islam remains Islam, the aspiration to a worldwide caliphate can be renewed in any generation, and eternal vigilance is required.

I also believe the West has lost its defense. It has removed the God of the Bible from the public square and made the honoring of other gods a badge of tolerance. A people that can no longer say what is right and wrong, good and evil, or who the true God is, cannot recognize a spiritual invader, let alone resist one. Islam's advocates have been invited in by leaders, and by a population, without spiritual discernment. That is a formula for slow-motion conquest: a bloodless change of power, achieved century by century, through the ballot box and through a tolerant people's willingness to cede ground.

Where is the evidence uncertain? Individual Muslims in the West vary greatly. Many are law-abiding, many are loyal citizens, and many live their faith privately; surveys in Britain find that most British Muslims were born there and identify as British. I do not doubt it. My concern is not with the individual Muslim neighbor but with the system of Islam, which is designed to outlast the loyalty of any one generation, and with what happens when its community grows large enough to govern itself apart from the nation around it. That is happening now in particular towns and boroughs of Britain and Europe, and it is the direction I fear for the West as a whole.

## The crusade I mean

When I say my campaign will include a crusade against Islam, I mean a crusade of the Word, not of the sword.

The Apostle Paul described the weapons of this war: *"For the weapons of our warfare are not carnal, but mighty through God to the pulling down of strong holds"* **(2 Corinthians 10:4)**. And he named the enemy: *"For we wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against the rulers of the darkness of this world, against spiritual wickedness in high places"* **(Ephesians 6:12)**. The Muslim is not my enemy. He is the captive I want to see set free. The only safe approach to Islam is constant, open, and loving proclamation of the Gospel, in obedience to Christ's command: *"Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost"* **(Matthew 28:19)**.

Force has its place, but only within the limits of a just war: against an actual attack, or the real threat of one. A war to liberate a people's hearts and minds is not won with armies. It is won by changing hearts.

Here is what this crusade asks of America:


 	- **Tell the truth about history.** Teach the Crusades in proportion, including their crimes, and teach the thousand years of Islamic conquest that preceded and followed them. Refuse to let a distorted past disarm the present.

 	- **Proclaim the Gospel openly.** Churches and believers should evangelize Muslims with courage and love, without apology and without fear, as the early church evangelized the pagan world.

 	- **Restore the God of the Bible to the public square.** A nation that will not name the true God cannot defend itself against a false one. Righteousness, not neutrality, is a nation's defense: *"Righteousness exalteth a nation: but sin is a reproach to any people"* **(Proverbs 14:34)**.

 	- **Make allegiance the condition of belonging.** No one should receive permanent standing in America who will not affirm that the Constitution and the laws of the land stand above any religious legal code, including sharia. Those who work to overturn that order should lose their status.

 	- **Defend with force only when attacked or threatened.** Meet violence with the full strength of the law and, where war is warranted, with arms; meet ideas with better ideas and with the truth.

 	- **Refuse the politics of guilt.** Acknowledge the excesses of the Crusades and put them in proportion. The drive to admit Islam into the heart of the nation as penance for the Crusades, or to prove one's tolerance and virtue, must be resisted.


The crusaders of the eleventh century went east with the sword and, in the end, lost everything they won. The early Christians went out into a hostile empire with nothing but the Gospel, and within three centuries that empire bowed its knee to Christ. I know which crusade I mean to join.

## Sources and their limits

This essay draws on the mainstream scholarship of the Crusades, especially the work of historians who have challenged the older view of the Crusades as simple aggression. That scholarship is careful, but it is not without debate, and I want to be plain about it.


 	- **The revisionist consensus.** Historians such as Jonathan Riley-Smith, Thomas Madden, and Thomas Asbridge have reshaped the field since the 1970s, emphasizing the defensive origins and religious motives of the Crusades. The older view, best represented by Sir Steven Runciman's three-volume history, judged the whole movement "a long act of intolerance in the name of God." Some scholars today still lean toward Runciman's judgment. I side with the revisionists.

 	- **Numbers.** Medieval figures for armies, massacres, and captives are unreliable on every side. The death toll at Jerusalem in 1099 and the number of Europeans enslaved by Barbary corsairs are scholarly estimates, not counts.

 	- **Muslim memory.** The claim that the Crusades were largely forgotten in the Muslim world before the nineteenth century is the standard view, drawn largely from the work of Carole Hillenbrand and others, but it has been challenged by historians such as Paul Cobb, who argue that Arabic sources from the late Middle Ages through the Ottoman era have not been fully studied.

 	- **Western sources.** Most Crusades historians work mainly from Latin, Greek, and other European sources. That gives the field a Western vantage point, even when its conclusions are critical of the West.

 	- **The Qur'an.** I quote the 1930 Pickthall translation. Muslims interpret Qur'an 9:29 in different ways, and I have stated the historical use of the verse, not the view of every Muslim today.

 	- **My convictions about Islam.** The section on Islam today states my own judgment about the nature of the system of Islam. It rests on its history, its texts, and what I see in Europe, but it is a conviction about the future, not a measured fact, and I have tried to state where the evidence is mixed.
