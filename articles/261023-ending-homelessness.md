# 261023 - Ending Homelessness

# Both Hands on the Problem: What Denver Teaches About Homelessness


*Dr. Thomas | 23 October 2026*


*Responding to German Lopez, [“We Know How to Fix Homelessness. Why Don’t More Cities Do It?”](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/13/opinion/homelessness-unhoused-denver-shelters.html?campaign_id=190&emc=edit_ufn_20261023&instance_id=184035&nl=from-the-times&regi_id=69706792&segment_id=228588&user_id=a7f564873fd7608c82f90080cbfecd2d) The New York Times, October 13, 2026.*


---


## My Position


I stand on the side of a two-handed policy on homelessness. With one hand, a city reclaims its streets, parks, and sidewalks for the public, and it enforces its laws against camping in them. With the other hand, it offers every person it moves a real place to go: a private, decent room, and a path to work, treatment, and permanent housing. Either hand alone fails. Enforcement without a door to walk through is cruelty. Shelter offered without any expectation that a man will leave the sidewalk is abandonment wearing the clothes of tolerance.


This is not a theory. Denver has been doing it for three years, and it is working.


## What Denver Did


When Mike Johnston became mayor of Denver in 2023, he set a goal of ending unsheltered homelessness in the city. His program, All In Mile High, pairs encampment clearing with expanded shelter, housing assistance, and mental health and addiction services. His rule is simple: when you move someone off the street, give him somewhere to go and the support to stay there.


The results, so far:


- **Street homelessness is down sharply.** The Urban Institute, which is independently evaluating the program, found that unsheltered homelessness in Denver fell 45 percent from January 2023 to January 2025. The city’s latest count puts the decline since 2023 at 64 percent.

- **Large encampments are nearly gone.** The Urban Institute found 98 percent fewer encampments of 20 or more people and 89 percent fewer of 10 to 20 people.

- **Overall homelessness has begun to fall.** The January 2026 count found total homelessness in Denver proper dropped from 7,327 to 6,411 people, a 12.5 percent decline and the first drop in nearly a decade.

- **People are moving through, not just in.** The city reports that since 2023 more than 8,500 people have entered shelter and about 7,700 have moved into permanent housing.


The shelters themselves matter. Denver did not simply open more gymnasiums full of cots. It built villages of tiny homes and bought hotels, so that each person has a locked door, a shower, and a place to cook. One former resident told the Times that in a traditional shelter he was assaulted within days; in the tiny-home village he finally had a home of his own. The mayor’s phrase for what he wanted was “a room of one’s own.” Staff spend their time preparing residents to leave: getting identification, connecting them to jobs, training, and housing assistance. Providers are judged by how many people they move forward.


The enforcement side is just as deliberate. Police, with social workers riding along, wake people in tents and explain the law: a verbal warning, then a written warning, then a ticket or arrest. The officer the Times accompanied said his aim was not to arrest anyone but to give people a reason to accept help. One morning, a couple from Texas told him they wanted to go home to family but had no money. Fifteen minutes later he handed them a bus ticket.


The mayor, a former school principal, explained it this way: a principal loves every child in the building, and when one child makes the others unsafe, the principal either helps him change or holds him accountable. That is the whole policy in one sentence.


## The Moral Test


On this site, every policy must answer three questions, in order. Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? And would I want it done to me?


### Is it righteous?


Scripture gives two commands that the homelessness debate usually sets against each other. The first is care for the poor. *“For the poor shall never cease out of the land: therefore I command thee, saying, Thou shalt open thine hand wide unto thy brother, to thy poor, and to thy needy”* **(Deuteronomy 15:11)**. *“He that oppresseth the poor reproacheth his Maker”* **(Proverbs 14:31)**. A city that sweeps a man’s tent into a garbage truck and gives him nowhere to sleep that night has oppressed the poor.


The second is justice and order in the common life. Parks, sidewalks, and libraries are held in trust for everyone: the mother with a stroller, the elderly man who walks for his health, the shop owner whose livelihood depends on a door customers can reach. When one person claims a public space as his permanent private dwelling, he takes something that belongs to his neighbors. Permitting that is not neutrality. It is a decision to let one neighbor’s need override every other neighbor’s rights, and to call that compassion.


Righteousness requires keeping both commands at once. The progressive error has been to keep the first and abandon the second. The opposite error is to keep the second and abandon the first. Denver’s policy is righteous because it refuses to choose.


### Is it loving?


Love is not the same as leaving a person alone.* “Thou shalt not hate thy brother in thine heart: thou shalt in any wise rebuke thy neighbour, and not suffer sin upon him”* **(Leviticus 19:17)**. A man dying slowly in a tent of fentanyl, exposure, and untreated psychosis is not exercising freedom in any meaningful sense. To humor him in that condition, because intervening is uncomfortable or politically costly, is not love. It is indifference.


But love also does not command without providing. Telling a man to move along when there is nowhere to move to is not discipline; it is expulsion. The Times reports that no one in Denver has refused an offer to move into the new facilities and that they run at or near capacity. People accept a real home when one is offered. The law’s firmness brings them to the door; the decency behind it keeps them there.


### Would I want it done to me?


Suppose I were the man in the tent, addicted and ashamed, too sick to see my way out. What would I want, judged not by that moment’s appetite but by the self I hope to be? I would want someone who cared enough to wake me and insist this couldn’t go on. I would want him to offer something real: a room with a lock, a shower, help with the papers I lost, a ticket home to the people who still love me. I would not want to be left alone to die, and I would not want to be hauled to a cell for the crime of having nowhere to sleep.


Denver’s policy passes that test. Enforcement-only policy fails it, and so does a policy of indefinite tolerance.


## The Law Now Permits Enforcement. That is not the same as Justifying It.


In June 2024, in *City of Grants Pass v. Johnson*, the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 that enforcing public camping bans does not violate the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment. The decision overturned the Ninth Circuit’s Martin v. Boise rule that a city could not punish people for sleeping outside when it lacked enough shelter beds.


I think the Court read the Constitution correctly. The Eighth Amendment governs the kind of punishment a government may impose, and a modest fine for camping is not cruel or unusual in that sense. Judges are not the right officials to manage a city’s shelter supply.


But a ruling that something is constitutional is not a ruling that it is right. After *Grants Pass*, a city may legally cite a man for sleeping in a park on a night when no bed exists anywhere in town. The question every mayor now faces is not “may we?” but “ought we, and how?” The answer righteousness gives is: not until you have offered him somewhere to go.


Here the citizen has a role. I regard jury nullification as a cornerstone of American liberty: the right of a jury to refuse to convict when applying the law would do injustice. If a citizen sits on a jury asked to convict a man of camping on a night when the city had no bed to offer him, I believe he would be right to vote to acquit. The law should be enforced, but only by a government that has first done its own part.


## What the Federal Government Should Do


Homelessness is chiefly a local problem, and it should be solved locally. Washington cannot run Denver’s tiny-home villages, and it should not try. But the federal government spends billions on homelessness programs through the Department of Housing and Urban Development and other agencies, and it has the right and the duty to ask what that money accomplishes.


In July 2025, President Trump signed Executive Order 14321, “Ending Crime and Disorder on America’s Streets.” It directs federal agencies to end support for “Housing First” policies, which provide housing without expecting treatment first, and it encourages states to broaden the use of civil commitment for people with serious mental illness or addiction. Supporters see it as a long-overdue demand for accountability; critics see it as a turn toward punishment and institutionalization.


I agree with the order where it insists on accountability, treatment, and recovery. A program that houses a man and asks for nothing of him mistakes shelter for restoration. But the order is dangerous when enforcement and commitment run ahead of the places where people go. Denver did not succeed by choosing a side in the Housing First debate. It succeeded by refusing to clear a camp until it had beds, and refusing to offer beds without also clearing the camp.


As president, I would advance these policies:


- **Fund results, not ideologies.** Federal homelessness grants should reward measured outcomes: people moved off the street, people still housed a year later, people working or in recovery. Cities should be free to use Housing First, treatment-first, or a blend, and judged by what happens to the people they serve.

- **Shelter before citation.** A city that receives federal homelessness funds and enforces a camping ban should have to show it offered the person a real place to go before citing them. This is the two-handed rule, written into the grant.

- **Private, dignified shelter.** Federal dollars should favor non-congregate shelter: tiny-home villages, converted hotels, small apartments. These are safer for residents, more acceptable to neighbors, and faster to build than traditional facilities. Federal environmental and procurement rules that slow these projects should be streamlined.

- **Treatment capacity, with due process.** The hardest cases on the street now involve serious mental illness and addiction, as Denver is discovering. The nation needs more psychiatric and recovery beds. Where civil commitment is necessary, it must remain tied to genuine danger to self or others, decided by a court, with counsel and periodic review. Being poor and outdoors is not a mental illness.

- **The way home.** Many people on the street have family somewhere who would take them in. A small, flexible federal fund for travel home and family reunification, like the bus ticket the Denver officer found, is among the cheapest and most humane tools available.

- **Free the churches and charities.** Religious congregations and ministries have served the homeless for centuries, and Denver’s mayor has drawn on them. Federal rules should never require a faith-based provider to abandon its faith to receive support for feeding, sheltering, or treating people, as long as no one must profess a belief to be served.

- **Build more housing.** The deepest cause of rising homelessness is the cost of a place to live. Denver’s own leaders admit that buying and renting remain out of reach for much of the poor and middle class. Federal policy should reward states and cities that loosen zoning and permitting so more homes get built. And as I have argued elsewhere on this site, abundant and affordable energy, the “E” in the Vision for a New ERA, lowers the cost of building, heating, and living in every home in America.

- **Count honestly.** The federal Point-in-Time count is a single night’s snapshot and misses many people. The federal government should fund better, continuous data so cities and citizens can see what works.


## Where the Evidence Is Uncertain


I hold the position above firmly, but the honest reader deserves to know where the evidence is incomplete. None of these points overturns the case; each tempers it.


- **The counts are imperfect.** The Point-in-Time count is a single night in January. Some advocates in Denver argue that enforcement has made people hide, so that fewer are counted rather than fewer exist. That is possible at the margin. But it cannot explain a 98 percent drop in large encampments, nor the thousands of people the city reports moving into permanent housing, nor the first fall in total homelessness in nearly a decade.

- **Sweeps alone do not work.** A University of Colorado study of Denver encampment clearings before 2023 found that sweeps by themselves did not reduce crime. That finding supports the two-handed approach rather than undercutting it: the earlier sweeps lacked the second hand.

- **The cost is real and disputed.** The city’s auditor estimated that All In Mile High cost about $178 million over two years, $20 million more than the city had reported. Much of the money came from temporary federal funds that have since expired, and Denver now faces a budget shortfall. Whether the effort can be sustained is not yet known.

- **The hardest cases remain.** Chronic homelessness in the Denver metro area was at a record high in 2025. Those still outside are disproportionately mentally ill or addicted, and the next phase will be harder than the first.

- **Leadership may not transfer.** The Times columnist concluded that the mayor’s determination was the decisive factor. A policy can be copied; will cannot.


## Why Other Cities Have Not Done This


If the approach works, why do so many cities still have encampments on their sidewalks? Several explanations are plausible, and none requires assuming anyone’s bad faith.


**Ideology over results.** Many officials have committed themselves to the view that enforcement is inherently cruel and that homelessness is purely an economic problem. Admitting that a firm push is often necessary means admitting the theory is incomplete. On the other side, some officials favor enforcement because it shows action, and resist the expense of building places to send people.


**Money that follows the problem.** Homelessness has become a large funding stream for agencies and nonprofits. Most of the people in that system are sincere. But an organization funded by how many people it serves, rather than how many it moves out of homelessness, has little structural reason to shrink its caseload. Denver’s practice of judging providers by how many people they move forward corrects that incentive.


**Neighbors who want the camp gone but the shelter elsewhere.** The Times reported that some residents near Denver’s tiny-home village point cameras at it. Every city that has tried to build shelter has met this resistance. A city cannot clear its camps humanely if every neighborhood vetoes the places people would go.


**Political fear.** Enforcement angers one constituency; spending angers another; building angers a third. A mayor who does all three needs conviction. Doing nothing angers everyone a little and no one enough to remove him.


## Conclusion


Mayor Johnston observed that when people see a problem every day and nothing is done, they turn either on their neighbors or on democracy itself. When people solve a hard problem together, he said, they learn to turn to each other instead.


I believe he is right, and I believe the lesson reaches beyond homelessness. A free people governs itself by holding together justice and mercy, order and love. Our public spaces belong to all of us. The man in the tent is our neighbor and our brother. We owe him both a firm word and an open door. Denver has shown that a city can give both. Other cities, and the federal government that helps fund them, should learn from it.


I hold this position as an argument, not a decree. If someone can show me a better way to keep both commands at once, I want to hear it.
