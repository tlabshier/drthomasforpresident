# Cost Control Dressed as Compassion


### A presidential position paper on government healthcare and assisted death — what Canada teaches America


Thomas Lee Abshier, ND — 1 November 2026


*Occasioned by a Facebook post from my friend Rick, written to Canadians about their “free” universal healthcare and the expansion of Medical Assistance in Dying (MAiD). Rick argues that a system which rations care and then offers death as a remedy for the suffering it failed to treat is practicing cost control dressed up as benevolence. I agree with him, and I want to set out why, with the evidence, the places where the evidence is soft, and what it means for the United States.*


---


## Plain language summary


Canada’s healthcare is not free; Canadians pay for it through taxes, about $9,000 per person per year. It is not truly universal either, because the median patient now waits more than half a year from referral to treatment. When a government is the only payer, it must ration care by decree, since it cannot ration by price. Into that system, Canada introduced assisted death in 2016, promised as a narrow option for the dying. In less than ten years, it has grown to more than one in twenty Canadian deaths, opened to people who are not dying, and Parliament is now considering letting a doctor end a patient’s life later, on the strength of a request he signed years earlier and can no longer confirm. I do not claim that Canadian officials sat down and planned to kill people to save money. My claim is narrower and, I think, harder to dismiss: when the same authority that controls the budget for care also offers death, it has a conflict of interest that no regulation can fully cure. America should never build that structure. As president, I would use the voice of the office to keep healthcare local and personal, keep federal money out of assisted death, and urge every state to protect the sick from being offered death in place of care.


---


## Part One: Where I stand


I stand on Rick’s side of this question. A healthcare system run by a single government payer must ration, and a government that rations care has no business offering death to the people waiting in its lines.


I will be specific about where the evidence is firm and where it is not. The wait-time numbers are firm. The growth of MAiD and the widening of its eligibility are firm. The documented cases of death being raised with people who asked for help are firm. What cannot be proven is motive: no one can show that a particular death was chosen because it was cheaper. But the argument does not depend on proving motive. It depends on recognizing a structure that rewards the wrong outcome, and on asking whether a righteous people should build such a structure at all. I believe the answer is no, and I’m open to anyone with a better argument.


---


## Part Two: “Free” is a price you are not shown


The Canadian Institute for Health Information estimated that Canada’s total health spending in 2024 reached about $372 billion, or $9,054 per person, amounting to 12.4% of GDP. That money comes from the same people who are told their care is free. It is collected through income taxes, sales taxes, and payroll levies, and it is spent by people the patient never meets.


The word “free” does two kinds of damage. First, it hides the cost, so citizens cannot weigh what they get against what they pay. Second, it severs the patient from the purchase. A man who pays for his care directly can walk out of a clinic that treats him poorly. A man whose care has been prepaid through taxes can only wait, petition, or leave the country.


In fairness to the Canadian reader, I must add that Americans have no ground for smugness. The United States spends considerably more per person than Canada, and our insurance companies ration care through denials, delays, and exclusions. I wrote about that in 2009, in my paper [Government Healthcare](https://drthomasforpresident.com/2019/03/government-healthcare/), and I have not changed my view. The lesson I draw from Canada is not that the American system is good. It is that replacing insurance-company rationing with government rationing exchanges one master for a more powerful one.


---


## Part Three: “Universal” is a promise of a place in line


The Fraser Institute has measured Canadian wait times since 1993. Its 2025 report found a median wait of 28.6 weeks from GP referral to the end of treatment, down from the 30-week record set in 2024. That 2025 figure is 208% longer than the 9.3 weeks recorded in 1993. The diagnostic waits are similar: a median of 18.1 weeks for an MRI and 8.8 weeks for a CT scan. About 1.4 million procedures were waited for in 2025.


Some patients do not reach the end of the line. [SecondStreet.org](http://www.SecondStreet.org), a Canadian think tank, files freedom-of-information requests with provincial health authorities each year. Its most recent report counted at least 23,746 patients who died while waiting for surgeries or diagnostic scans in fiscal 2024–25. Two cautions are necessary. The count includes people who died of causes unrelated to their wait, so it is not a count of deaths *caused* by waiting. And part of the year-over-year increase may reflect more agencies supplying data; among agencies that reported in both years, the increase was about 3%. But the count is also incomplete, because several provinces do not track the data at all. A system that cannot say how many of its patients die in its queues has not earned the word “universal.”


---


## Part Four: Rationing by command


In 2009 I wrote, “healthcare is a finite productive resource, and it must be rationed either by price or command.” That remains the heart of the matter. Medical needs are effectively unlimited; doctors, nurses, beds, and scanners are not. In a market, price balances the two. When price is removed, something else must take its place: a committee, a budget, a waiting list, and a bureaucrat’s priority score.


The defenders of single-payer healthcare call this efficiency, and in one narrow sense they are right: a monopoly buyer can hold down what it pays. But budget efficiency is not patient efficiency. The cost has not vanished. It has been moved off the government’s ledger and onto the patient’s body, in the form of months spent in pain, a job lost while waiting for a hip, or a cancer that advanced while the referral sat in a queue.


Rick is right that this is the logic of central planning. Central planners do not intend shortages; they produce them, because they have removed the signal that tells producers what to make and consumers what to forgo.


---


## Part Five: Whoever pays, decides


Rick’s sharpest point is that the system was never only about compassion. It was about control. I would put it this way: a single payer is a single decider. Whoever holds the medicine budget decides which treatments are covered, which drugs are approved for reimbursement, which doctors are licensed to practice, and how long a sick man must wait before anyone will see him.


That is an enormous concentration of power over the most intimate part of a person’s life, his body. During the COVID pandemic, Canadians saw how quickly the levers of a centralized health system can be turned from serving citizens to directing them. Whatever one concludes about the policies of those years, the principle is clear: a government that controls access to care controls the person who needs it.


The Founders understood this kind of concentration as a danger. The Tenth Amendment leaves to the states, the communities, and the people every power not given to the national government. Healthcare is not among those powers. The farther a decision is made from the patient, the less the decision-maker knows about him, and the less he is accountable to him.


---


## Part Six: What MAiD was promised to be, and what it has become


Canada legalized Medical Assistance in Dying in 2016 for adults whose natural death had become “reasonably foreseeable.” The promise was narrow: relief for the dying in their final suffering. Few people could argue with the scenario as it was presented.


Then the boundaries moved.


**2021.** After a Quebec court struck down the “reasonably foreseeable” requirement, Parliament passed Bill C-7, creating a second track for people whose death is *not* reasonably foreseeable — people who are disabled or chronically ill but not dying. The same law scheduled an expansion to people whose only condition is mental illness.


**2023.** A special joint committee of Parliament recommended extending MAiD to “mature minors.” I must be accurate here, because this point is often overstated. The Criminal Code still requires a person to be at least 18 to receive MAiD, and the Ministry of Health says no legislative proposal to change that is before Parliament. But the committee’s final report did recommend amending the Criminal Code to include minors judged to have decision-making capacity; those recommendations are non-binding and have not been enacted. The recommendation exists. The law does not. It is fair to say that the proposal has been made at the highest level of Parliament and that its sponsors have not abandoned it.


**2024.** Health Canada reported 16,499 MAiD provisions, which equals 5.1% of all deaths in Canada. Of these, 732 were Track 2 cases, people whose death was not reasonably foreseeable.


**October 2026.** The government tabled Bill C-41, called the MAID Safeguards Act. It does two things, and I will report both. On the side of restraint, it would halt, indefinitely, the expansion of MAiD to people whose only condition is mental illness, which had been scheduled for March 17, 2027. That is a genuine retreat, and I credit it. On the other hand, it would permit advance requests. The patient must have a diagnosis of an illness expected to destroy his capacity to decide, and the death cannot be carried out until he has lost all capacity to consent, is suffering unbearably, and is in irreversible decline.


Consider what that means. The person who signed the request is, under the law, no longer present to confirm it. A practitioner decides when, judging the inner suffering of someone who can no longer describe it. The bill says a practitioner must stop if the patient resists by words, sounds, or gestures, which concedes the problem: the safeguard is to watch whether the patient objects as the procedure begins. Rick asks who decides when a life is no longer worth supporting. He answers, “government bureaucrats.” More precisely, clinicians act under a law written by the government and within a system financed by the government. The decision has passed from the person to the system.


---


## Part Seven: Offered death when asking for help


Rick mentions reports of people being offered MAiD when they sought treatment. The best-documented case is Christine Gauthier, a retired Canadian Army corporal and former Paralympian. She testified before Parliament that while describing her worsening condition to a Veterans Affairs Canada caseworker, the caseworker told her they could help her with assisted dying and offered to supply the equipment. She had been asking for a wheelchair ramp for her home, and she had waited about five years.


The government’s answer deserves to be heard. Minister Lawrence MacAulay told Parliament that an internal investigation found four instances of MAiD being offered to veterans between 2019 and May 2022, all by one employee, and that it was not widespread or systemic. The prime minister called the offer absolutely unacceptable.


I accept that one employee is not the whole system. But consider what made that employee’s conduct possible. A veteran asks the state for a ramp. The state, which controls the ramp’s budget and installation timeline, also administers a legal program of death. The employee who speaks to her has both options in front of him. One costs money and requires years of paperwork. The other is lawful, available, and final. The fault lies with the man who made the offer, but the system put the two options on the same desk.


Health Canada’s own data show what weighs on those who choose MAiD. Among Track 2 recipients in 2024, 44.7% cited isolation or loneliness as a source of their suffering, and about half in both tracks cited feeling like a burden on family, friends, or caregivers. Loneliness and the sense of being a burden are real forms of suffering. They are also exactly the sufferings that a family, a church, and a community exist to relieve. When the state’s answer to loneliness is death, it has confessed it has no other answer.


Ontario’s Chief Coroner reviewed MAiD deaths in that province and published reports in October 2024. As summarized by psychiatrist Sonu Gaind, the data showed that 28.4% of Track 2 recipients came from the lowest-income fifth of neighborhoods, compared with 21.5% of Track 1 recipients, and 48.3% of Track 2 recipients came from the fifth with the least stable housing. The reports also described some Track 2 recipients with inadequately treated mental illness or addiction, and others who sought MAiD because of social or housing vulnerability.


---


## Part Eight: The conflict of interest


In October 2020, as Bill C-7 was being debated, Canada’s Parliamentary Budget Officer produced a cost estimate. It projected that provincial health budgets would save $149 million in 2021, largely through lower spending on end-of-life care, with an additional 1,164 MAiD deaths expected under the revised law.


Fairness requires the rest of the record. The PBO noted that the savings amounted to 0.08% of provincial health spending, and cautioned that the report should not be read as suggesting MAiD be used to reduce costs. Those are honest caveats, and I take them at face value. The savings are small against the whole budget, and I do not claim the program was designed to reduce costs.


But notice what the report reveals. The government that controls the healthcare budget calculated, in advance, that more deaths would mean lower costs. The sum is small at the national level. It is not small at the level where decisions are actually made: the hospital ward with a bed shortage, the long-term care home with a waiting list, the caseworker with a backlog of ramp requests. Institutions respond to pressure at the margin, and the pressure on a rationed system always runs in one direction.


That is why I call it a conflict of interest. We do not allow a judge to rule on a case in which he has a financial stake, even if he is an honest man. We do not need to prove he was bribed. We disqualify him because the structure invites corruption, and because the people who come before him deserve a judge whose interests are not set against their own. A single-payer health system that offers death is a judge with a stake in the verdict. Rick’s blunt summary — “it’s cheaper to kill ’em than to care for ’em” — is not a claim about anyone’s intention. It describes the incentive.


---


## Part Nine: The moral test


The law and the economics matter, but they rest on a moral foundation, and I want to state it plainly. I evaluate every policy by three questions, in this order.


**First: is it righteous?** Is it consistent with what God has established? Life is not the property of the state, and it is not finally the property of the individual either; it is a trust from God. The command not to murder does not lapse when the victim consents or when a physician administers the act. A society may relieve suffering by every means short of killing the sufferer. It may give pain relief generously, even when that relief shortens life as a side effect. It may stop futile treatment and allow natural death. It may not make death itself the treatment. Assisted death fails this first test, and that alone settles the question for me.


**Second: is it loving?** Even setting the first test aside, MAiD fails the second. Love for a suffering neighbor means presence, relief of pain, a ramp for his wheelchair, a visit for his loneliness, and the assurance that he is not a burden but a person worth carrying. To say to a lonely, disabled, or poor man, “We can help you die,” is to tell him that the community has assessed his life and found it more expensive than it is worth. No one hears that offer as love.


**Third: would I want it done to me?** Put yourself in Christine Gauthier’s place. You have served your country, you have lost the use of your legs, and you have waited five years for a ramp. You call the agency responsible for your care, and the person on the line offers you death. I would not want that done to me, nor to my wife, my children, or my friends. Neither would you.


---


## Part Ten: Why the concern remains plausible


Some will answer that the large majority of MAiD cases involve cancer patients near death, that most received palliative care, and that the abuses are rare. Some of that is true, and I address it in the next section. But I want to explain why Rick’s concern is not paranoia.


**The intuitive sense that something is wrong.** For two and a half millennia, the Hippocratic tradition held that a physician does not give a deadly drug to anyone who asks for it. When a doctor, a nurse, or a caseworker raises death with a patient who came for help, almost everyone feels that a line has been crossed. That instinct is a moral perception, not a prejudice, and it should be respected.


**The money motive.** The government’s own budget office calculated savings from more deaths. A rationed system lives under constant pressure to reduce cost. Institutions under financial pressure respond to incentives, whether or not any individual within them intends harm.


**The link between the system and the behavior.** When the same authority funds care, rations care, and authorizes death, the three functions cannot be kept apart in practice. The ramp and the lethal injection come from the same budget and are approved within the same bureaucracy.


**The speed of expansion.** In ten years, Canada moved from death for the dying, to death for the disabled who are not dying, to a parliamentary recommendation for “mature minors,” to a scheduled expansion for mental illness, to a bill for advance requests carried out on people who can no longer consent. One expansion has now been paused. The direction of the other changes has not reversed. A boundary that has moved four times in a decade is not a boundary.


**The pattern of who chooses it.** The government’s own data show that loneliness and the feeling of being a burden weigh heavily on those who choose MAiD, and the Ontario coroner’s data show that recipients whose death is not foreseeable come disproportionately from poorer and less securely housed neighborhoods. Those are precisely the people who suffer most when care is rationed.


---


## Part Eleven: Where the evidence is uncertain


I hold my position firmly, and I owe the reader an honest account of where the evidence is incomplete.


- **Most MAiD deaths are in the dying.** About 95.6% of 2024 cases were Track 1, where death was reasonably foreseeable, and 74.1% of recipients received palliative care. The worst abuses are concentrated in a small share of cases.

- **Correlation is not causation.** The Ontario neighborhood data show who receives MAiD, not why. Poverty and poor health travel together for many reasons.

- **The wait-list death count counts deaths during waits**, not proof that each death was caused by the wait.

- **Canadians largely support MAiD as originally enacted.** Polling shows strong support for the 2016 law and much softer support for its expansions. Rick speaks for a real constituency, but not yet a majority.

- **Canada has pulled back in one area.** Bill C-41 halts the mental-illness expansion, and the government says it is not pursuing MAiD for minors.


None of these qualifications removes the central problem. A system that offers death to the dying, with good palliative care, is still a system with the power and the incentive to offer it more widely. The pause on mental illness shows that public resistance works; it does not show that the pressure is gone. The fact that most cases are in the dying does not console the veteran who asked for a ramp. My thesis stands: a government that rations care should not dispense death, and a people who wish to remain free should not give it that power.


---


## Part Twelve: What this means for America


Rick wrote to Canadians. I am writing to Americans, because the American left continues to propose single-payer healthcare as an act of compassion, and because a growing number of our own states, including my state of Montana through the 2009 court decision in *Baxter v. Montana*, have opened the door to assisted death. We are being offered the same package Canada accepted, wrapped in the same words.


As president, I would advocate the following. These are positions I hold, and I would argue for them as a leader with reasons, not impose them as final authority.


- **No federal single-payer healthcare.** Healthcare is not among the powers the Constitution delegates to the national government. It belongs to states, communities, and, above all, individuals and families. I would oppose any federal takeover of health insurance, under any name.

- **Restore the patient as the purchaser.** Every citizen should provide for his own routine care through a Medical Savings Account, and should be expected, as a civic duty, to carry catastrophic coverage for trauma, serious disease, and end-of-life care. When the patient pays, the patient can leave, and the provider must earn his trust.

- **Return charity to the community.** Care for the poor, the disabled, and the dying is a duty of families, churches, and local communities first, and of local and state government second. When the giver knows the receiver, care stays personal and accountable.

- **Keep federal money out of assisted death.** Federal law already bars federal funding of assisted suicide. I would defend that line without exception in Medicare, Medicaid, the military health system, and above all the Department of Veterans Affairs. No American veteran should ever hear from his own government what Christine Gauthier heard from hers.

- **Use the voice of the office to urge the states.** The states that have legalized assisted death have done so within their own authority. I would not try to override them by federal decree. I would use the voice of the presidency to urge their citizens and legislators to repeal those laws, and in the meantime to forbid any physician, nurse, or caseworker from raising death with a patient who has not raised it himself.

- **Fund the alternatives.** Palliative care, hospice, pain relief, home support, and the presence of family and friends are the righteous and loving answer to suffering. A nation that spends generously on these will rarely hear a request for death.


---


## Closing


Rick ends his post by saying that Canadians should question the money and power they have handed over before the house of cards falls. I would give Americans the same warning before we hand it over.


The promise of free and universal care is attractive because it appeals to something good in us: the desire that no neighbor go without help. But a promise that cannot be kept becomes a system of rationing, and a system of rationing that is also permitted to offer death has placed the weakest among us before a judge with a stake in the verdict. Real compassion is costly. It builds the ramp, sits with the lonely, relieves the pain, and stays until the end. That is what love requires, and it is the standard I will hold a government to.


I hold this position, and I hold it open. If someone can show me a single-payer system that does not ration, or a regime of assisted death that does not widen, I will read his argument with care.
