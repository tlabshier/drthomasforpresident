# 260907 - 911 and Whistleblowers

# Don’t Kill Your Mavericks – Position Paper


*On “The Man Who Knew,” PBS Frontline, October 2002, and the career of FBI Special Agent John O’Neill*


Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | September 7, 2026


In January 1995, agents flew Ramzi Yousef into New York in a helicopter, blindfolded. Yousef had built the bomb that went off under the World Trade Center in 1993. As the helicopter came over Manhattan, an agent asked permission to remove the blindfold. Yousef’s eyes fixed on the towers, and the agent beside him nudged him and said, “You see, it still stands.” Yousef answered, without heat, that it would not have — if they’d had more funding.


The man who had run the operation to catch him, working the phones for three days without leaving his office, was a new section chief named John O’Neill. Six and a half years later, O’Neill was found under a collapsed stairwell beneath the South Tower. He had been chief of security at the World Trade Center for nineteen days. The FBI had pushed him out of the Bureau two weeks before that.


I want to write about what happened in between, because it is the clearest case I know of a pattern I have been describing all week, in pesticide records and grant files and fraud investigations: an institution that has the information it needs, and a person inside it who understands what the information means, and a machinery that removes the person rather than acting on the information.


## What He Saw


O’Neill came to counterterrorism late, at forty-two, after working on white-collar crime and drug cases. He read everything. Within a year he had reached a conclusion almost no one else in the government held: that Osama bin Laden was not a financier of terrorism, as the intelligence community then described him, but the builder of a worldwide network whose purpose was attacking the United States. He drew the connections — a phone number in Yousef’s safe house, a training manual titled *al-Qaeda*, the Bojinka plot to bring down twelve airliners, a confessed plot to fly a plane into a federal building, Middle Eastern men training at American flight schools in 1995. Richard Clarke at the White House said he first heard the name bin Laden from O’Neill. So did Robert “Bear” Bryant, who would become the FBI’s deputy director.


O’Neill said, years before it was fashionable, that al-Qaeda had cells inside the United States. He could not get most field offices to look. He said the Saudis were not cooperating on the Khobar Towers bombing, and told Director Freeh so on a plane in language the Director did not appreciate. He was right; at the highest level, the Saudis had decided to give the FBI only part of the picture. He called the East Africa embassy bombings for al-Qaeda within hours of seeing them on CNN. He was right again. He predicted an attack on an American warship. Al-Qaeda hit the Cole in October 2000.


When the millennium came, his office ran down the Ressam plot from a border crossing in Washington State to a co-conspirator in Brooklyn, and two million people stood in Times Square without incident. By February 2000, Clarke says FBI leadership had finally concluded that O’Neill had been right about the cells. It had taken five years.


## What They Did About It


Here is what the institution did with the man who kept being right.


When the embassies were bombed, O’Neill’s New York office — the one with six years of al-Qaeda casework — asked to lead the investigation. The deputy director who supported him was on vacation and out of cell range. The acting decision-maker, Tom Pickard, a longtime friend of the Director’s from the criminal side of the house, gave the case to Washington. Two weeks later, when it was obvious that O’Neill had been right about who did it, headquarters reversed itself and handed the investigation to his agents — but still would not send him.


The reorganization O’Neill and Bryant had pushed for years — an analytic branch of the Bureau, separate from the criminal division, with the technology to know what the FBI knew — was never funded. Bryant put it plainly: the FBI never knew what it knew.


When the Director’s job in New York opened up, they passed O’Neill over. His record included a lost Bureau cell phone, a lost Palm Pilot, and an incident in which his old Buick broke down and he borrowed a Bureau car from a safe house to drive his companion home. Headquarters opened a formal inquiry. They logged letting her use the bathroom in the safe house as a security breach.


Then he left his briefcase in a room full of FBI agents at a conference in Florida, stepped outside to take a call, and came back to find it gone. It contained classified material he should not have had outside the office. They recovered it within hours, and fingerprint analysis showed nothing had been touched. The Office of Professional Responsibility opened a criminal investigation. It stayed open for thirteen months.


When the *Cole* was hit, headquarters resisted sending him to Yemen; it took the New York office chief hours to persuade the Director. In Yemen, O’Neill and the military wanted a hard perimeter around several hundred investigators in what one agent described as a fifteenth-century country full of AK-47 fire. The ambassador wanted good relations with the Yemeni government and full control over every American in the country. She lobbied the Director against him. When O’Neill came home for Thanksgiving, twenty-five pounds lighter, she denied his visa to return. FBI headquarters did not fight it. Yemeni officials had taken to calling him the brother; without him, the investigation slowed to a crawl, and one of the suspects he had been waiting to question, Fahd al-Quso, turned out to know about a meeting in Malaysia attended by two men who would fly Flight 77 into the Pentagon.


In the summer of 2001, with the intelligence community warning of a major attack in the coming weeks, the Phoenix memo about flight schools reached headquarters. It didn’t go to New York. The Minnesota office’s August search for Moussaoui’s laptop also didn’t reach New York. The FBI’s most experienced counterterrorism office was, in the documentary’s words, out of the loop. And thirteen months after the briefcase, with the investigation still technically open, someone leaked it to the *New York Times*. O’Neill’s companion says he confronted the man he believed had done it — Pickard, by then interim Director — and was told, absolutely not, I would never hurt you. O’Neill retired at the end of August. On his last day he stayed until six in the evening to sign the order sending his agents back into Yemen, because he had promised them he would.


A friend told him the security job at the Trade Center would be easy, since nobody would bomb the place twice. O’Neill said no — they had always wanted to finish the job.


## Two Things a Position Paper Has to Say Honestly


**O’Neill was not a saint, and the record should not be flattened into one.** He took classified material out of the building. He borrowed a government car. He separated from his wife and didn’t tell the woman he lived with for two or three years that he was married. By every account, including his friends’, he was arrogant, flamboyant, and hard on the people above him. The infractions were real.


The question is not whether they were real. It is whether an institution should be permitted to select, from among its people, the one who is chronically right and chronically inconvenient, and then apply to him a standard of administrative perfection it applies to no one else, until the file is thick enough to leak. That is not discipline. It is a method, and every large organization has one.


**This is not a paper about whether 9/11 could have been prevented, and I will not pretend it is.** The 9/11 Commission found failures at the CIA, the FAA, the INS, and the White House as well as the FBI; the two hijackers from the Malaysia meeting were on no watch list until late August 2001 because of a failure that was not O’Neill’s or his office’s to fix. Chris Isham, who knew him as well as anyone, said only that O’Neill would have had a fighting chance in Yemen if he had been allowed to do his job, and that we will never know. That is the honest ceiling on the claim, and I hold to it.


The record does establish something narrower and not dependent on counterfactuals: the information existed, the man who could read it existed, and the institution spent its energy on his briefcase.


## The Pattern


Every paper I have written this week has this shape. A whistleblower reported the Feeding Our Future ringleader in 2018 and was referred elsewhere; the money kept flowing. A CDC scientist’s undisclosed conflict was resolved by a reprimand from her own supervisor that expired in two years. Landscaping contractors in California did not keep the pesticide records the law required, and the homeowners who asked were called troublemakers. In each case, the institution could address the substance or manage the person raising it, and it chose the latter.


The FBI’s version had a specific mechanism, and it is worth naming because it generalizes. The Office of Professional Responsibility is meant to police misconduct. In O’Neill’s case, it functioned as a way for the people he had embarrassed to convert his style into a file. Bear Bryant, who ran the Bureau’s operations, described the logic without irony: they don’t want to promote somebody with a cloud over him, even a minor thing like a vehicle. The cloud is the point. Once the disciplinary machinery is available to the chain of command as a tool against a subordinate it finds threatening, every mistake is an opportunity, and a man who works a hundred hours a week will eventually make one.


Bryant’s father, a Bureau man himself, had a rule: don’t kill your mavericks; they might save your life someday. Bryant said it on camera, about O’Neill, a year after the towers fell.


## Positions


- **A dissent channel with a required answer.** Any agent or analyst may escalate a threat assessment directly to the Director and the Inspector General, on the record, and the Director must respond in writing within thirty days. The State Department has had a version since 1971. The FBI had a section chief who could reach the Attorney General only by having her aide dial his cell phone and hope headquarters did not find out.

- **Independent review of discipline against anyone in an active dispute with his chain of command.** When the Inspector General opens a disciplinary inquiry against an employee who, within the prior year, formally disagreed with a superior on a matter of substance, the Inspector General reviews the inquiry for proportionality and motive before it proceeds. The people O’Neill had embarrassed should not have been the people investigating his briefcase.

- **A clock on every disciplinary file.** Ninety days to charge or close, extendable once with written justification. A criminal inquiry left open for thirteen months without a charge is not an investigation. It holds a man’s career hostage, waiting for a use.

- **Leaking a disciplinary file is misconduct in itself, investigated by the IG.** Whoever gave the *Times* the briefcase story ended a twenty-five-year career to do so. That act should have been the subject of an inquiry, but it wasn’t.

- **Expertise governs assignment.** Command of a major investigation goes to the office holding the relevant casework, by rule, unless the Director states in writing why it should not. Turf disputes over office of origin, decided by whoever happens to be in the operations center on a given afternoon, cost weeks in East Africa and decided nothing on the merits.

- **No unilateral ambassadorial veto over a federal investigation of an attack on Americans.** Chief-of-mission authority is real, and it should remain. But when the ambassador and the investigating agency cannot agree on the presence of a federal investigator in the country where Americans have been killed, the dispute goes to the President within seventy-two hours. It isn’t decided by whoever lobbies the Director hardest.

- **Threat information automatically routes to the office of origin for that threat.** The Phoenix memo should have been in New York the day it reached headquarters. The Joint Terrorism Task Forces and the National Counterterrorism Center built after 2001 exist to prevent exactly this; they should be audited annually for whether they still do, because institutions re-silo as surely as water finds its level.

- **Distinguish the infraction from the man.** No disciplinary history consisting of administrative violations — lost equipment, procedural lapses, the use of a vehicle — may be dispositive against a documented record of operational performance in a promotion or assignment decision. It may be considered. It may not be the reason.


## Closing


The reform O’Neill and Bryant wanted — an intelligence arm of the FBI, separate from the criminal side, able to know what the Bureau knew — was created in 2005. The wall between intelligence and criminal investigators came down in 2002. The task forces were expanded, the center was built, the watch lists were merged. They did nearly everything he argued for. It was done because three thousand people died, and one of them was him.


Yousef said the tower would not have stood if he’d had more money. O’Neill spent six years trying to make sure the next man did not get the chance. In exchange, the institution took from him a car, a briefcase, a promotion, a visa, and finally the job.


Don’t kill your mavericks. They may save your life someday. Or they may end up working at the place they warned you about because you left them nowhere else to go.
