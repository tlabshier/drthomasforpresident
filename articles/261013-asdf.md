# 261013 - Love Thy Neighbor as Thyself

# As Thyself: The Measure of Love Is Learned from the Inside


*Dr. Thomas — Essay, October 2026*


When we began writing position papers for this site, we judged every policy by one question: would my neighbor, receiving this act in the public square, have reason to know he was loved? The question pointed the right way, but something was missing. Love not governed by anything above it drifts toward whatever the crowd or the ruler finds pleasing. So we put righteousness first, the R in A New ERA, and ordered the test the way Jesus ordered the commandments: love God first, then love your neighbor.


Reading back over our articles, I still felt something missing. The second commandment does not end with *“love thy neighbour.”* It ends with “as thyself.” Those two words are a measuring rod, and we had not yet said how the measurement is made. So I added a third question to our test, and this essay explains why it belongs there and what it requires.


The test now reads:


- **Is it righteous?** Does it conform to what God has established and will judge?

- **If so, is it loving?** Would my neighbor, receiving this act in the public square, have reason to know he was loved?

- **Would I want it done to me?** I can feel the pain of what my neighbor would feel in my own body, mind, and heart. Do I want to experience that because it is righteous?


And the standard for “loving” is not my feelings or his, but the way Christ loved — truthfully, sacrificially, without flattery and without cruelty.


## Love is known from the inside


I cannot look inside another man and see whether he feels loved. I can only guess from the outside. But I do know, from the inside, what it is to be in a body that hurts, a heart that is ashamed, and a mind that has been deceived. I know what it is to be flattered and later find out the flattery cost me. I know what it is to be told a hard truth by someone who cared about me, and I know the difference between that and being told the same truth by someone who wanted to see me humbled.


That inner knowledge is the instrument God gave us for loving others. Scripture assumes it: *“For no man ever yet hated his own flesh; but nourisheth and cherisheth it”* **(Ephesians 5:29)**. Jesus builds on it: *“All things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them: for this is the law and the prophets”* **(Matthew 7:12)**. The second question asks whether my neighbor would know he was loved. The third question tells me how to answer it: put myself in his place, take what is about to be done to him into my own body, mind, and heart, and ask honestly whether I would want it.


## Which self?


Here is the difficulty. If “as thyself” means “as I want to be treated right now,” the rule becomes license. The drunkard wants the bottle handed to him. The adulterer wants his friends to keep his secret and say nothing. The man who is spending himself into ruin wants to be told he deserves it. If I measure my neighbor by my appetites, I will hand him whatever destroys him, because that is what my appetites would ask for.


So the self that does the measuring matters. It cannot be the self that wants what is unrighteous because it feels good in the moment. That self is real, and every one of us carries it, but its wants lead to bitterness, shame, regret, sickness, and death. *“There is a way which seemeth right unto a man, but the end thereof are the ways of death”* **(Proverbs 14:12)**. Paul asks the believers in Rome to look back at what they once wanted: *“What fruit had ye then in those things whereof ye are now ashamed? for the end of those things is death”* **(Romans 6:21)**.


The self that measures rightly is the self committed to righteousness, the self that has already loved God first. That self has learned to ask not “what do I want now?” but “what will I be glad, at the end, that someone did for me?” Moses chose this way, *“choosing rather to suffer affliction with the people of God, than to enjoy the pleasures of sin for a season”* **(Hebrews 11:25)**. Christ Himself, *“for the joy that was set before him endured the cross”* **(Hebrews 12:2)**. The measure is taken from the end, not from the moment.


This is why the third question ends as it does: *Do I want to experience that because it is righteous?* I may not want the pain. No one does. *“No chastening for the present seemeth to be joyous, but grievous: nevertheless afterward it yieldeth the peaceable fruit of righteousness”* **(Hebrews 12:11)**. The righteous self can want what hurts, because it wants what the hurt produces.


## When my neighbor does not share the commitment


What about the neighbor who is not committed to righteousness, who would gladly be left alone with his appetites? Does the third question mean I must give him what he wants, since that is what he would want done to him?


No. The question is not what feels good to him from where he now stands. It is what would feel good to his soul from eternity: the soul saved from the torment of consequences he did not realize were coming, or thought he could avoid, or simply decided to ignore to please himself in the moment. That soul, looking back, will not thank the man who stood by and smiled. It will thank the man who warned him.


I measure my neighbor by that soul because I measure myself by mine. Looking back on my own life, I am not grateful for the people who told me what I wanted to hear. I am grateful for the ones who loved me enough to risk my displeasure.


## Silence is a form of hatred


The verse that gives us “love thy neighbour as thyself” does not stand alone. Read the verse just before it:


*“Thou shalt not hate thy brother in thine heart: thou shalt in any wise rebuke thy neighbour, and not suffer sin upon him. Thou shalt not avenge, nor bear any grudge against the children of thy people, but thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself: I am the LORD”* **(Leviticus 19:17–18)**.


God sets two things against each other here. On one side is hatred kept in the heart. On the other is rebuke spoken aloud. The man who sees his neighbor in sin and says nothing has not chosen love over judgment. He has chosen hatred over rebuke, and he has let sin rest on his brother. Proverbs uses the same word: *“He that spareth his rod hateth his son”* **(Proverbs 13:24)**.


The rest of Scripture agrees. *“Better is open rebuke than secret love. Faithful are the wounds of a friend; but the kisses of an enemy are deceitful”* **(Proverbs 27:5–6)**. *“A man that flattereth his neighbour spreadeth a net for his feet”* **(Proverbs 29:5)**. God told Ezekiel that a watchman who sees the sword coming and does not blow the trumpet will answer for the blood of those who die: *“his blood will I require at thine hand”* **(Ezekiel 33:8)**. And James gives the other side: *“he which converteth the sinner from the error of his way shall save a soul from death, and shall hide a multitude of sins”* **(James 5:20)**.


So humoring my neighbor is not loving him. To pretend he has the total right before God to whatever value system he holds, and to leave him to live by it without a word, is not tolerance. It is the kiss of the enemy.


I want to be precise here, because this is a political site. My neighbor has liberty before men. I do not have the right to force his conscience, and a government that tries will produce hypocrites, not righteous men. But liberty before men is not a right before God, and a man who confuses the two is in danger. Love respects the first and tells the truth about the second.


## What I would not want done to me


The third question cuts the other way as well. It doesn’t just require me to warn. It forbids me to wound in ways I would not want to be wounded, even when what I say is true.


When we wrote about the livestreamed execution, we said the act might be righteous in its substance, since Scripture permits the civil authority to take the life of a murderer, but it failed as love. Now I can say more exactly why. If I had done something worthy of death, I could accept the sentence as just. I would not want my death made into a show for strangers. Neither would any man. The law of Moses limited even a just punishment *“lest… thy brother should seem vile unto thee”* **(Deuteronomy 25:3)**. Truthful, yes. Loving, no.


The same holds for shameful things people do in private. *“Have no fellowship with the unfruitful works of darkness, but rather reprove them. For it is a shame even to speak of those things which are done of them in secret”* **(Ephesians 5:11–12)**. Notice that Paul requires both: reprove the works, but do not parade the details. I would not want my private failings broadcast, even if every word were accurate. So I may not broadcast my neighbor’s. Jesus gave the order: go to him *“between thee and him alone”* first **(Matthew 18:15)**.


This is not concealment of crime. Where there is a victim, justice must be done openly, because the victim is also my neighbor. But exposure for its own sake, gossip, public shaming, and dragging private sin into the street to enjoy someone’s fall fail the third question.


Paul ties all of this together in one verse that could serve as a commentary on “as thyself”: *“Brethren, if a man be overtaken in a fault, ye which are spiritual, restore such an one in the spirit of meekness; considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted”* **(Galatians 6:1)**. Considering thyself. A man who knows he could be the one receiving it speaks the rebuke.


## How Christ loved


Our standard for “loving” is the way Christ loved, and two scenes show how the three questions work together in Him.


A rich young man came to Jesus and asked what he must do to inherit eternal life. *“Then Jesus beholding him loved him, and said unto him, One thing thou lackest”* **(Mark 10:21)**. He loved him, and because He loved him He told him the hardest thing he could have heard. The young man went away grieved, and Jesus let him go. He did not flatter him to keep him, and He did not compel him to stay. Truth, without flattery and without force.


A woman taken in adultery was brought to Him by men who wanted to use her shame as a weapon. Jesus did not deny the sin, and He did not expose her further. He dispersed her accusers, then said, *“Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more”* **(John 8:11)**. Truth without cruelty. He named the sin and closed the matter in the same breath.


That is what speaking the truth in love looks like **(Ephesians 4:15)**. It is also what acting the truth in love looks like, because a policy is a kind of speech. A law says something to every citizen it touches. The question for any act, private or public, is whether it says the truth, whether it says it to his good, and whether I would want it said to me in the same way.


## Loving God first is living in reality


Underneath all three questions is the first commandment, and it is not one priority among several. It is the one thing we must do.


God is the all of everything. He experiences everything we do, say, and think. *“Neither is there any creature that is not manifest in his sight: but all things are naked and opened unto the eyes of him with whom we have to do”* **(Hebrews 4:13)**. And He hates evil. *“Ye that love the LORD, hate evil”* **(Psalm 97:10)**. When I do evil, I am not doing it in private. I am doing it in the presence of the One who sees it fully and grieves over it.


Any act that leaves God out is therefore not living in reality. It treats the world as if only my neighbor and I were present, and that is not the world we live in. To love myself rightly is to want for myself what God wants for me. To love my neighbor as myself is to want the same for him: not what pleases him for a season, but what he will be glad of at the end, in the presence of the One who has seen everything.


That is the measure. Righteousness tells me what is true. Love tells me to apply it to my neighbor’s good. And “as thyself” tells me how: take what I am about to do into my own body, mind, and heart, as the man I hope to be at the end, and ask whether I would want it.


This is my position, and I’m open to a better argument. If you believe I have misjudged any of these points, I want to hear your reasons.
