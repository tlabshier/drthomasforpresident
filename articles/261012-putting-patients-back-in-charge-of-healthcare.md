# Putting Patients Back in Charge of Healthcare


Oct 12, 2026 · @Thomas Abshier


## Plain language summary


Health insurance for a family now costs about $27,000 a year, more than four times what it cost in 2000. Representatives Andy Harris and Eric Burlison argue that the cure is to let people control their own healthcare money through larger Health Savings Accounts and to make hospitals and insurers post their real prices. Their bill, H.R. 8324, the Great American Healthcare Plan, would do that.


I support the direction. A man who spends his own money asks what things cost, and a doctor who must answer him competes for his trust. But the authors’ account leaves out three things. First, both parties built the present system, so neither party can claim to be its rescuer. Second, savings accounts help those who can save; they do not by themselves answer for the poor, the chronically ill, or the catastrophe that no account can cover. Third, prices will not fall until the supply of care is set free: more practitioners, more kinds of practitioners, fewer permission slips to build a clinic or open a practice.


I propose: lifelong health accounts owned by the person and usable for any lawful care he chooses, including natural and preventive care; insurance kept for catastrophe; posted cash prices that are binding, so that a bill above the posted price cannot be collected; the same tax treatment for coverage a man buys himself as for coverage his employer buys; an open door for new practitioners and clinics; and charity for the poor restored to churches, communities, and sharing ministries, with existing promises to the elderly honored in full. Under all of it lies the question the bill does not touch: most of what we spend treats the consequences of how we live.


*Provenance: Claude drafted this paper in my voice at my request on 12 October 2026, responding to a Washington Times column by Reps. Andy Harris and Eric Burlison (link under Resources) and drawing on my earlier essays “Government Healthcare” (2009) and “Reforming Healthcare – The Anecdotal Database” (2024). Positions here are held open to a better argument.*


## What the authors get right


Harris and Burlison accurately describe the patient’s situation. Imagine a grocery store with no price tags, where you learn your total weeks later, and someone else decides what you can buy. That is the American medical system from the patient’s chair.


The numbers support them. The average employer family premium was about $6,400 in 2000; in 2025 it was $26,993, with the worker paying $6,850 of it directly and the rest coming out of wages he never sees. Prices in general did not quite double over that span; family premiums roughly quadrupled. National health spending reached $5.3 trillion in 2024, about $15,500 per person and 18 percent of everything the nation produces.


The most telling figure is this: of that $5.3 trillion, patients paid only about 11 percent out of pocket. Someone else paid the other 89 cents of every healthcare dollar —an insurer, an employer, Medicare, Medicaid, or another government program. When someone else pays, the patient doesn’t shop, and the provider doesn’t need to compete for him. Prices drift upward, and nobody at the counter has reason to resist. The authors are right that this is the root of the price problem, and right that a man spending his own money becomes a participant in his own care rather than a case to be processed.


They are also right that transparency is plain honesty. A seller who will not tell you the price before you buy is asking for trust he has not earned. Federal rules have required hospitals to post prices since 2021. Five years later, the most recent independent review found that only about half of 2,000 hospitals examined were in compliance. A rule that half the regulated parties ignore is a suggestion.


## What the bill would do


Rep. Burlison introduced the Great American Healthcare Plan in April 2026; it is now H.R. 8324. From the sponsor’s release and from the groups backing it, the bill would:


- **Open Health Savings Accounts to more people.** Today an HSA is tied to a high-deductible plan. The bill would drop that tie, raise the yearly contribution limits, let people on Medicare, Medicaid, and Marketplace plans contribute, and let members of healthcare sharing ministries contribute.

- **Widen what HSA money can buy**, to include healthy food, vitamins, and fitness costs.

- **Codify price transparency** for hospitals and insurers, requiring actual dollar prices rather than estimates, with enforcement, and require insurers to report what they actually pay.

- **Broaden who may prescribe low-risk drugs.**

- **Give small businesses more flexibility** in the coverage they offer, with savings that follow the worker from job to job.

- **It would pay for itself in part by ending the Federal Reserve’s interest payments to banks on their reserves**, which the sponsor estimates at about $1 trillion over ten years.


These provisions build on changes already made. Beginning in 2026, the 2025 budget law made bronze and catastrophic Marketplace plans eligible for HSAs and let account holders pay up to $150 a month for direct primary care memberships from their HSAs. H.R. 8324 would go considerably further.


## Where the argument falls short


### Both parties built this house


The column lays the crisis at the feet of Democrats and Obamacare. The Affordable Care Act raised premiums and deepened dependence on subsidies. But the system that separates the patient from the price is older than Obamacare and was built by both parties:


- The tax break for employer-paid insurance, which ties a man’s coverage to his job and hides its cost inside his paycheck, began with wartime wage controls in the 1940s and was written into the tax code in 1954.

- Medicare and Medicaid came in 1965 under a Democratic president and Congress.

- The HMO Act of 1973 was signed by a Republican president.

- The federal mandate that emergency rooms treat all comers, without paying for that care, was signed by a Republican president in 1986.

- A Republican Congress passed the Medicare prescription drug benefit, the largest entitlement expansion between 1965 and 2010, and a Republican president signed it in 2003.

- A Republican Congress in 1997 capped the number of medical residencies Medicare would fund, which limits the number of new doctors to this day.


I raise this because it matters for the remedy, not to score a point. If the problem were one party’s law, repealing that law would fix it. Because the problem is a cartel of government, insurers, hospital systems, and drug makers, built over eighty years by both parties, the remedy must break the cartel. Partisan framing also makes the reform harder to pass and easier to reverse. A man persuaded by reasons will hold his position; a man persuaded by blame will switch sides when the blame shifts.


The column also says the government finances more than half of national health spending. By the government’s own accounts, federal, state, and local governments directly sponsor about 47 percent. Counting the tax break for employer coverage pushes it past half. The point stands, but it needs to be precise, because careless numbers give opponents an easy way to dismiss a sound argument.


### “It really is that simple” — it is not


Savings accounts work for the man with steady income, good health, and the discipline to save. They do little for three groups:


- **The poor**, who have nothing to put in an account.

- **The chronically ill**, whose costs exceed anything an individual can save. One child with a serious genetic illness can cost more in a year than a family earns in ten.

- **Anyone struck by catastrophe** — the car accident, the cancer, the premature birth — whose bill arrives before his savings could have grown to meet it.


For these, something must pool risk or provide charity. The bill does not say what. I wrote in 2009 that medical savings accounts should be every man’s first line of defense, and I still hold that. But I also wrote that an account is only a reasonable solution where a man has enough income to save, and that catastrophic care requires either insurance or the community. A reform that leaves those cases unaddressed will be defeated by the stories of those it leaves out, and those stories will be true.


The transition is already showing this. The enhanced Marketplace subsidies expired at the end of 2025. Paid Marketplace enrollment fell from about 21.8 million in February 2025 to 19.2 million in February 2026, and those who stayed saw their average premium payments rise by more than half. I do not argue for restoring those subsidies; they paid insurers to keep charging what they charge. But when we withdraw public support, we must have something ready to take its place. That something is not more government. It is restored charity and a market where care costs less. Neither appears overnight, and a reform should be paced to what can actually catch the man who falls.


### Demand without supply raises prices


Giving patients more money to spend, without increasing what they can buy, raises prices. That is basic economics. The column notes that providers are “in short supply,” then proposes nothing to increase the supply. The bill’s provision letting more practitioners prescribe low-risk drugs is a good start. It is not enough. The supply of care is restricted by:


- **Certificate-of-need laws**, still on the books in most states, require a hospital or clinic to get permission, often over competitors’ objections, before it can build or expand.

- **Scope-of-practice rules** that forbid nurse practitioners, pharmacists, naturopathic physicians, midwives, and others from doing what they are trained to do.

- **The residency cap**, which limits how many doctors finish training each year, regardless of how many qualified graduates there are.

- **State-by-state licensing**, which keeps a physician licensed in Montana from treating a patient across the line in Idaho, by telehealth or in person.

- **The cost and delay of drug approval**, which keeps cheaper generics and drugs already approved abroad off American shelves.


Every one of these protects an incumbent from a competitor. Each began with a reasonable safety claim, and now each operates as a toll gate.


### Transparency needs teeth


The bill promises enforcement, but the existing rule already carries penalties, and half of hospitals still do not comply. A posted price that a hospital may ignore when it bills you is not a price. The remedy is a simple rule a citizen can enforce himself: the posted price is the price that can be charged. If a provider bills a cash-paying patient more than its posted price for the same service, the patient may recover the excess before a jury. That requires no new agency. It requires only that the law hold a seller to his word.


### The Federal Reserve funding source deserves care


Ending the Fed’s interest payments on bank reserves is a proposal I welcome for its own sake. Those payments transfer public money to banks for holding reserves that the Fed itself created. But the projected trillion dollars is not guaranteed. If banks move their reserves elsewhere, the Fed may have to sell bonds, and interest rates, including on the national debt, may rise. The proposal should be judged on its own merits as monetary reform, not as a reliable revenue source for healthcare.


## The moral question beneath the policy


The test I apply to any law is this: Is it lawful — does it accord with what God has established as righteous? If so, is it loving — does it treat my neighbor as Christ would treat him?


**On lawfulness.** Taking one man’s earnings by force to pay for another man’s care, even with good motives, is not charity. It is compulsion dressed as compassion. The giver gets no blessing for a gift he did not choose to give, and the receiver learns no gratitude toward a giver he never meets. At the same time, Scripture plainly commands care for the widow, the orphan, and the stranger, and it does not let us walk past the man in the ditch. Both truths stand. The question is not whether the sick man will be cared for, but by whom, and whether the giver gives freely.


**On love.** The current system fails the test of love in a way the column doesn’t mention. It tells patients nothing about cost, then sends them bills they cannot understand and cannot pay. Medical debt is a leading cause of family bankruptcy. A doctor who would never pick a patient’s pocket at the bedside participates, through the billing office, in a system that does so by mail. A neighbor would not feel loved by that. Honest prices are not only efficient; they are what an honest man owes the person he serves.


**On the root cause.** I have practiced as a naturopathic physician for many years. Most of the chronic disease I have treated grew from how people ate, drank, smoked, slept, and moved. Much of it was preventable; much was reversible when patients were willing. Diabetes, obesity, smoking, and excessive drinking together cost this nation well over a trillion dollars a year in care and lost work. Our medical system largely manages the consequences of intemperance. No financing reform changes that. However, a man who takes charge of his own health dollars has a reason to take charge of his health. That is the strongest argument for the accounts, and the authors do not make it.


## My proposals


### 1. A lifelong health account that belongs to the person


Every citizen should have a tax-free health account from birth to death, owned by them, not their employer or the government, that follows them from job to job and passes to their heirs. Parents, grandparents, churches, and employers should all be free to contribute to it. The money should be usable for any lawful care the owner chooses: conventional medicine, naturopathic, chiropractic, and other licensed practitioners, direct primary care memberships, nutrition, and supplements. The government should not decide which healers a free man may pay with his own money. H.R. 8324’s expansion of eligible expenses to food, vitamins, and fitness moves toward this, and I support it.


### 2. Insurance for catastrophe, prepayment for nothing


Insurance is for what is rare and ruinous. When it pays for routine visits and checkups, it becomes an expensive way to prepay ordinary bills, with an insurer’s overhead and permission system attached to every one. Every citizen able to work should, as a matter of civic duty, carry protection against catastrophe — through insurance, a sharing ministry, or a community or church pool — and pay routine costs from his own account. The law should let insurers sell plain, low-cost catastrophic coverage to anyone, across state lines, without mandated benefits that turn it back into prepayment.


### 3. Equal tax treatment


A man who buys his own coverage should receive the same tax treatment as a man whose employer buys it for him. Today the employer exclusion rewards the job-based plan and penalizes the self-employed, the small-business worker, and the man between jobs. Equalizing it ends the tie between a man’s job and his health and makes the cost of coverage visible to the one who bears it.


### 4. Binding posted prices


Every provider should post its cash price for every standard service. The posted price is the most a cash-paying patient can be charged. A bill above it cannot be collected, and the patient may sue to recover any excess, with a jury of his neighbors deciding the facts. Insurers should report what they pay, as the bill requires, so patients can see whether paying cash would cost them less.


### 5. Open the door to more healers


- Repeal certificate-of-need requirements, state by state, and make federal health funding conditional on their repeal.

- Let every licensed practitioner work to the full scope of his training, and let competing private certifiers, who carry liability for what they approve, stand alongside state boards, as I proposed in the administrative-state paper.

- Recognize licenses across state lines through interstate compacts, especially for telehealth.

- Lift the residency cap and let states, hospitals, and private funders train as many physicians as qualified students and patients will support.

- Allow drugs approved by trusted foreign regulators to be sold here after a short review, and speed up generic approvals.


### 6. Restore charity to the people


Over time, care for the poor should return to churches, charitable hospitals, community clinics, and sharing ministries, which can see the person, hold him to account, and give with a face and a name. The law should let doctors deduct the value of free care they give, offer a tax credit for gifts to charitable clinics, and protect sharing ministries from being regulated out of existence. Medicaid should become a block grant administered by the states, which can then work with the charities in their own communities. Healthcare is a matter for states and people, not a national bureau.


### 7. Keep the promises already made


Those who paid into Medicare all their working lives planned around it, and the nation must honor that promise. Medicare beneficiaries should be free to direct their benefit into an account they control and use it for any care they choose, including preventive and natural care the current system does not pay for. Younger workers should be offered a path to their own lifelong accounts instead of payroll taxes.


### 8. Learn from every patient


In 2024, I proposed a public database where patients and practitioners of every kind can record what helped and what did not, grade it by credibility, and search it with AI to find patterns worth formal study. Combined with posted prices and quality reporting, such a database would let a man choose a practitioner and a treatment on evidence of outcome, not only on what an insurer will cover.


## My assessment of H.R. 8324


I support the bill’s direction and most of its provisions: expanded and flexible health accounts, wider eligible expenses, price transparency, broader prescribing, savings that follow the worker, and the end of interest on reserves. I would amend it to make posted prices binding and enforceable by the patient, equalize the tax treatment of individually purchased coverage, open the supply of care, and state plainly how catastrophic illness and the poor will be provided for as government support is withdrawn. A bill that gives people their money and leaves them a market with too few sellers and no honest prices will disappoint the people it means to help.


I hold this position open. If someone shows me a better way to care for the man who cannot save, with less coercion and more love, I will take it.

## Open questions and editorial notes


### Questions still open


- **Catastrophic coverage as civic duty.** Should it be an expectation enforced by the community (for example, a state-level charge on those who carry no catastrophic protection and later need charity care), or left wholly voluntary? My 2009 essay left this for each community to decide.

- **Pacing the withdrawal of subsidy.** How fast can charity and lower prices grow to catch those who lose public coverage, and what measurable signal should govern the pace?

- **Block-granted Medicaid.** What protections keep a state from simply cutting the poor off, rather than handing their care to charities and communities?


### Editorial notes


- **Source headline.** The Washington Times headline is inferred from the article’s URL; confirm the exact wording.

- **Premium figures.** The 2025 family premium ($26,993) and worker share ($6,850) are from KFF’s 2025 survey. The 2000 figure (about $6,400; KFF reported $6,438) is from KFF’s survey archive and should be confirmed against the archived 2000 report. “Prices did not quite double” refers to the Consumer Price Index from 2000 to 2025.

- **Spending figures.** National health spending, per-person spending, the 11 percent out-of-pocket share, and the 47 percent direct government share (31 percent federal, 16 percent state and local) are from the CMS 2024 fact sheet.

- **H.R. 8324 details.** The bill’s provisions are taken from the sponsor’s release, the NTU and NFIB letters, and press reports, not the bill text. Check contribution limits and the exact transparency enforcement on congress.gov before publication. Press reports say the bill is unlikely to pass in the current Congress.

- **History.** The employer exclusion grew out of 1940s wage controls and a 1943 IRS ruling, and Congress codified it in the Internal Revenue Code in 1954. EMTALA was enacted in 1986, the Medicare drug benefit (Part D) in 2003, and the Medicare residency cap in the Balanced Budget Act of 1997.

- **Medical debt and bankruptcy.** The claim that medical bills are a leading cause of family bankruptcy is widely reported but disputed in its magnitude; a specific study should be cited, or the sentence softened.

- **Chronic-disease costs.** The trillion-dollar figure combines estimates used in *Transforming America* (diabetes over $400 billion, smoking over $600 billion, excessive drinking about $250 billion, obesity care about $170 billion); these overlap and should be presented as a rough order of magnitude.

- **Certificate-of-need.** “Most states” reflects the count of states with some CON program; confirm the current number, including Montana’s status after its 2021 changes.
