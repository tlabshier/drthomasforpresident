# The True Proportion of the Threat:


### Weighing Sarah Paine’s Warning of World War III


**A Position Paper — Dr. Thomas for President** | *October 31, 2026*


**Source interview:** *The Diary of a CEO* with Steven Bartlett, interview with Dr. Sarah C. M. Paine, retired professor of history and grand strategy at the U.S. Naval War College (released October 2026). [Episode link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3kuNW8hRc2U)


---


## Plain language summary


Dr. Sarah Paine is a respected historian of Russia and China. In a [recent interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3kuNW8hRc2U), she warned that the world is “barreling” toward a third world war, that the Iran war has been one of the most incompetent wars in history, that Russia is an empire that will keep advancing unless it is stopped in Ukraine, and that Donald Trump belongs in the same category as Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping as a danger to the free world.


I find she is right about more than her critics will want to admit, and wrong in ways her admirers will not notice. She is right that the Iran war has cost America far more than it has gained, that it has taught every threatened nation to seek a nuclear weapon, and that Russia’s war against Ukraine is an unjust war of conquest. She is wrong in proportion: she puts an American president’s reckless words on the same scale as a Russian army’s four and a half years of killing, she replaces analysis with name-calling, she calls a real (if marginal) diesel deal “disinformation,” she dismisses a genuine Russian grievance as pure propaganda, and her remedy is to vote for the other party, as if righteousness were a party label.


My position is this. The danger of a wider war is real and higher than at any time since the Cold War, but it is not a fate. It comes less from one man’s personality than from three wars of choice running at once — Russia’s in Ukraine, ours in Iran, and our economic war on our own allies — and from the collapse of the habit of keeping one’s word. The remedy is not a party but a standard: tell the truth, keep your word, make peace where peace can be made, never pull the shield away from people under bombardment, and stop coveting our neighbors’ land.


---


## 1. Why this interview deserves a careful answer


Dr. Paine is not a cable-news pundit. Her academic work on the Russo-Chinese border and on the wars of Asia is serious scholarship, and many of the “concepts” she offers in this interview — limited and unlimited objectives, positive and negative objectives, the pivotal error, the difference between a problem that can be solved and a condition that can only be managed — are genuinely useful tools for thinking about war. I will use several of them below, including against some of her own conclusions.


She is also, by her own account, newly partisan: “I never used to be partisan,” she says, “but I’m looking at the Republican Party and thinking, ‘How can you do this?'” That candor is to her credit. It also tells the reader where to look for the bias. A scholar’s expertise does not travel with her into every judgment, a point she makes herself when she warns that “just because you’re really good at something doesn’t make you really good at everything.”


The question I ask of this interview is the one I ask of every claim about war: *what is the true proportion?* Hyperbole is lying by proportion. A statement can be built entirely of true facts and still deceive if it presents one danger as the whole danger, or one man as the whole cause. In a matter where the wrong diagnosis is paid for in other people’s sons, getting the proportion right is not an academic nicety. It is a moral duty.


## 2. The ground I stand on


I state my principles first, so the reader can test my conclusions against them.


**God lives every life from the inside.** The center of everything I teach is that God is not a distant spectator of human suffering. He inhabits and experiences each person’s life — we are, in a real sense, God’s experience of the world. When a missile falls on an apartment block in Zaporizhzhia, God is not watching it on a screen; He is in the bodies of the twenty-one people killed, three of them children. When an Iranian conscript dies on a patrol boat in the Strait of Hormuz, or an American sailor dies on a destroyer, God feels it. This is why His law is not arbitrary: He keeps us from harming one another because He bears every harm. And it is why the decision to start a war is the gravest decision a ruler can make. Every life a war consumes is a life God was living.


**The three questions.** I test every policy by three questions, in order. First: *Is it righteous?* — does it conform to what God has established as right, including honesty, keeping one’s word, and not taking what belongs to another? Second: *If so, is it loving?* — does it serve the real good of the people it touches? Third: *Would I want it done to me?* — not measured by momentary appetite, but by a self committed to righteousness, feeling the neighbor’s pain in my own body, mind, and heart.


**Explanation is not justification.** To find that an aggressor had a real grievance is not to find that he had a right to invade. And to find that an American policy was foolish is not to find that its author is the moral equal of a man who has killed tens of thousands.


**No policy built on a diagnosis.** Calling an adversary a sociopath, a madman, or a narcissist ends thought. It may even be true, but it still tells us nothing about what he will accept or how to stop him. *“He that answereth a matter before he heareth it, it is folly and shame unto him”* **(Proverbs 18:13)**.


## 3. Where Dr. Paine is right


I begin with agreement, because it is substantial, and because a critique that cannot acknowledge what its subject gets right has already failed the test of proportion.


### The Iran war has cost far more than it has gained


Dr. Paine applies a simple test: in a war, if you must have one, you put the costs on the enemy and minimize your own. By that test, the war in Iran is failing. The war that began on February 28, 2026, killed Ayatollah Ali Khamenei and much of Iran’s military leadership in its opening hours, but eight months later the Strait of Hormuz is still contested. After Gulf oil flows recovered to over 80 percent of pre-war levels in September, Iranian attacks on shipping surged in October; Al Jazeera reported 15 attacks in a single week, flows collapsing to under 3 million barrels on one day against a pre-war average of more than 17 million, and Brent crude above $106. U.S. diesel averaged $6.18 a gallon in mid-October, up from $3.48 at the start of the year, according to the Energy Information Administration. The Defense Department put the war’s cost at $43.6 billion as of September. Open-source assessments report that the Fifth Fleet’s base in Bahrain was rendered inoperable, and NATO members declined the President’s request for help in Hormuz.


Dr. Paine also identifies the failure in the original assessment: anyone who studied Iran knew that the first thing it would do is close the Strait. Narrow seas close in wartime. I made the same argument in July on this site — that Iran’s strategy was never to win battles but to impose intolerable costs on the world economy, and that the likely American failure would be a costly stalemate rather than a battlefield defeat. That is where we are.


### The war has taught the world to want the bomb


This is her most important point, and I stand with her. “What do you suppose the lesson is for everybody now? Get a nuke.” Libya gave up its program and its ruler was killed. Ukraine gave up its arsenal and was invaded. Iran was negotiating and was bombed. Whatever one thinks of each case, the lesson that hostile and frightened governments draw is the same. Since the war began, Iranian state media aligned with the Revolutionary Guard have called openly for withdrawal from the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty, and officials have said publicly that Iran would seek a weapon if the regime’s survival were threatened. A war launched in part to prevent an Iranian bomb has made an Iranian bomb more likely, not less. By Dr. Paine’s own term, that is a pivotal error: the options America had before the war were better than the options it has now, and the old options do not come back.


### Russia’s war is a war of conquest, and Russia’s conduct is evil


Dr. Paine is right that Russia’s objectives in Ukraine are unlimited in her sense of the term. Moscow’s stated terms, as of late October, are full control of Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia, Kherson, and Crimea — including territory its army has not captured — the withdrawal of Ukrainian forces, no NATO membership, and a smaller Ukrainian army. Russia rejects a ceasefire before a comprehensive settlement on those terms. That is not a negotiating position; it is a demand for surrender.


She is also right about what unlimited objectives do to the people who receive them. They put a nation on “death ground,” where it must fight because it has no other way to live. Ukraine’s resistance is the predictable result, and so is its ingenuity: Ukrainian long-range drones have forced Russian refineries down to roughly 3.8 to 4 million barrels a day against a capacity of about 5.5 million, and the Carnegie Endowment reports that Russia’s exportable diesel fell nearly tenfold between April and September. Meanwhile, the war consumes Russia from within; the Institute for the Study of War estimated that military spending took 46 percent of Russia’s federal budget in early 2026.


And she is right to name the cruelty. Mass graves of bound and tortured civilians, the deportation of children, the bombing of apartment blocks — these are not the incidental costs of war. They are what God detests.


### Dictators have no off-ramp


Her observation that a dictator like Putin has arranged things so that leaving power means death, and therefore has every reason to double down long after the war is lost, is sound and important for policy. A settlement that offers him nothing but humiliation will not be accepted. That does not mean he should be rewarded; it means the terms must be designed with his incentives in view.


### Norms matter


Her image of traffic is good. Most order rests not on enforcement but on the habit of following the rules; there are never enough policemen for a society in which everyone decides to do as he pleases. The same is true among nations. When a great power treats its promises as negotiable, it teaches everyone else to do the same. *“Let your communication be, Yea, yea; Nay, nay”* **(Matthew 5:37)**. A nation’s word is a moral act, and so is breaking it.


## 4. Where the proportion fails


### “Disinformation” about diesel — right about the effect, wrong about the fact


Dr. Paine says the Trump–Putin diesel deal is “disinformation” because Russia “has no capacity to export diesel in sufficient quantities to make any difference whatsoever.”


The deal is real. On October 9, the President announced that Russia would supply more than 300,000 tons immediately, 500,000 tons in November, and roughly a million tons a month afterward; the Treasury issued a license suspending sanctions on Russian diesel deliveries until April 2027, and on October 16 the President signed an order exempting Russia from key sanctions under the Graham Act. Nor is it true that Russia has never had capacity: before this year Russia exported about 40 million tons of diesel annually — nearly a tenth of the world’s international diesel trade.


What *is* true is that the deal will do very little now. Ukraine’s strikes have collapsed Russian export capacity, and analysts at the Council on Foreign Relations and the Carnegie Endowment expect a negligible effect on prices; Carnegie called it largely for show. So Dr. Paine is right about the result and wrong about the claim. The irony is that her conclusion is true *because* of the Ukrainian strikes the President wants stopped.


Her further charge — that the President is blaming Ukraine for prices caused by his own war — is substantially accurate. On October 10, he said it was “time for Ukraine to get a new president” and accused President Zelensky of making problems “for our farmers, our ranchers, with diesel fuel.” The CFR analysis attributes the diesel spike to both the Iran war and the refinery strikes, but the Iran war came first and closed the larger supply. To relieve sanctions on the aggressor, in the middle of the war, in exchange for a marginal quantity of fuel, while blaming the victim for defending itself, fails the first of my three questions. It is not righteous.


### Trump is not Putin


Here is the largest error of proportion in the interview. Dr. Paine places Donald Trump with Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping among the “continentalists,” says the “enemy” of the democratic world includes the United States under Trump, and suggests he might drop a tactical nuclear weapon on Iran.


Some of what she points to is real, and some is wrong. The President said again at an Arizona rally on October 14 that Canada will become the 51st state, “the only way it works.” He has repeatedly claimed Greenland. U.S. forces seized Nicolás Maduro, a sitting head of state, in January; his trial is set for June 2027. Coveting a neighbor’s land is forbidden by the Tenth Commandment, and a nation that talks this way about its closest allies should not be surprised when they seek protection elsewhere. On that point, I agree with her.


But words of annexation that have not been followed by an army are not the same as an army that has killed and maimed hundreds of thousands, deported children, and leveled cities. Xi Jinping’s internment of a million or more Uyghurs is not the same as a tariff. Putting them in the same category tells the reader the difference doesn’t matter. It does. A man who cannot tell a bad neighbor from an arsonist will defend his house poorly against both.


As for the nuclear fear, Dr. Paine concedes that she has “no idea” whether it is plausible. There is no public evidence that the use of a nuclear weapon has been contemplated. To raise it anyway, on a program watched by millions, is the kind of statement that frightens without informing. It is hype.


### Name-calling in place of analysis


“Vlad the Bad.” “Senile narcissists.” “Dark personalities.” “Major Pete,” who she says “is really good at identifying blacks and women and firing them.” “A dysfunctional South African.” These phrases do no analytical work. They invite the audience to stop thinking at the moment when thinking is most needed. Some of what lies behind them is factual — Secretary Hegseth did fire senior officers, among them General Charles Q. Brown, the chairman of the Joint Chiefs, who is Black, and Admiral Lisa Franchetti, the first woman to lead the Navy, and he did announce testosterone screening for troops in July — but the attribution of motive is hers, not the record’s. A historian who rightly warns against propaganda should hold herself to the standard of fact, not ridicule.


The same applies to her complaint about “tech billionaire immigrants” buying elections. Elon Musk gave more than $150 million through the Senate Republicans’ leading super PAC to the Texas Senate race in September, according to federal filings. The concentration of political money in a few hands is a legitimate concern. But the principle, if it is a principle, applies to every billionaire, native-born or not, on either side of the aisle. Singling out the foreign-born ones, in a passage that also mocks concern about illegal immigration, is not an argument. It is a mood.


### “Recreational war”


Dr. Paine calls the Iran war a “recreational war.” It was a war of choice, and I have argued it was imprudent in its design and costly in its execution. But Iran’s nuclear program was not a fantasy, and Iran’s regime was not harmless. The late Supreme Leader’s religious ruling against nuclear weapons, which Dr. Paine cites as evidence he opposed the bomb, was never published in writing; Western intelligence assessed that Iran ran a covert weapons design program until 2003; and before the war, the International Atomic Energy Agency could no longer verify Iran’s stockpile of highly enriched uranium. The President was facing a real danger and chose a bad remedy. “Recreational” erases the danger in order to sharpen the indictment. That is lying by proportion.


### NATO expansion: both things are true


Dr. Paine dismisses the argument that NATO’s eastward expansion provoked Russia as nothing more than Putin’s “propaganda points.” On this I part with her, though not entirely.


The strongest part of her answer is true and too often forgotten: Eastern European nations were not dragged into NATO. They ran to it, because they remembered 1953 in East Germany, 1956 in Hungary, 1968 in Czechoslovakia, and decades of occupation. Their choice deserves respect, and their fear has been vindicated.


But it is also true that the United States gave verbal assurances in 1990 — about Germany, to the Soviet Union, never written into a treaty — that NATO would not move “one inch” eastward; that our own ambassador in Moscow warned in 2008 that Ukrainian membership was the brightest of all Russian red lines; and that NATO declared that same year, at Bucharest, that Ukraine “will become” a member, without any plan to defend it in the meantime. As I wrote in September, the NATO grievance is a real cause, overstated by Moscow, but not a justification. Calling it mere propaganda is no more accurate than calling it the whole story.


Dr. Paine also says the United States “promised to protect” Ukraine when it gave up its nuclear weapons. The 1994 Budapest Memorandum did not promise that. The United States, Britain, and Russia pledged to respect Ukraine’s borders and refrain from the threat or use of force against it, and to seek immediate Security Council action if Ukraine were threatened with nuclear attack. Russia broke its pledge outright. America broke none of its written commitments — but the moral weight of having persuaded a nation to disarm in exchange for assurances is real, and it is one reason I hold that we may not walk away from Ukraine’s defense.


### “Ukraine is winning”


Dr. Paine says Ukraine “is winning right now.” Ukraine is not losing, and its strikes are inflicting serious damage on Russia’s economy. But Russia occupies about a fifth of Ukraine, the war is in its fifth year, and the front lines move slowly in Russia’s direction. Neither side is winning in any sense its people would recognize. The honest word is *stalemate* — and stalemates are where negotiations become possible, if leaders will have them. To tell Ukrainians and Americans that victory is at hand is to invite more years of the same killing on the strength of a hope.


### The remedy is righteousness, not a party


Dr. Paine closes with a political prescription: vote, elect the other party, and have the next Congress impeach and convict the President. She says Americans must “talk across these silos,” and then she attributes the whole of the present danger to one party.


The Republican Senate did confirm Secretary Hegseth, and it has not checked the President’s war. But Congress’s abdication of its constitutional power to declare war did not begin in 2026. It ran through Korea, Vietnam, the open-ended 2001 authorization, the 2003 Iraq war, and the 2011 Libya campaign under a Democratic president. The disease is bipartisan, and so must the cure be. A congressional vote on the Iran war under the War Powers Resolution is owed regardless of which party holds the gavel after Tuesday.


Elections matter, and every citizen should vote. But a nation does not become righteous by changing which party holds power. It becomes righteous when its people and leaders choose what God calls right — truthfulness, faithfulness to one’s word, refusal to covet another’s land, and mercy toward the defenseless — and when they hold every leader of every party to that standard.


## 5. The true proportion: the actual threat


Weighing all of this, I take the following stand.


**The risk of a wider war is real and elevated.** Russian drones have violated NATO airspace repeatedly this year; Romania has shot several down, and a cargo ship was struck by a drone and sank near Romanian waters in late October. Any of these incidents could trigger an Article 5 crisis. Iran’s leaders are publicly debating the bomb. The United States has strained its alliances at the moment it needs them most. This is the most dangerous convergence since the Cold War, and a leader who denies it is not serious.


**It is not inevitable, and “barreling” overstates it.** Mutual deterrence among nuclear powers, which Dr. Paine dismisses too quickly, has held through Korea, Cuba, Vietnam, Afghanistan, and four and a half years of this war. India and China have both warned Moscow against nuclear use. European nations are rearming, which makes a Russian attack on NATO less likely, not more. The danger is serious; the fatalism is not warranted. Fatalism is itself a danger, because it tells people that nothing they do matters.


**The cause is not one man’s personality.** It is three wars of choice running at once: Russia’s war of conquest in Ukraine, our war in Iran, and our economic war on our own allies. Behind all three lies the same failure: leaders who will not keep their word, who covet what is not theirs, and who cannot admit an error once made. *“Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall”* **(Proverbs 16:18)**. That diagnosis applies in Moscow, in Tehran, and, in its proportion, in Washington.


## 6. What righteousness requires


These positions follow from the principles stated above. Some extend what I have written before; I hold each open to a better argument.


- **A vote on the Iran war.** The President should seek, and Congress should hold, an up-or-down vote under the War Powers Resolution. The objective should be narrowed to what is achievable: a verified end to weapons-grade enrichment and safe passage through Hormuz — enforcement by inspection, not invasion — with no goal of regime change by bombing. The deeper policy remains transformation over containment: reach the Iranian people, seventy percent of whom are under forty, with truth, commerce, and the gospel, so that the change comes from within.

- **No sanctions relief for the aggressor outside a settlement.** Relief from sanctions is leverage. It should be traded for a ceasefire, not for a headline about fuel. The diesel license and the Graham Act exemption should be tied to a halt in strikes on Ukrainian cities.

- **Never pull the shield.** I have argued that America should not supply an endless stalemate as though it were a strategy, and should press both sides toward the table. But a moral line exists between the sword and the shield. Air defense, early warning, and the communications that let interceptors stop drones aimed at apartment blocks protect civilians, and they must never be bargaining chips. Whoever ordered the Starlink speed cap that disabled Ukrainian interceptor drones in mid-October — and that question is still disputed between Washington, SpaceX, and Kyiv — the result was to leave civilians less protected.

- **An honest settlement for Ukraine.** An armistice on the line of contact; strong security guarantees for what remains of Ukraine, in whatever form Ukraine and its European neighbors can bear; non-recognition of conquered territory, as America refused for fifty years to recognize the Soviet annexation of the Baltic states; and a door left open for Russia when it is ready to live as a neighbor. Ukraine decides for Ukraine. No settlement should be imposed over the heads of the people who have done the dying.

- **Stop coveting our neighbors’ land.** The talk of Canada as the 51st state and of taking Greenland should end. It harms American workers — Jim Beam has shut its main Kentucky distillery for all of 2026 as the Canadian market closed to American spirits — and it destroys the trust on which American security rests.* “Thou shalt not covet thy neighbour’s house”* **(Exodus 20:17)**.

- **Tell the truth about causes.** The President should stop blaming Ukraine for prices his own war created, and his critics should stop calling real events disinformation when they are merely exaggerated. A nation cannot reassess what it will not honestly describe.

- **Treat the next generation as the stake.** On this, Dr. Paine and I agree without qualification. “What is more important than the generations that follow? Nothing.” Every policy above should be judged by whether our grandchildren will inherit a world of kept promises or broken ones.


## 7. Why the warning still deserves a hearing


I have spent much of this paper on Dr. Paine’s errors of proportion, so I want to be clear about why her central concern remains plausible, and why a reader who rejects her partisanship should not reject her warning.


**First,** the structure she describes is real. Wars do not start because anyone wants a world war; they start because each side takes a step that seems limited to itself and threatening to the other, and because the losing side will not admit it. Both the Russian and the Iranian regimes now believe their survival is at stake. Men who believe that are capable of things they would never otherwise consider.


**Second,** the incentives point the wrong way. Russia earns more from every relief of sanctions, Iran gains from every week Hormuz stays dangerous, and American leaders gain politically from declaring victory rather than admitting stalemate. None of these incentives rewards peace.


**Third,** the generation that remembered the last world war is gone. Dr. Paine is right that the leaders who built the postwar order did so because they had seen what the alternative looked like. Those of us who have not seen it are tempted, as the interviewer candidly admitted, to assume it cannot happen. That temptation is the most dangerous thing in the interview, and her warning against it is the most valuable.


Finally, and most of all, God feels every life a war would take. We must measure the proportion of the threat not only in probabilities, but in what is at stake if the probability comes due. A small chance of an immeasurable evil still demands our full attention.


*“Blessed are the peacemakers: for they shall be called the children of God”* **(Matthew 5:9)**. Not the appeasers, and not the crusaders — the *makers* of peace, which is work, craft, and courage. That is America’s calling now: to make peace where peace can be made, to shield the defenseless where it cannot, and to keep our word in both.


I hold these positions open to a better argument. If Dr. Paine or any reader can show me where my proportion is wrong, I will say so.

## Notes and corrections to the interview


These are points where the interview misstated or omitted facts. None changes its central argument.


- **Russian diesel.** The deal Dr. Paine calls “disinformation” was announced on October 9, 2026, and accompanied by a Treasury license and an executive order. Russia’s export capacity is now sharply reduced by Ukrainian strikes, which is why its market impact is expected to be small (see Carnegie and CFR, below).

- **Russian refining.** The interviewer’s figure that Russia has lost “up to 50%” of its operating refineries reflects peaks during intense strike periods; Carnegie estimates current runs at about 3.8–4 million barrels a day against roughly 5.5 million of capacity.

- **The Budapest Memorandum (1994)** gave Ukraine assurances, not a guarantee of defense. See section 4.

- **The late Supreme Leader’s anti-nuclear ruling** was never issued in writing, and Iran’s pre-2003 weapons design work is documented by Western intelligence and the IAEA.

- **Sputnik.** The dog Laika flew on Sputnik 2 (November 1957), not the first Sputnik.

- **Pearl Harbor.** Japan’s decision to attack followed, above all, the U.S. oil embargo of summer 1941, not only American aid to China.

- **European institutions.** The Nordic-Baltic Eight has cooperated since the early 1990s; it is not new. Britain’s Shetland spaceport at SaxaVord is already licensed, not merely planned. The pace of European defense cooperation since 2022 is, however, genuinely remarkable.

- **“ICE at polling stations,” “walking away with voting machines,” and “$5,000 to vote Republican.”** These were raised in the interview. Federal law bars armed federal agents from polling places except to keep the peace, and the Homeland Security Secretary has said ICE will not patrol polling stations; the President has said only that he would do “anything necessary” for honest elections. I found no reporting of plans to seize voting machines. The $5,000 claim is substantially accurate, though the host’s phrasing compresses it: since September 9, the President has repeatedly promised a $5,000 “dividend” to all adult citizens, and news reports describe it as contingent on Republicans keeping both the House and the Senate (Fortune, October 8, 2026). It is not a payment to individuals for their votes. It is a promise of public money — about $1.3 trillion, with no stated source — tied to a party’s victory. By this paper’s standards, that is a promise made to win an election rather than a policy argued on its merits; it also fails the test of truthfulness if it cannot be paid, and an Economist/YouGov poll found only 21 percent of registered voters believe it will be.

- **Territory.** The President has said Ukraine has “lost 30%” of its territory; independent estimates put Russian control at about 20 percent.
