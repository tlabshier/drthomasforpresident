# 261029 - Banking as a Necessity

# The Millstone and the Algorithm


## Who Decides Whether a Man May Buy or Sell? A Response to the Malones on Global Financial Power


*Dr. Thomas — Position Paper, 29 October 2026*


---


### My Position


Drs. Robert and Jill Glasspool Malone published an essay, “The Financial Takeover of Globalism,” that deserves a wide readership. Their argument is that much of what we call globalism is, at bottom, an economic project; that the rules governing American banks are increasingly written in international committees that no American voter elected; that those rules tend to favor the largest institutions over their smaller competitors; and that artificial intelligence may soon turn this concentrated financial power into a system of automated exclusion that no court, legislature, or citizen can effectively challenge.


I stand with them. I believe the concentration of financial power, and especially the power to decide who may take part in economic life, is one of the most serious threats to freedom in our time. It is not the loudest threat. It does not march in the streets. It works quietly, through compliance manuals, risk scores, and account-closure letters that give no reason. That quietness is exactly what makes it dangerous.


Below, I explain why I hold this position, where the argument must be careful, why the concern remains even after those cautions are honored, and what I would do about it.


---


### What the Malones Argue


The story begins in 1974, when German regulators closed the Herstatt Bank in Cologne in the middle of the trading day. Banks worldwide had already paid Herstatt in German marks and were waiting for dollars from New York. The dollars never came. In response, central bankers from the major industrial nations formed the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, hosted by the Bank for International Settlements in Switzerland, an institution owned by central banks.


Over fifty years, the Committee produced a series of international banking standards (Basel I in 1988, Basel II in 2004, Basel III after the 2008 crisis). These are not treaties. The Senate never ratifies them. American regulators from the Federal Reserve, the FDIC, and the Comptroller of the Currency help write them in Basel, then bring them home and adopt them as binding federal regulations under authority Congress delegated long ago. Similar international bodies set the standards for anti-money-laundering rules and financial reporting.


The Malones then follow the money. Compliance costs hit small banks hardest, which have shrunk from 14,483 commercial banks in 1984 to about 4,000 today. The three largest asset managers now oversee more than $33 trillion. When international rules go wrong, as when Basel treated Greek government bonds as riskless, the error spreads everywhere at once. And the same infrastructure that moves money can stop it: SWIFT cut off Iranian and Russian banks; JPMorgan Chase closed accounts tied to Dr. Joseph Mercola; Coutts closed Nigel Farage’s accounts in part because of his views; Canada froze protesters’ accounts under emergency powers. They close by asking Congress to reclaim oversight of international banking standards and to protect citizens against unjustified debanking and AI-driven exclusion.


---


### The First Problem: Rules Made Out of Sight


My platform is built on a frame I first used in 2003, *A New ERA*: Energy, Righteousness, and Accountability. The Basel process fails the test of Accountability at its root.


The principle is simple. A law that binds a free people should be made by people that free people can remove. When a committee of central bankers meeting in Switzerland drafts a standard, and American agencies then adopt it as a binding rule without any elected legislator voting on it, the chain of accountability breaks in two places. The people who wrote the rule cannot be voted out, and the people who could have voted on it never did.


Defenders of this arrangement will say our regulators retain full legal authority to accept, change, or reject any standard. That is true on paper. In practice, a regulator who helped negotiate an international standard, and whose foreign counterparts are watching to see if America keeps its word, is not a neutral judge of whether to adopt it. The negotiation itself creates the commitment. Congress delegated the authority; it did not delegate the conscience. It remains responsible for what is done in its name.


Adam Smith understood the danger in 1776. Near the end of the first book of *The Wealth of Nations*, he warned that any proposal for a new law or regulation of commerce from merchants and dealers “ought always to be listened to with great precaution,” because their interests are never quite the same as the public’s. Smith was not a socialist suspicious of commerce. He was commerce’s great defender, and he knew that the people who profit from a market should be the last to write its rules without scrutiny.


---


### The Second Problem: The Compliance Wall


A bank must hire compliance officers, lawyers, and risk analysts whether it holds three trillion dollars or three hundred million. The giant spreads that cost across an enormous balance sheet. The community bank that lends to farmers in the Flathead Valley cannot. Every new layer of complexity is therefore a quiet subsidy to the largest institutions, and every merger of a small bank into a large one removes a lender who knew his borrowers by name.


This is how regulatory capture works. Rules sold as public protection become a wall that shields incumbents from competition. The wall need not be built on purpose. It only needs to be built by people whose own institutions can climb it and their competitors cannot.


In my judgment, the most important part of that wall is not the capital standard the Malones focus on, but the anti-money-laundering and “know your customer” machinery that sits beside it. Small banks feel the heaviest burden there, and, as we will see, it is also the machinery through which individual citizens get shut out.


---


### The Third Problem: The Power to Exclude


Here the argument moves from economics to the heart of the matter.


In the ancient world, a man’s livelihood was his millstone. The Law of Moses forbade a lender to take it as security:* “No man shall take the nether or the upper millstone to pledge: for he taketh a man’s life to pledge”* **(Deuteronomy 24:6)**. The principle is that you may not seize the means by which a man feeds his family, because to take that is to take his life.


In our world, the millstone is the bank account. Without one, a man cannot receive wages, pay rent, run a business, or buy groceries online. A bank account is no longer a convenience. It is the gate through which a person enters economic life.


The record shows that this gate has been closed on lawful people for reasons that had nothing to do with fraud or crime:


- **Operation Choke Point (2013).** Federal regulators pressured banks to drop lawful but disfavored businesses, including firearms dealers, under the banner of “reputational risk.” Those customers had broken no law.

- **Canada (2022).** During the trucker protests, the government invoked the Emergencies Act and authorized banks to freeze accounts without a court order. Canada’s finance department told Parliament that it froze roughly 206 to 210 accounts holding about C$7.8 million. In January 2024, the Federal Court ruled that the invocation was unreasonable and that the account freezes violated the Charter’s protection against unreasonable search and seizure, because no objective standard had to be met before an account was frozen. The government appealed, and the Federal Court of Appeal upheld that ruling.

- **Coutts (2023).** The British bank’s internal documents showed that it weighed Nigel Farage’s political views when deciding to close his accounts. The chief executive of its parent company resigned.

- **The executive order (August 2025).** The President directed federal regulators to remove “reputational risk” as a basis for supervisory action and to address politically motivated debanking. A government does not issue such an order against a problem that does not exist.


The Canadian case teaches the central lesson. A free country, with courts, a charter of rights, and a free press, froze its citizens’ bank accounts for joining or supporting a protest, and its courts took years to say that was unlawful. The money was frozen in days. The vindication came in years. That gap is where freedom dies.


---


### The Fourth Problem: The Algorithm


Human beings did all of the above and could, at least in principle, be named, questioned, and held to account. Artificial intelligence threatens to remove even that.


Banks already use automated systems to flag transactions and score customers for risk. As those systems grow more capable and more uniform, a person flagged by one model may be flagged by all of them, because the models are trained on similar data, sold by the same few vendors, and tuned to satisfy the same regulators. Shared fraud and risk databases mean that a closure at one institution can follow a person to the next. No one has to issue an order. No official has to sign anything. The exclusion simply happens across many banks, and possibly many countries, and the excluded person doesn’t know why or whom to ask.


This is what the Malones mean when they warn of a Western social credit system that needs no official score. I agree with them that no grand conspiracy is required. A system can produce injustice without anyone intending it if it rewards avoiding risk and rewards no one for protecting the innocent customer. The algorithm does not hate anyone. It simply has no reason to care.


I have written before, in my series on artificial intelligence and in my paper on AI regulatory capture, that AI’s danger is not only that it may become powerful, but that it may become the tool through which the already powerful escape accountability. Financial exclusion is where that danger first touches ordinary lives.


---


### The Test of Righteousness


I measure every policy by three questions, in this order. Is it righteous: does it accord with what God has established as just? If so, does it love my neighbor? And would I want it done to me, feeling its effect in my own body, mind, and heart, as a man committed to righteousness and not to his momentary convenience?


**Is it righteous?** Scripture requires that a man be condemned only on evidence, *“at the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses”* **(Deuteronomy 19:15)**, and that he be allowed to answer the charge. Closing a lawful person’s account because of his opinions, his associations, or a score he cannot see condemns him without a charge, without a witness, and without a hearing. It takes his millstone. It fails the first question.


**Is it loving?** Love does not mean approving everything a neighbor believes. It does mean refusing to cut him off from the means of his livelihood because he believes it. A society that can starve its dissenters into silence has stopped treating them as neighbors.


**Would I want it done to me?** Imagine the Canadian farmer the Malones met, whose assets were frozen because he had hosted a pig roast for the truckers and their families. Imagine opening your banking app to find your account closed, your direct deposit returned, your mortgage payment bounced, and a letter that says only that the bank “has made a business decision.” None of us would accept that for ourselves. We should not accept it for anyone.


I also hold that God inhabits and experiences each person’s life from within. When a person is shut out of economic life without cause, it is not an abstraction in a risk model. It is a life, and it is experienced.


---


### Where the Argument Must Be Careful


A position worth holding should be stated in a form that survives its strongest critic. These are the places where precision strengthens the case rather than weakening it.


**What is established.** International standards are written in bodies no American elects and adopted here without a congressional vote. Community banks have declined dramatically. Lawful people and businesses have been debanked for reasons unrelated to crime, in documented cases in the United States, Britain, and Canada. Canadian courts have ruled such freezes unconstitutional.


**What is probable.** Compliance costs systematically favor the largest institutions. The largest institutions have outsized influence on the rules. Standardized regulation spreads errors across the whole system, as the treatment of Greek debt and of mortgage securities before 2008 showed.


**What is a serious warning, not yet a fact.** A unified, AI-driven system of financial exclusion operating across institutions and borders does not yet exist in the West. The pieces are being assembled. The warning is about where the pieces lead.


Three specific points call for precision:


- **Bank consolidation has several causes.** The end of restrictions on interstate banking in the 1990s, mergers, and the 2008 crisis account for much of the decline. The Basel capital rules themselves are applied in full only to large banks; since 2019, smaller American banks may use a simpler capital measure. A stronger argument is that the overall compliance burden, especially the anti-money-laundering regime, falls disproportionately on small banks. For example, the $10,000 cash reporting threshold set in 1970 has never been adjusted for inflation, so it sweeps in vastly more ordinary transactions every year, and every report costs staff time that a small bank can least afford.

- **The dollar’s declining share of world reserves is real, but slower than headlines suggest.** Its share has fallen from about 71 percent in 2000 to 56.7 percent in mid-2026. Part of each quarter’s change reflects currency valuation rather than central banks actively selling dollars. The long-term trend is the point, and it shows that weaponizing financial access invites rivals to build alternatives.

- **Not every account closure has a political motive.** JPMorgan Chase never publicly explained the Mercola closures, so motive in that case is inferred, not proven. That is precisely the problem. When banks give no reasons, the innocent cannot clear their names and the public cannot tell a legitimate closure from a political one. Secrecy is not a defense of the system. It is one of its defects.


---


### Why the Concern Stands


None of these cautions weakens the core of the case. They sharpen it.


**The burden of proof belongs on the one who excludes.** When a bank or a government shuts a lawful person out of economic life, it is the one acting, and it is the one that should have to justify itself. The fact that we often cannot know why an account was closed is not a reason to doubt the concern. It is the concern.


**The incentives all point one way.** For a large bank, closing a controversial customer’s account costs almost nothing, and keeping it carries regulatory and reputational risk. For a regulator, pressing banks to avoid risk is safe, and defending an unpopular customer’s access is not. For an AI vendor, a model that flags more people is easier to defend than one that misses a single bad actor. No one in the chain is paid to protect the innocent. A system with those incentives will drift toward exclusion even if every person in it means well.


**The pattern has already appeared in free countries.** Operation Choke Point, Coutts, and the Canadian freezes were not the work of dictatorships. They happened in democracies with courts and constitutions. If this is what free societies did with the tools of 2013 and 2022, we should take seriously what they could do with the tools of 2030.


**The intuition is sound.** Many people sense that something is deeply wrong when a man’s ability to feed his family depends on the approval of an institution he cannot see and an algorithm he cannot question. Christians hear in it an echo of the warning that a day may come when no man *“might buy or sell”* except by permission **(Revelation 13:17)**. One need not read current events as the fulfillment of prophecy to recognize the shape of the danger. That intuition is not paranoia. It is moral perception, and it is older and wiser than any risk model.


---


### What I Would Do


The Malones are right that the answer is not to abandon international cooperation or to surrender the dollar’s leadership. Cooperation since Herstatt has produced real gains: modern settlement systems have largely closed the gap that destroyed confidence in 1974, and stronger capital requirements helped the banking system weather 2020. The answer is to restore accountability and due process. I propose:


- **Congressional approval of international standards.** Any federal rule implementing a standard from the Basel Committee, the Financial Stability Board, or the Financial Action Task Force, above a defined economic impact, should take effect only after Congress votes to approve it. If a standard is good, it will pass. If it cannot pass, it should not bind Americans.

- **A statutory right to fair access.** Any bank that holds FDIC insurance or uses the Federal Reserve’s payment systems enjoys a public privilege. In return, it should not deny or end service to a lawful person or business except on objective, individualized, documented grounds of financial risk or legal violation. “Reputational risk” should be removed as a basis for supervision by law, not merely by executive order, so that the next administration cannot quietly restore it.

- **Reasons and an appeal.** A customer whose account is closed or frozen should receive written reasons and the right to appeal to an independent adjudicator, with a decision within a fixed and short time. Where the law requires secrecy, for example in an active criminal investigation, a judge, not a bank, should approve keeping the customer in the dark.

- **No government-directed freezes without a court order.** The Canadian lesson should be written into American law. No government official may direct or pressure a financial institution to freeze or close a citizen’s account without an individualized order from a court.

- **A human being behind every adverse decision.** When an automated system recommends closing an account or denying service, a named human being must review it, the customer must be told that an automated system was involved, and the models must be open to audit. No shared database may brand a person across institutions without notice to him and a chance to challenge the entry.

- **Relief for community banks.** Index the cash-reporting thresholds to inflation, simplify reporting for small institutions, and require every major banking rule to carry an honest estimate of its burden on banks under $10 billion. A rule that wipes out community banks should have to say so before it is adopted.

- **A permanent ban on a federal digital currency.** The law passed this year bars the Federal Reserve from issuing a central bank digital currency through 2030. That ban should be made permanent. Programmable government money would place the power to exclude in a single set of hands.

- **A strong dollar built on real strength.** The dollar’s leadership is a genuine national asset, and it rests finally on the productive strength of the American economy. That is why abundant, affordable energy is the foundation of my platform. A nation that produces is a nation whose currency the world wants to hold. A nation that leans on financial coercion invites the world to find another.


---


### The Stakes


A free society cannot remain free if a person’s access to banking, commerce, and livelihood depends on the approval of institutions and algorithms that no citizen can see, question, or hold to account. The Malones have described how we arrived at this point. The question now is whether we will act before the machinery of exclusion is complete, or wait until we discover that it is already running.


I hold this position for the reasons given here, and I hold it open to a better argument. The millstone of the modern man is his bank account. The Law forbade taking it without cause three thousand years ago. We should forbid it now.
