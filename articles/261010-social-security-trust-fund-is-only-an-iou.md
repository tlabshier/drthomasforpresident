# The Social Security Trust Fund: A Fund of IOUs


Oct 10, 2026 · Thomas Abshier


## Plain language summary


The Social Security Trust Fund exists, but it holds no savings. It holds only special government bonds — IOUs the government wrote to itself for payroll taxes it collected and then spent on other things. Taxpayers must pay every dollar of those IOUs again. When the fund “runs out” around 2034, nothing will have been lost that wasn’t already gone; the IOUs will simply have all been cashed, which will trigger a reduction in the benefits paid to retirees (but that can change, and probably will be changed by passing a law that increases the benefits, so nothing will happen when the SSTF bonds are all cashed – it is a meaningless gesture to pretend there are consequences). Social Security is a transfer from today’s workers to today’s retirees. Social Security is not a pension fund set aside and invested, paying pensioners from interest and principal saved. The SSTF does not appreciate with the economy, and it does not increase in value with inflation like the stock market.


I propose honoring the promise to those who are retired or near it, giving younger workers accounts they own and can pass to their children, telling the truth about the books, and restoring care of the needy to families and voluntary charity.

## Why I am revisiting a 2005 essay


In April 2005, President George W. Bush visited the Bureau of the Public Debt in Parkersburg, West Virginia. He stood beside a filing cabinet labeled “Social Security Trust Fund” and told the country what it contained: not money, but government bonds. “There is no trust fund,” he said, “just IOUs that I saw firsthand.” The New York Times called it a “shameless photo-op.” The paper argued that the bonds were as real as the Treasury bonds Japan held, and that the president was disparaging the United States’ full faith and credit.


At the time, I wrote that the president was right and the Times was wrong. Twenty-one years later, I still believe that. But I also overstated my case. I compared the trust fund to Hitler’s “big lie.” I still think this is true. The big lie is a lie so big that people cannot believe it is a lie because of the deception’s magnitude.  We are being told that the 12.4% taken out of our paycheck every week is going into a trust fund (which implies savings), when actually it is being spent every week by the government, and nothing is being saved, invested, or put in a lockbox other than a ledger entry saying how much was spent on other expenditures other than a trust fund.


This big lie has been bipartisan; both Republican and Democrat administrations have perpetuated the lie. GW Bush tried to say it openly, and it may be believed by a percentage of the population in 2026, but the establishment media in 2005 attacked him and debunked his truthful statement as a “shameless photo op”. At its worst, this indicates we are being lied to by an intentional media-government cartel propaganda campaign designed to deceive. The alternative is a massively misinformed/uninformed/ignorant news media and government employee cohort who cannot distinguish truth from fiction. Either way, we, as the public, are being misinformed by ignorant people (or intentionally lied to by deceivers) about one of the most consequential investments we make as responsible stewards of our finances.


## What the trust fund is


Every working American pays 12.4 percent of his wages into Social Security, split between himself and his employer. In most years since the 1980s, those payroll taxes brought in more than Social Security paid out. Since 1939, the law has required that any surplus be lent to the Treasury in exchange for special-issue government bonds that only the trust fund can hold. The Treasury takes the cash and spends it on whatever the government is buying that year: roads, defense, interest on other debt. The bonds go into the file.


So the trust fund is real in a legal sense. It is a set of Treasury accounts, and the bonds are legally binding obligations. It is not a store of savings. Nothing in it was invested in a farm, a factory, or a business that will produce something in the future. The money was spent as soon as it arrived. At the end of 2025, the fund held $2.56 trillion in these bonds, down $160 billion from the year before.


## Why an IOU to yourself is not savings


Imagine a father who sets up a college fund for his son. Each month he deposits money into it. Then, each month, he takes the money back out to pay the mortgage and puts a note in the fund that says, “I owe this account $500.” After eighteen years, the fund holds a thick stack of notes. Are they real? In one sense, yes: the father really does owe the money. But when the son goes to college, the tuition will have to come out of the father’s paycheck that year, exactly as if the fund had never existed. The notes told the father what he owed. They did not save a single dollar.


That is the Social Security Trust Fund. The Times was right that the bonds are as legally binding as Japan’s bonds. But that proves the point; it doesn’t refute it. A bond held by Japan is a debt the American taxpayer owes to Japan. A bond held by the trust fund is a debt the American taxpayer owes American retirees—for money the taxpayer already paid once in payroll tax and will now pay a second time in income tax or borrowing when the bond is cashed. To the program, the bonds are an asset. To the nation, they are a debt. Counted together, the country saved nothing.


The ordinary man hears the words “trust fund” and pictures money set aside and invested on his behalf. He does not picture the government taxing him for one purpose, spending the money on another, and then billing him again. That gap between what the words suggest and what they mean is the dishonesty I am objecting to. Scripture condemns “divers weights and divers measures” — one scale for buying and another for selling. A word that means one thing to the people who hear it and another to the people who use it is a divers measure.


## How the illusion grew


None of this began with one president or one party.


When Congress created the trust fund in 1939, it required that its reserves be invested in government securities. From the start, the Treasury lent surplus payroll taxes and spent them.


In 1968, President Johnson’s budget adopted the “unified budget,” which counted the Social Security surplus together with all other federal income. That made the Vietnam-era deficits look smaller than they were. Later laws moved Social Security “off budget” in the accounting, but they didn’t change what happens to the money: the Treasury still borrows it and spends it.


In 1983, a bipartisan commission led by Alan Greenspan raised payroll taxes and raised the future retirement age to build a large surplus so the program would be ready when the baby boomers retired. The surplus was built. Congress, in both parties, also spent it year after year. In 2000, both parties promised to put it in a “lockbox.” No lockbox was ever built.


The trust fund peaked at about $2.9 trillion in 2020. Since then, Social Security has been paying out more in benefits than it takes in from payroll taxes. The Treasury now “redeems” bonds to cover the difference, but redeeming them is only a ledger entry: one government account marks down what another government account owes it. The cash to pay benefits comes from the same place as all federal spending—taxes and borrowing—exactly as it would if the trust fund had never existed. Only the flow has changed direction. Whether payroll taxes exceed benefits in a given year changes nothing about this. All federal revenue goes into one pot, all federal spending comes out of it, and whatever the pot lacks is borrowed. The trust fund ledger records an earmark; it does not hold or move any money. The baby boomers’ retirement is being paid by today’s taxpayers and today’s lenders, not by any savings set aside for it.


## What “running out” means


The 2026 Trustees Report projects that the combined trust funds will be exhausted in 2034. The retirement fund alone runs out late in 2032. Most news stories describe that as Social Security going broke. That is not quite accurate, and the inaccuracy hides the real problem.


When the fund is exhausted, payroll taxes will still come in and cover about 83 percent of promised benefits. Unless Congress acts, benefits would be cut to that level. So 2034 is not the year the money disappears. The money disappeared long ago, as it was spent. In 2034, when the SSTF ledger reaches zero, the government must reduce benefits to Social Security recipients by law to 83%. (This just meant that over the entire history of Social Security that the amount collected and amount dispensed was even.) Not a think changes on that day. The day before the zero-balance day the SSA took money from the general fund and paid the SS recipients. The day after, the SSA took money out of the general fund and  the shortfall by redeeming bonds it wrote to itself. At that point, Congress will have to pass a new bill that says we are increasing the rates to 100% benefits even though we have taken in less money than we have paid out. This is a meaningless marker. We should admit what has been true all along: people working now pay for current Social Security recipients’ benefits, and nothing backs Social Security payments but their taxes.


## A transfer, not a pension


Social Security presents itself as a pension: you pay in while you work and draw out when you retire. It is actually a transfer: today’s workers pay for today’s retirees.


That was true from the first check. Ida May Fuller of Vermont, the first person to receive a monthly benefit, paid about $25 in Social Security taxes over three years. She received her first check in 1940 and lived to be 100, receiving nearly $23,000 in total. Her benefits were paid by the workers who came after her. Every generation since has passed the bill to the next.


That worked while there were many workers for each retiree. In 1950, about sixteen workers paid in for every beneficiary. Today there are fewer than three, and the number is still falling. The arithmetic of a transfer depends on the size of the next generation, and the next generation is smaller than the last.


I should say plainly that I collect Social Security myself. Isak’s generation pays for it. I am not criticizing the people who receive benefits; most of them paid in all their lives and planned around the promise, as I did. I am criticizing a system that told all of us our money was being saved for us when it was not, and that asks our grandchildren to pay for a promise they had no part in making.


And because no account holds a man’s contributions, nothing passes to his children when he dies. Apart from limited benefits for a surviving spouse or minor children, a man who works forty years and dies at sixty-four leaves his family nothing from what he paid in. A man who saved the same money in his own name would have left it to them.


## The moral question


The test I apply to every public act is this: would the neighbor on the receiving end feel loved by it? Two neighbors receive Social Security, and the system fails both.


The young worker is told that his taxes are building his own retirement. They are not. They are paying someone else’s, and his own will depend on workers who haven’t been born yet. Telling him otherwise is not love. It is a comfortable falsehood.


The retiree is told that his benefits are safe in a trust fund. They are safe only as long as the next generation is willing and able to pay for them. Telling him otherwise is not love either.


We all share the deeper problem I describe in Chapter 5 of *Transforming America*. Most of us would never take a neighbor’s money by force. But many of us will vote for a government to do it for us and call it a pension. Scripture commands us to care for the widow and the orphan and to provide for our own households: *“But if any provide not for his own, and specially for those of his own house, he hath denied the faith, and is worse than an infidel.” ***1 Timothy 5:8. ** It does not command us to do either by compelling our neighbors and calling the compulsion savings.


## What I propose


I want to be careful here, because the promise has been made, and people have built their lives around it. Breaking that faith would be a second wrong. So I propose a transition, not an abolition, and I hold each part open to a better argument.


**Keep the promise to those who relied on it.** Pay benefits for those already retired or near retirement in full. They paid in under the rules they were given.


**Give younger workers accounts they own.** A worker under a chosen age should be able to direct his share of the payroll tax into a personal retirement account in his own name, invested in the real economy, which he owns and can pass on to his children. Disability and survivor protection should continue alongside it.


**Tell the truth about the books.** The government should report its obligations to future retirees as what they are: debt. Every bond in the trust fund is a claim on future taxpayers, and the national accounts should show it.


**Return care of the needy to family and voluntary charity.** The man who cannot work, and the widow without a family to care for her, have a rightful claim on the generosity of their neighbors and their churches. Voluntary giving is honest. It lets the giver do good and know he did it, and it lets the receiver know he was cared for, not processed.


This transition has a cost. While younger workers save in their own accounts, someone still has to pay the retirees who were promised benefits. That cost doesn’t come from privatizing; it comes from money spent decades ago. Either way, it has to be paid —either by the workers of 2034 under the current system, or over a longer transition that leaves the next generation owning something instead of owing something. Personal accounts also carry market risk, and they need sound rules so that no one raids savings again under another name. I believe these costs are worth paying. The alternative is to keep passing the bill to our children, and to keep calling it a trust.


## Conclusion


Bush was right in 2005, and the essential point has not changed: the trust fund holds IOUs, not savings. What has changed is that the IOUs are now being cashed; they will run out in less than a decade, and then we will be forced to pass legislation to change the payout from 83% to the 100% that people had grown accustomed to. At that time, possibly the government will acknowledge the truth: all Social Security payments are paid by current taxes and borrowing. One would hope that the arithmetic would dispel the myth, and that the word “trust” was always a cover for theft.


A nation cannot be restored on a falsehood, even a convenient one. The first step is to call the thing by its right name. The second is to keep the promise we made to those who relied on it. The third is to stop making the same false promise to those who come after us, and to give them instead something they own, something they can pass on, and the freedom to care for one another as neighbors.

## Notes


- **President Bush’s visit.** April 5, 2005, Bureau of the Public Debt, Parkersburg, West Virginia. The New York Times editorial “Shameless Photo-Op” followed on April 7, 2005.

- **The trust fund’s holdings.** Reserves are invested in special-issue securities of the U.S. Treasury, not marketable Treasury bills. The Social Security Act Amendments of 1939 created the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance Trust Fund and required it to invest its reserves in government obligations.

- **The unified budget.** Adopted in President Johnson’s budget for fiscal year 1969, submitted in January 1968, following the recommendation of the President’s Commission on Budget Concepts (1967). Social Security was later designated “off budget” in law (1983, 1985, and the Budget Enforcement Act of 1990), but its surpluses continued to be lent to the Treasury.

- **The 1983 reforms.** Social Security Amendments of 1983, following the National Commission on Social Security Reform chaired by Alan Greenspan.

- **Current figures.** The 2026 Annual Report of the Social Security Trustees (released June 9, 2026) projects depletion of the combined OASI and DI trust funds in 2034. Social Security payments will be lowered to 83 percent of scheduled benefits payable at that time; the OASI fund alone is projected to be depleted in the fourth quarter of 2032, with 78 percent payable. Combined reserves were $2.56 trillion at the end of 2025, down $160 billion during the year. Reserves peaked at about $2.9 trillion in 2020.

- **Ida May Fuller.** Received check number 00-000-001 in January 1940. She paid $24.75 in Social Security taxes and collected $22,888.92 in benefits before her death in 1975 at age 100 (Social Security Administration history office).

- **Workers per beneficiary.** About 16.5 in 1950; about 2.7 in 2025, per the Trustees Reports.

- **Scripture.** Deuteronomy 25:13–15 and Proverbs 20:10 (divers weights); James 1:27 (the widow and the orphan); 1 Timothy 5:8.
