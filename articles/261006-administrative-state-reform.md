# Reforming the Administrative State

Oct 6, 2026 · @Thomas Abshier
## Plain language summary

The administrative state is not a natural law. It grew because Congress handed its lawmaking to agencies, because printed money removed the limit on spending, and because citizens handed their duties of charity and judgment to the state. Most of the trillions the federal government spends do not run agencies; they pay for Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, defense, and interest. So shrinking government is mainly a question of restoring charity and family responsibility, and only secondarily a question of firing bureaucrats.

The reforms proposed here: Congress votes on every major rule; every rule and agency expires unless renewed; no agency writes, enforces, and judges its own rules; any penalty may go before a jury; citizen panels and the Voting Network audit agencies; competing private certifiers carry liability for what they approve. Fund government by charging rent for the scarce things God gave to all — land, minerals, spectrum — rather than taxing a man's labor. As AI and robotics make goods abundant, most regulation (rationing) becomes unnecessary. The rent from the remaining scarce commons can pay for a limited government and a common dividend to every citizen, without making work optional for the soul.

*Provenance: Claude drafted this essay in my voice at my request on 6 October 2026, evaluating a set of proposals from Microsoft Copilot and building on the 5 October dialogue with Isak. I've marked points where I am taking a position that's still open to revision.*
## Is the administrative state inherent to government?

Some administration is inherent; its present scale is not. Any government that keeps courts, defends borders, and collects revenue needs clerks, auditors, and officers. Washington governed with four departments. Today there are fifteen, plus scores of independent agencies, and together they issue far more binding rules each year than Congress passes laws.

Five forces drive the growth, and none is a law of nature:

- **Delegation.** Congress passes broad goals ("safe," "fair," "in the public interest") and lets agencies write the actual rules. Legislators avoid blame for hard choices; agencies gain power no voter granted.

- **Concentrated benefit, dispersed cost.** A rule worth a billion dollars to one industry costs each citizen a few dollars. The industry studies and lobbies; the citizen does not notice. This is how regulators are captured.

- **No budget constraint.** When the government can borrow without limit and the Federal Reserve can buy its debt, no program must ever be cut to pay for another.

- **Moral abdication.** When citizens stopped caring for widows, orphans, and the poor through family, church, and community, the state took the duty — and the staff, rules, and money that come with it. This is the forced charity we discussed on 5 October.

- **Rationing scarcity.** Much regulation exists to decide who gets what is limited: spectrum, water, land use, licenses, medical care, permits. Where goods are scarce, and the market is distrusted, a bureau rations them.

The first three are failures of constitutional discipline. The fourth is a failure of character. The fifth is about to change, because AI and robotics are changing what is scarce. A reform plan must address all five, or the agencies will regrow from whichever root remains.
## Evaluating the Copilot proposals

Copilot's analysis is sound on structure, and I adopt most of it. Its main weakness is what it leaves out: the money creation that fuels expansion, the moral cause behind it, and the shift that abundance will bring.

What Copilot missed:

- **The fuel.** Agencies grow without limit because spending has no limit. Without ending deficit financing by money creation, every other reform leaks.

- **The moral cause.** Copilot treats the agencies as a design problem. Much of the state exists because citizens abandoned charity and family duty. Restoring those duties is what actually removes functions.

- **The jury.** Copilot proposes "independent courts or specialized tribunals." Specialized tribunals staffed by experts are part of the problem. The citizen jury is the oldest check on official power.

- **Abundance.** Copilot assumes permanent scarcity. AI and robotics will change which things are scarce, and therefore which things need regulating.

## Structural reforms

The moral justification for all of these is the same: no neighbor should be bound by a rule no elected person voted for, punished by the same office that accused him, or forced to pay for an agency that never has to show it serves him.
### 1. Congress makes the law again

Any rule with major economic effect takes force only after both houses of Congress approve it, and the President signs it. Bills like the REINS Act have proposed this. The Supreme Court began the work in 2024 when *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* ended the doctrine that courts must defer to an agency's reading of an ambiguous statute. Legislation should finish it: Congress may delegate the details of carrying out a law, never the choice of what the law requires.
### 2. Everything expires

Every regulation expires after ten years and every agency after twenty unless Congress renews it on a recorded vote. The burden shifts from "keep it until someone repeals it" to "it must justify itself." Each renewal must answer Copilot's questions: what demonstrated harm does it prevent, could ordinary fraud, contract, property, or liability law do the job, and what does it cost a small newcomer?
### 3. No agency is legislator, prosecutor, and judge

Rule-writing stays with Congress. Investigation and enforcement go to an executive office that must prove its case. Judgment goes to independent courts. Agency in-house judges, who now decide cases their own agency brings, are abolished.
### 4. A jury for every penalty

In *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024), the Supreme Court held that the Seventh Amendment guarantees a jury when the government seeks civil fraud penalties. I would extend this by statute to every fine, license revocation, or property seizure an agency seeks. Twelve citizens must agree the rule is just, that it was broken, and that the penalty fits. This is where jury nullification does its proper work: when an agency rule is unjust or absurd in a real case, ordinary neighbors can refuse to enforce it, and that refusal is information Congress must hear.
### 5. Competing certifiers who carry liability

For product safety, building codes, food, and professional standards, government sets the minimum standard by law and accredits private certifiers to test against it. Certifiers compete on accuracy, publish their records, and pay damages when they negligently approve something that harms people. Insurers, who lose money when certifiers are lax, become a second line of enforcement. A public auditor randomly re-inspects a share of every certifier's work.
### 6. Return functions to their rightful owners

Each department and program faces four questions, in order:

- Is it among the powers the Constitution grants? If not, it returns to the states or the people (Tenth Amendment).

- Is it a duty God assigns to the family, church, or charity — care of the poor, education of children? Then the transition plan moves it there.

- Can a market with liability and competing certifiers do it? Then it becomes a standard plus certifiers.

- Only what remains — defense, foreign relations, the currency, courts, interstate commerce rules, genuine national commons — stays a federal agency.

### 7. A regulatory budget

Congress sets an annual ceiling on the total compliance cost all new rules may impose. An agency that wants a new rule must retire old ones of equal cost. An office outside the agency estimates costs, and those estimates are checked against actual outcomes.
## Oversight by people who see how the world works

The people who know whether a rule works are the ones who live under it: the farmer filling out the form, the nurse charting for the auditor, the small builder waiting on the permit, and the front-line inspector who sees which rules matter. Today they have almost no voice in whether a rule survives. Three channels would give them one.
### The Voting Network as a feedback system

The Christos Voting Network can become a standing audit of every agency:

- **Who rates.** Verified citizens, with ratings weighted toward people who have actually dealt with that agency — a business that went through the permit, a veteran who used the clinic.

- **What is rated.** Specific rules and services, not the agency in general: Did this rule prevent a real harm? What did it cost you in time and money? Was the official honest and timely?

- **Inside voices.** Agency employees can report anonymously which rules they believe are useless or harmful, and where they see capture. This is protected dissent before it becomes whistleblowing.

- **What it does.** Ratings are public and must be entered into the record at each sunset renewal. A rule rated harmful by a clear majority of those who live under it triggers an automatic hearing.

The ratings are advisory, not binding. A plebiscite on every rule would replace the tyranny of the bureau with the tyranny of the loudest. The network's job is to make failures impossible to hide.

Safeguards, following Copilot's principle: the network records what people say about the exercise of public power, never what they do in private; identities are verified but votes are not published by name; and the network itself publishes its methods and is audited.
### Citizen review juries

Before any major rule takes effect, and at each renewal, a panel of twelve to twenty-five citizens is drawn by lot, as a grand jury is. They hear the agency, the affected businesses, consumers, and critics; they ask questions; and they issue a public finding on whether the rule is necessary, clear, and fair. Congress must answer the finding before voting. Random selection cannot be lobbied in advance the way an appointed board can.
### Practitioner seats

Every standard-setting board reserves a majority of seats for working practitioners on rotating terms — people currently doing the work, not former officials or industry lobbyists. Revolving-door rules bar regulators from taking jobs with firms they regulated for a fixed period, and require disclosure of any job negotiations while in office.
## Funding: what should pay for government?

### Do we need a government that spends trillions?

No — but the trillions are not where most people think. Federal spending in fiscal 2025 was roughly $7 trillion. About seventy percent went to five things: Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, defense, and interest on the debt. Interest alone now exceeds a trillion dollars a year. The salaries and offices of the regulatory agencies are a small fraction of the total.

That changes the question. Firing bureaucrats would reduce the burden of rules, which matters greatly, but it would barely move the budget. The budget falls only when the transfer programs are gradually returned to savings, family, and charity, when wars are limited to just ones, and when the debt stops growing so interest can shrink. Before the First World War the federal government spent around two or three percent of the nation's output. I am not proposing a return to 1912. I am proposing that the size of government follow from its proper functions, not the other way around.
### The income tax

In 1895, the Supreme Court held in *Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust* that a federal tax on income from property was a direct tax that had to be apportioned among the states by population, which made a general income tax impractical. The Sixteenth Amendment, ratified in 1913, removed that requirement. Some argue the ratification was defective. I do not recommend building the campaign on that argument: federal courts have consistently rejected it, and people have been penalized for relying on it. The stronger argument is moral, not procedural.

A tax on wages takes a share of a man's labor before he has decided how to use it — to feed his family, save, or give. It requires the government to know his earnings, his household, and his affairs, which is why the tax code runs to thousands of pages, and the IRS employs tens of thousands. And because it is collected by withholding, most people never feel what government costs them.
### What would be fairer?

My position, held open to a better argument: government should be funded first by charging rent for the use of what no man produced — the land, minerals, waters, airwaves, and other commons God gave to all people in common — and only second by taxing exchange.

## AI, robotics, and abundance: from rationing scarcity to stewarding the commons

Most of what the administrative state does is decide who gets what is scarce, and on what terms. Licensing decides who may practice. Permits decide who may build. Price and wage rules, quotas, subsidies, and eligibility tests decide who receives limited goods and services. When there is not enough to go around and the market is distrusted, a bureau rations.

AI and robotics are beginning to change what is scarce. When machines can produce food, housing components, clothing, transport, routine medical diagnostics, and manufactured goods at falling cost, the rationing of those goods loses its reason to exist. Much of the regulatory state was built for a world of shortage. In a world of material abundance, it becomes a cost with no purpose — or worse, a means of keeping abundance artificially scarce to protect incumbents.
### What stays scarce

Abundance in goods does not mean abundance in everything. Some things remain limited no matter how productive the machines become:

- **Land and location** — there is only so much shoreline, farmland, and downtown.

- **Energy and raw materials** — minerals, rare earths, fresh water, until new sources change that.

- **The electromagnetic spectrum and orbital slots.**

- **The machines themselves** — the computing power, models, and robot fleets, which very few may own.

- **Human time, attention, trust, and love** — which no machine supplies.

This is the key to the integrated system. In an age of abundance, government's economic role narrows to three tasks: stewarding the genuinely scarce commons, preventing anyone from monopolizing the means of production, and judging disputes. The first task also supplies the revenue, because the rent on the scarce commons is exactly the land value charge and resource royalties described above. As goods become abundant, the share of the economy's value that sits in land, resources, and spectrum rises — and so does the revenue that can be raised without taxing anyone's labor.
### The new danger: monopoly of the machines

The greatest risk of the robot economy is not unemployment. It is that ownership of the machines concentrates in a handful of companies, which then use government to protect their position — the same cartel problem Isak raised on 5 October, at a far larger scale. My earlier position paper on AI regulatory capture warned that AI licensing regimes would hand the field to whoever is already largest.

The answer follows the same principles. Govern AI by liability for harm, not by licenses to operate. Keep entry open to small builders. Enforce antitrust against anyone who controls an essential input, such as advanced chips, data centers, or foundational models, and uses it to exclude rivals. Where the risk is catastrophic and cannot be repaired after the fact — Copilot's category of delayed or catastrophic harm — keep a narrow federal role with measurable thresholds and court review.
## Work, survival, and the common dividend

### Toil is the curse; work is the calling

In the garden, before the fall, God placed man there to work it and keep it (Genesis 2:15). Work came before sin. What came with the fall was toil: by the sweat of your face you shall eat bread (Genesis 3:19). AI and robotics can lift much of the toil — the dangerous, repetitive, exhausting labor done only to survive. They do not lift the calling. A man still needs to tend, build, teach, heal, raise children, serve his neighbor, and create. A world that removed toil and also removed purpose would not be a garden; it would be a playground that rots.

So the goal is to separate survival from toil, not to separate a man from work. The expectation of a job changes from "what I must do to eat" to "what I am called to contribute."
### A dividend, not a dole

On 5 October I argued that the able-bodied man who will not work should not eat at another's expense, and I hold to that. A universal basic income funded by taxing one man's labor to support another man's idleness is forced charity on a larger scale. But a different kind of payment does not violate that principle: a share of an inheritance.

In Israel, every family received a portion of the land, and in the Jubilee year land returned to the family it belonged to (Leviticus 25), so no family was permanently cut off from the means of life. Alaska applies a modern version: residents receive an annual dividend from the state's oil royalties because the oil belongs to them in common.

A **common dividend** works the same way. It is paid from the rent on the national commons — land value, resources, spectrum — and in time from a share of the output of a robotic economy built on those commons. It is not charity, because it is each citizen's portion of what God gave to all. It is not funded by taxing labor or by printing money.
### Tied to real productivity

The dividend can only grow as fast as real production grows. It starts small, funded only by the commons rent that actually comes in, and rises as AI and robotics push down the cost of necessities and push up the value of what is produced. If productivity stalls, the dividend stalls. This keeps it honest and prevents it from becoming a political promise paid with counterfeit money.
### What the dividend must not do

- It must not replace family responsibility. A father still provides; the dividend is a floor, not a living earned by doing nothing.

- It must not replace charity. The tithe still cares for those whose needs exceed the floor: the sick, the disabled, the widow and orphan.

- It must not fund self-destruction. For a man consumed by addiction, his share may be held in trust by family or a charitable steward who provides food and shelter until he can manage it. *(Open: how to do this without creating a new bureaucracy of judges of fitness.)*

- It must not be a lever of control. It can never be conditioned on speech, belief, or political loyalty.

## The integrated system and the transition

Put together, the pieces form one economic-political system. Moral culture supplies the purpose and the charity. Free markets, with liability and competing certifiers, produce and allocate goods. A limited government stewards the commons, defends the nation, prevents monopoly, and keeps the courts. Citizens audit it through juries and the Voting Network. Rent on the commons funds both the government and each citizen's dividend.

### The transition, in three stages

No one who was promised a benefit and planned his life around it should be abandoned. The change must be gradual and announced in advance.

**First two years: stop the growth.**

- No new major rule takes effect without a vote of Congress; the President can order this for executive agencies at once.

- Every agency publishes a complete inventory of its rules, costs, and staff, opened to Voting Network ratings.

- Propose the bills and amendments: congressional approval of rules, sunsets, jury right for all penalties, a balanced budget, and a ban on the Federal Reserve financing deficits.

- Create a charity credit: a dollar-for-dollar tax credit, up to a limit, for gifts to approved charities that serve the poor, sick, and disabled. As voluntary giving rises, federal welfare spending falls by the same amount.

**Years three through ten: return what does not belong.**

- Apply the four-question test to every department and program; devolve functions to states, charity, and markets on published schedules.

- Begin the land value charge and resource royalties, lowering income tax rates as that revenue grows.

- Honor Social Security and Medicare in full for those near or in retirement; give younger workers personal accounts they own and can pass to their children.

**After ten years: complete the system.**

- Repeal the income and payroll taxes by constitutional amendment.

- Begin the common dividend when commons revenue exceeds the cost of limited government, and let it rise with real productivity.

Each stage depends on the one before. Without honest money and a balanced budget, devolution leaks back. Without restored charity, the welfare functions cannot leave the state. Without real productivity gains, the dividend cannot grow. That is why this is a single system and not a list of separate reforms.
## Open questions and editorial notes

### Questions still open

- **Who receives the dividend** — each person, or each household through its head, to strengthen the family as the unit of provision?

- **The addicted and the incapable** — how to hold a share in trust without creating a new bureaucracy that judges fitness.

- **The land value charge and the family farm** — what homestead exemption or deferral keeps a land-rich, cash-poor farmer or widow from being taxed off the land?

- **Catastrophic risks** — exactly which areas (pandemics, systemic finance, advanced AI) justify a standing federal role, and with what measurable thresholds.

- **Pacing** — can AI-robotics productivity arrive fast enough to fund the dividend before the transfer programs wind down, or must the stages overlap longer?

### Editorial notes

- **Figures are approximate.** Fiscal 2025 federal spending (about $7 trillion), the roughly seventy percent share of the five largest items, interest above $1 trillion, and pre-1914 federal spending at two to three percent of output are from general knowledge and should be checked against Congressional Budget Office and Treasury data before publication.

- **Court cases.** *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024) overruled *Chevron* deference. *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024) held that the Seventh Amendment requires a jury when the SEC seeks civil penalties for securities fraud; extending that to all agency penalties would take legislation or further rulings. *Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust* was decided in 1895.

- **Sixteenth Amendment.** The claim that ratification was improper rests mainly on Bill Benson's *The Law That Never Was* (1985). Courts have repeatedly rejected it, and the IRS lists it among frivolous positions that carry penalties.

- **Land value tax.** The idea is chiefly associated with Henry George's *Progress and Poverty* (1879). Economists from Adam Smith to Milton Friedman described a tax on land value as among the least harmful taxes.

- **Alaska Permanent Fund.** The annual dividend has been paid since 1982 from earnings on a fund built from oil revenue.
