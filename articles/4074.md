# 261019 - Virginia Abortion Bill

# The Same Child on Both Sides of Birth


##  Virginia’s Question 1


*Dr. Thomas | October 19, 2026*


---


On October 19, Drs. Robert and Jill Malone published a commentary titled “The Babies in the Drain.” They live in Madison County, Virginia, and on November 3 they will vote on Question 1, a proposed amendment that would write a “fundamental right to reproductive freedom” into Virginia’s Bill of Rights. Their piece opens with a 1988 discovery at Ashkelon: the bones of nearly a hundred newborns in the drain beneath a fourth-century Roman bathhouse. No one knows exactly how those children died. What we do know is that the ancient world often treated the killing or abandonment of unwanted infants as a private family matter, and that it took the spread of Jewish and Christian teaching on the sanctity of every human life to change that.


The Malones then ask the question beneath Virginia’s vote: at what point does a child’s life deserve the law’s protection, independent of the wishes of the adults deciding about him?


I want to take up that question, because it is not only Virginia’s. Since *Dobbs* returned the regulation of abortion to the states, every state will face some version of it, and every citizen should be able to say where he stands and why.


## The moral foundation comes first


Before looking at the legal text, I want to state the moral standard I am using, so that the reader can judge my reasoning by it rather than by my conclusions.


I test every public policy by three questions, in order:


- **Is it righteous?** Does it accord with what God has established as right — in this case, that innocent human life is not to be taken?

- **If so, is it loving?** Does it serve the real good of every neighbor it touches?

- **Would I want it done to me?** If I stood in the place of each person affected—feeling their situation in my own body, mind, and heart—would I accept this treatment as just?


Notice that the third question requires us to stand in the place of *everyone* affected. In a late pregnancy there are at least two people in the room: a mother, often frightened and sometimes genuinely in danger, and a child who can already live outside her. A law that considers only one of them has not passed the test.


## What Question 1 actually says


The text is short enough to read in full, and every voter should. The operative provisions are these:


- Every individual has a fundamental right to make and carry out decisions about prenatal care, childbirth, postpartum care, contraception, abortion care, miscarriage management, and fertility care.

- That right may not be “directly or indirectly” burdened unless the state shows a *compelling state interest* achieved by the *least restrictive means* — the strictest standard in American law.

- A state interest counts as compelling **only** if it is for “the limited purpose of maintaining or improving the health of an individual seeking care,” and even then it may not infringe on that individual’s autonomous decision-making.

- The state may regulate abortion in the third trimester, but may never prohibit one that “in the professional judgment of a physician is medically indicated to protect the life or physical or mental health of the pregnant individual,” or where a physician judges the fetus not viable.

- The state may not penalize or prosecute “any individual” who assists another in exercising the right with her voluntary consent.

- The section is self-executing: courts enforce it directly, without enabling legislation.


## What would change


The Malones’ summary of the changes is accurate, and I will restate it here:


**From three physicians to one.** Virginia law today (Code § 18.2-74) permits a third-trimester abortion only in a licensed hospital, and only when the attending physician and two consulting physicians certify that continuing the pregnancy is likely to cause the woman’s death or to “substantially and irremediably” impair her physical or mental health. It also requires life-support measures to be available when there is clearly visible evidence of viability. Question 1 replaces that with the judgment of a single physician that the procedure is “medically indicated.”


**It shifts from a defined threshold to an undefined one.** “Substantial and irremediable” impairment is a high bar. “Medically indicated to protect … mental health” sets no minimum at all. Anxiety, distress, and fear about the future are real burdens, and every one of them bears on mental health. The amendment does not say where the line falls; it hands that question to one physician and then forbids the state from second-guessing him.


**The child disappears from the list of compelling interests.** To me, this is the heart of the matter. The amendment’s own definition says that the *only* compelling state interest is the health of the person seeking care. The child’s life is not on the list. In the third trimester, the state retains a limited power to regulate, but through the second trimester — including the weeks from about 22 to 27 when many babies born prematurely now survive with neonatal care — the state would have no constitutionally recognized reason to protect the child at all. Current Virginia statute already permits abortion for any reason in that period. Question 1 makes that permanent and places it beyond the reach of any future legislature.


**“Any individual.”** The ballot tells voters the amendment protects “doctors, nurses, and patients.” The text protects “any individual” who assists. Defenders argue that existing licensing laws remain in force; critics argue that a licensing prosecution is exactly the kind of “adverse action” the amendment forbids. The point here is not that one side is certainly right. A voter reading only the ballot would not know the question exists.


**Permanence.** A statute can be corrected by the next General Assembly. A constitutional amendment requires two separately elected legislatures and another statewide vote. Whatever mistakes it contains will stand for a long time.


## What the ballot does not tell voters


The ballot question says the amendment would “allow for restrictions on access to abortion during the third trimester of pregnancy except when the patient’s health is at risk, or the pregnancy cannot survive.” It does not mention that the three-physician requirement becomes one, that “substantial and irremediable” becomes “medically indicated,” that “health” explicitly includes mental health, or that the life of the unborn child is excluded from the definition of a compelling state interest.


The Association of American Physicians and Surgeons and others sued in Tazewell County in April, arguing that the ballot language misleads by omission. As of late August, no ruling had been issued. The court dismissed a separate procedural challenge in July, and it is headed to the Virginia Supreme Court.


I will say plainly what I think of the ballot language: a summary that omits the most consequential changes to existing law does not give voters what they need to consent. Consent obtained without disclosure is not consent. That is true in medicine, and it is true at the ballot box.


## The strongest case for the amendment


Supporters say that third-trimester abortions are rare, and that most of them involve devastating diagnoses discovered late — conditions incompatible with life — or real threats to the mother. They say that requiring three physicians to agree in a crisis causes delay, and delay can cost a woman her health or her life. They say that a woman and her doctor know her situation better than any legislator, and that a constitutional protection is needed precisely because legislatures change. Polling shows a majority of Virginians are inclined to agree.


These are serious concerns, and some are valid. A woman in genuine danger must be treated without bureaucratic delay. A family that learns their child cannot survive birth deserves compassion, not suspicion.


## My answer


As a physician, I want to make one point this debate too often leaves out.


**After viability, ending a pregnancy and ending a child’s life are not the same thing.** When a mother’s health genuinely requires that a pregnancy end at 30 or 34 weeks, the pregnancy can be ended by delivery — induction or cesarean — and the child can be cared for. The mother is protected, and so is the child. Some cases are hard, and I do not pretend medicine is always simple. But in the great majority of situations where a third-trimester pregnancy must end for the mother’s sake, the child does not have to die for her to be safe.


That is why the amendment’s language matters so much. A law that protected the mother’s life and health *and* required that a viable child be delivered and cared for wherever possible would answer every legitimate concern supporters raise. Question 1 does not do that. It protects the mother’s health under a standard with no defined floor and gives the child no recognized interest.


The “fetus is not viable” clause already covers the tragic cases of lethal diagnoses. The rest of the third-trimester protection, read alongside the undefined mental-health standard, reaches much further than the hard cases supporters describe.


## Where I qualify the Malones’ stand


I agree with the Malones’ central argument, and I respect the care they took to mark the limits of the evidence. But two parts of their piece warrant qualification.


First, the section on fetal-tissue markets. The Malones themselves conclude that they found no evidence connecting tissue procurement to the amendment. The history they recount is worth investigating on its own terms. If this speculation has merit, we should include it in our consideration. A case this serious should rest only on what we know.


Second, I would avoid language suggesting the amendment legalizes infanticide. It does not, and the Malones say so. Killing a child after birth remains a crime in Virginia and under federal law. The honest case — and it is strong enough — is that the amendment draws a legal line at birth that has no biological meaning, and then removes the state’s power to protect the child on the near side of it.


## The question itself


A child at 34 weeks is the same living human being a moment before delivery and a moment after. Nothing in his nature changes as he passes through the birth canal. Only his legal status changes. The born-alive rule of English common law drew that line because medicine could not see into the womb. Today it can. We watch the heartbeat, measure the growth, and save children born months early. The law has not caught up with what we know.


The philosophers the Malones cite — Singer, Giubilini, and Minerva — have seen this clearly and drawn the opposite conclusion: if birth makes no moral difference, then perhaps a newborn has no more claim to life than a viable unborn child. That argument should frighten us, because it is logically sound if one accepts its premise. The answer is not to deny the logic. It is to reject the premise — to hold that the child’s worth does not depend on what he can do, or on whether he is wanted, but on what he is.


So I return to the three questions.


**Is it righteous** to write into a constitution that the life of a child who could live outside the womb is not a reason the state may protect him? I do not believe it is.


**Is it loving?** Love for the mother requires that she receive prompt, skilled care when she is in danger. Love for the child requires that he not be killed when he could be delivered and saved. A law can do both. This one does only the first.


**Would I want it done to me?** If I were that child, I would want the law to see me. If I were that mother, frightened and in danger, I would want my doctor free to act at once — and I would want him to save my baby too.


## What I would ask of Virginians


I am not a Virginian, and the decision is yours. But I would ask you to read the full text of the amendment, not only the ballot summary, before you vote. If, having read it, you conclude that it protects women without abandoning children who could live, vote for it with a clear conscience. I have read it, and I have concluded the opposite: that it would remove from your constitution the one thing a just law owes the weakest person in the room — recognition that he is there.


I hold this position as an argument, not a decree. If someone can show me a reading of this text that protects the viable child, I will listen. Until then, I believe the right vote on Question 1 is no.


The children in the drain at Ashkelon had no one to speak for them. The children of Virginia still do.
