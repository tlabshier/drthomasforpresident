# 261030 - Justice in Whistleblowing

# No Hearing, No Justice:


### Sibel Edmonds and the Secret That Outranked the Truth


*Dr Thomas — Position Paper  | 30 October 2026*


## The Story in Brief


Sibel Edmonds was born in 1970 and grew up in Iran and Turkey, the daughter of a physician. She came to America at eighteen, studied criminal justice and psychology, married an American, and became a citizen. She spoke Turkish, Farsi, and Azerbaijani — languages the FBI badly needed after September 11, 2001, when it found itself holding mountains of intercepted calls and documents that too few people could read.


Within days of the attacks, the Bureau hired her as a contract linguist in its Washington Field Office, with a Top Secret clearance. She came to serve the country that had taken her in.


What she found troubled her. By her account, recordings tied to terrorism and counterintelligence cases sat untranslated, some translations were careless, and a supervisor told linguists to slow down so the backlog would look larger and justify a bigger budget. Then she reviewed the work of a fellow Turkish linguist and concluded that this co-worker had left out material from wiretaps — material about people seeking American military and intelligence secrets. Edmonds also reported that this same co-worker had earlier invited her to join a Turkish organization of the kind that was itself a target of FBI surveillance.


She reported all of this up the chain of command, repeatedly. In March 2002 she took her concerns to the Senate Judiciary Committee. On March 22, 2002, after a little more than six months on the job, the FBI terminated her contract and called her disruptive. The co-worker kept her job.


In the summer of 2002 the FBI acknowledged to Senate staff that the co-worker had in fact omitted important information. Senators Chuck Grassley and Patrick Leahy began asking questions. Edmonds sued.


Then the government did something it rarely does. In October 2002 Attorney General John Ashcroft invoked the *state secrets privilege*, telling the court that the case could not go forward at all without endangering national security. In May 2004 the Justice Department went further and retroactively classified information about her case that the FBI had already given to Congress in unclassified briefings — including material that senators had published. In July 2004 a federal judge dismissed her lawsuit on state secrets grounds. Her deposition in a separate lawsuit, brought by families of September 11 victims, was blocked on the same grounds. In November 2005 the Supreme Court declined to hear her appeal.


She never had a trial. No jury ever heard her.


One official finding did reach the public. In January 2005 the Justice Department’s own Inspector General released an unclassified summary of his investigation. He found that many of her allegations were supported, that the FBI had not taken them seriously enough, that its investigation of the co-worker was significantly flawed, and that her allegations were the most significant factor in the FBI’s decision to terminate her. He observed that firing her for raising concerns could discourage others from raising theirs. Because she was a contractor rather than an employee, she was not even covered by the FBI’s whistleblower regulations.


After a watchdog group sued over the retroactive classification, the Justice Department reversed itself and conceded that the information was not, after all, a danger to national security.


More than twenty years later, her case has still never been tried on its merits.


## The Moral Question


This site asks three questions of every public act. *Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? Would I want it done to me?*


**Is it righteous?** Scripture sets the standard for how a people judges. “Ye shall do no unrighteousness in judgment” (Leviticus 19:15). “He that is first in his own cause seemeth just; but his neighbour cometh and searcheth him” (Proverbs 18:17). And when the rulers in Jerusalem wanted to condemn Jesus without hearing Him, Nicodemus asked the question that should be carved over every courthouse door: “Doth our law judge any man, before it hear him, and know what he doeth?” (John 7:51).


What was done to Sibel Edmonds fails this test at its root. One party to the dispute — the government — decided that the other party would not be heard, and the court accepted that decision without the evidence ever being weighed. The accused institution became the judge of whether it could be accused. That is not a close call. It is a reversal of the order God establishes for judgment, in which the powerful and the weak stand equal before the law and the matter is searched out before it is decided.


There is a second failure. “He that covereth his sins shall not prosper: but whoso confesseth and forsaketh them shall have mercy” (Proverbs 28:13). By the government’s own later admission, part of what was classified was not secret at all. When secrecy is used to cover error rather than to protect the nation, it is concealment, and a government that conceals its own wrongs teaches its servants to do the same.


**Is it loving?** Love for the neighbor in the public square means that the citizen who tells the truth about a danger to his fellow citizens is protected, not punished. Edmonds was warning of failures that bore directly on the safety of Americans in the months after the worst attack on our soil. Silencing her did not love her, and it did not love the people she was trying to protect.


**Would I want it done to me?** Put yourself in her place. You join the government in a crisis because you have a rare skill. You find what looks like a breach. You report it the right way, to your superiors and then to Congress. You are dismissed, and when you go to court the government tells the judge that your case is too secret to exist. You are not allowed to show the evidence that would vindicate you, and the government is not required to show the evidence that would justify itself. No one who has committed himself to righteousness would accept that for himself. We should not accept it for anyone.


## The Constitutional Question


The moral judgment comes first; the law should follow it.


The Constitution gives the judicial power to the courts (Article III), guarantees a jury in civil suits at common law (Seventh Amendment), and protects the right to petition the government for redress of grievances (First Amendment). The state secrets privilege appears nowhere in that text. It is a doctrine the courts themselves built, and its founding case should give us pause.


In *United States v. Reynolds* (1953), the widows of three civilians killed in the crash of a B-29 bomber sued for the Air Force accident report. The government said the report contained military secrets, and the Supreme Court let it withhold the report without the judges even reading it. Nearly fifty years later the report was declassified. It contained no state secrets. It showed that the crash was caused by poor maintenance of the aircraft. The doctrine that has since shut the courthouse door on countless citizens was born from a cover-up of negligence.


I hold that the jury is the conscience of the community and the last line of defense against an overreaching state — which is why I regard jury nullification as a cornerstone of our liberty. The state secrets privilege, as applied in cases like Edmonds’s, removes that conscience altogether. The case never reaches twelve citizens who might weigh the government’s conduct and find it wanting. When the executive can decide which cases against itself may be heard, the separation of powers has quietly failed.


## Where I Stand


I stand with Sibel Edmonds on the central point: she was entitled to be heard, and the government’s use of secrecy to end her case before it began was wrong. Our own Justice Department’s watchdog found that she was terminated because she spoke up. That finding alone should have produced a remedy. It produced none.


There are honest limits to what we know, and I want to name them. The Inspector General did not conclude that the co-worker was a spy; he found that the FBI failed to investigate the question properly. Some of Edmonds’s larger claims — about foreign influence networks reaching into Congress and about bribery — have never been proven. Some details of the popular retelling of her story are imprecise. But these uncertainties exist *because* the claims were never tested. Uncertainty created by the government’s refusal to allow a hearing cannot be counted in the government’s favor. The proper response to “we do not know” is “then let us find out,” under oath, before a judge and jury.


## Why the Concern Remains


Some will say this is one old case. I do not think it is, for several reasons.


First, the incentives have not changed. An agency whose budget grows with its backlog has a reason to keep the backlog large. An institution embarrassed by a breach has a reason to remove the person who exposed it. These are ordinary human temptations, and power without accountability magnifies them.


Second, the pattern has precedent. *Reynolds* began with a claimed secret that turned out to be a hidden failure. In Edmonds’s case, material declared too sensitive for Congress was later admitted to be harmless. When the claim of secrecy is tested, it has more than once turned out to be a claim of convenience.


Third, the doctrine is still with us. The privilege that ended Edmonds’s case remains available to every administration, of every party, and the courts still too often accept it on the government’s word.


Fourth, the people most likely to see wrongdoing inside our security agencies — contractors and employees with clearances — are the people with the least protection when they report it. Edmonds was a contractor and fell outside the FBI’s whistleblower rules entirely. A nation that punishes its truth-tellers will soon have none.


## What I Propose


I offer these as positions I hold and will defend, open to a better argument.


- **No case dismissed on the government’s word alone.** A judge must personally examine the evidence the government claims is secret before applying the privilege. The privilege should apply item by item to specific evidence, never as a blanket bar that ends the whole case.

- **The party who keeps the secret pays its price.** In criminal cases the government must choose between disclosing evidence and dropping the prosecution. The same principle should hold in civil cases against the government: if evidence the government withholds is necessary to decide the case, the government may not win by its silence. Where the facts cannot be tried, the presumption should favor the citizen who was harmed, not the institution that holds the secret.

- **Cleared counsel and secure proceedings.** Where real secrets are involved, courts should use cleared attorneys, sealed hearings, and unclassified summaries so the case can proceed rather than vanish.

- **No retroactive gagging of Congress.** Information lawfully given to Congress or the public in unclassified form should not be reclassified to win a lawsuit. Any reclassification should require written justification reviewable by the courts and the oversight committees.

- **Whistleblower protection for everyone who holds a clearance.** Contractors and employees alike should have a protected path to report wrongdoing to Congress and to the Inspectors General, and a right to bring retaliation claims before a jury.

- **Inspector General findings must carry consequences.** When the government’s own Inspector General finds that a person was terminated for reporting misconduct, that finding should trigger reinstatement or compensation and accountability for the officials responsible, rather than sitting in a file.


## Conclusion


Sibel Edmonds came to this country, took up its cause in its hour of need, told the truth as she saw it through the proper channels, and was removed for it — by the finding of the government’s own watchdog. Then she was denied the one thing every citizen is owed: a hearing.


A free people does not judge a man before it hears him. It does not let the accused be his own judge. And it does not allow secrecy to become a shelter for error. The test is simple, and it is ancient: is it righteous, is it loving, would I want it done to me? On all three counts, what was done here fails, and the law that allowed it should be changed.
