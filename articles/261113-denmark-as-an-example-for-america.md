# 261113 - Denmark as an Example for America

# What Denmark Really Teaches America


### *The disciplines worth copying, and the bill that comes with them*

By Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | 13 November 2026


---


## Why I am writing about Denmark

For years Americans have been told that Denmark proves what a big government can do: free health care, free college, a gentle safety net, and a contented people. If we were wiser, the argument goes, we would build the same thing here. Today Tom Woods, the libertarian writer who sends a daily email from the small-government school of Ludwig von Mises, answered that argument with a list he translated from a French post on X. It was titled, in effect, "the Denmark they kept from you." The list says Denmark spends less than France, runs budget surpluses, has no minimum wage, lets employers dismiss workers easily, demands that the unemployed look hard for work, keeps a tight immigration policy, taxes corporations lightly, has no wealth tax, and ranks near the top of the world in economic freedom. I checked each item. Most of it is true. But the list leaves out the single biggest fact about Denmark, and that missing fact changes the lesson. I stand on this side: America should adopt the disciplines that make Denmark work, but not the size of Denmark's state or the taxes that pay for it. Below are my reasons, the evidence, and the places where the evidence is less certain than either side admits.

## The list, checked

Here is what holds up when I go to the primary sources. **Spending lower than France.** True. Denmark's government spent about 48 percent of its economy in 2025; France spent about 57 percent. The gap is roughly nine points, close to the ten the list claims. **Surpluses and low debt.** True. Denmark ran a surplus of 2.9 percent of GDP in 2025, one of only five EU countries in surplus, and its government debt is under 30 percent of GDP. France's debt is about 116 percent. From 2019 through 2024, Denmark ran the largest budget surplus in the EU every year. **No statutory minimum wage.** True. Denmark has no wage floor in law. Wages are set in collective agreements between employers and unions, which cover roughly 80 percent of employees. So the floor is set by private contract rather than statute, but most workers have a floor. **Easy dismissal.** Mostly true. Denmark's labor model, which the Danes call "flexicurity," lets employers dismiss with short notice and little or no severance. Salaried employees have legal notice periods ranging from one month to six months, depending on years of service, and long-serving staff are protected from unreasonable dismissal. "A simple letter" is close, but it is a letter with a notice period attached. **Unemployment of 3 percent.** True by Denmark's own register-based measure, which stood at 3.1 percent in September 2026. By the international survey method that the European Commission uses, Danish unemployment is about 6 percent. Both numbers are honest; they count different things. A fair comparison with America uses the survey measure. **Private health insurance covers about 48 percent.** True, with an important qualification. Every resident is covered by the public system. The private policies mostly pay for what the public system does not, such as copayments, dental care, and prescriptions, or they buy faster access. Danes do not use private insurance in place of public care; they buy it on top of public care they have already paid for in taxes. **Strict immigration and the "paradigm shift."** True. In 2019, Denmark formally changed the purpose of refugee protection from integration to eventual return. Permanent residence normally requires eight years of legal residence, a Danish language test, and three and a half years of full-time work out of the last four. **Hard conditions on unemployment benefits.** True in substance. Recipients must log their job search weekly, document applications, widen the search beyond their own field when jobs are scarce, accept referred work on a day's notice, and accept commutes of up to four hours a day. Failure brings extra interviews and loss of benefits. (The list's "two applications per week" overstates the rule at the funds I checked; the requirement is weekly logging with a monthly minimum of documented applications.) **Civil servants barred from striking.** True for the small group with formal civil-servant status. Most public employees are hired under ordinary collective agreements, as the list says. **Low corporate tax and no wealth tax.** True. The corporate rate is 22 percent, below the OECD average of about 24 percent. Denmark's Social Democrats abolished the wealth tax in 1997. **Economic freedom.** True. The Heritage Foundation's 2026 Index ranks Denmark 7th in the world and France 65th. On every item but one detail, the list is accurate. If the question is whether Denmark is a socialist economy, the answer is no. It is a market economy with secure property, open trade, and a flexible labor market.

## What the list leaves out: the bill

Now, the fact the list omits. In 2024, Denmark collected 45.2 percent of its entire economy in taxes, the highest share of any country in the OECD. France was second, at 43.5 percent. The United States collected about 25.6 percent. Read that again in light of the list. The country held up as the alternative to France taxes its people more heavily than France does. Denmark spends less than France and taxes more than France, and that is how it runs surpluses. Denmark does not have a small government. It has a large government that pays its bills in full, in cash, every year. The burden falls on ordinary earners, not only the rich. Denmark charges a 25 percent value-added tax on nearly everything sold, with almost no reduced rates. In January 2026, it added a new top income-tax bracket, raising the top marginal rate from 55.9 percent to 60.5 percent. Earlier this year, the sitting prime minister campaigned on bringing the wealth tax back. She dropped it in May 2026 to form a coalition, so the item survives, but only by a political bargain, not by settled conviction. So the honest summary is this: Denmark has the economic freedoms the list describes, and the welfare state its American admirers describe, and the Danes pay for the second with the heaviest tax burden in the developed world. Both camps in our debate have been telling half the story.

## The moral test

I do not judge a policy by whether it works on a spreadsheet. I ask three questions in order. Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? And would I want it done to me, measured not by my momentary appetite but by the self I am trying to become before God? Here is how Denmark's policies fare.

### Paying what you owe

*"The wicked borroweth, and payeth not again"* **(Psalm 37:21)**. Denmark taxes for what it spends. It does not hand the bill to its grandchildren. America does the opposite: we vote for benefits, refuse to vote for the taxes that would pay for them, and borrow the difference. Our national debt now exceeds the size of our entire annual economy. On this point, Denmark is more righteous than we are, and I would rather learn from it than lecture it. Whatever size our government is, we should pay for it.

### Help that expects work

*"If any would not work, neither should he eat"* **(2 Thessalonians 3:10)**. Denmark helps the unemployed generously and requires them to look for work, widen their search, and take the job offered. That is righteous. It is also loving, because love wants the neighbor to stand up again, not to settle into dependence. And it passes the third test: if I lost my work, I would want my neighbors to carry me for a season, and I would want them to expect me to get back on my feet. Help with no expectation of effort treats a man as less than a man.

### Freedom of contract in work

Denmark sets no minimum wage by law and lets employers end a job with notice. Security comes from the unemployment fund, not from chaining the employer to the employee. I count this as righteous because it respects both parties as free men making an agreement. Notice the Danish method, too: the wage floor is set by voluntary associations of workers and employers bargaining with each other, not by politicians in the capital. That is closer to the American principle of self-government than our own federal minimum wage is.

### Borders, language, and the stranger

Scripture holds two truths together. *"The stranger that dwelleth with you shall be unto you as one born among you, and thou shalt love him as thyself"* **(Leviticus 19:34)**, and *"One law shall be to him that is homeborn, and unto the stranger"* **(Exodus 12:49)**. Denmark asks newcomers to learn the language, work, and support themselves before they are granted permanent standing. I stand with that principle. A nation that loves the stranger invites him into the common life, and that common life requires a common tongue and a common duty to work. Here is the third question: Since 2019, Denmark has reviewed the protection of Syrian refugees from Damascus and revoked it for some of them, on the judgment that the city was safe. Its critics, including the UN refugee agency, dispute that judgment. Would I want to be sent back to a place still dangerous? No. So I hold to temporary protection as the default and return as the goal, with this condition: return must be to genuine safety, judged honestly, not to a safety declared for convenience. I hold that position: give refuge to the persecuted stranger.

But compassion for the refugee cannot be the only principle governing a nation's relationship with him. A host nation also owes a duty to its own people and to the faith and law that formed it. When newcomers arrive faster than they can be joined to the common life, and when some bring Islam, a religion that teaches a law of its own above the law of the land, they can, over generations, reshape towns, then leadership, then culture. In parts of Britain and Europe this is not a distant prospect; it is visible now in particular cities and boroughs. In the 2021 census of England and Wales, Muslims were 6.5 percent of the population nationally, but about 40 percent in the London borough of Tower Hamlets and about 35 percent in Blackburn and in Newham. In the same census, those calling themselves Christian fell below half of the population for the first time, from 59 percent to 46 percent in a decade. The bigger change is not only that another faith is growing; it is that the faith which once anchored those nations is emptying out.

Within a century of Muhammad, Islamic armies had conquered the Christian lands of the Middle East and North Africa and crossed into Spain. The Crusades, whatever their sins, began as a response to that advance and to Christian Byzantium's plea for help. Spain was not fully reclaimed until 1492, and Ottoman armies stood at the gates of Vienna as late as 1683. Today the Crusades are taught almost entirely as a story of Western aggression, and that one-sided telling has persuaded much of the West that it owes a debt to be repaid by opening its doors without condition. The excesses of the Crusaders must be acknowledged and put in proportion. A nation does not atone for the eleventh century by surrendering its culture in the twenty-first, and it does not prove its tolerance by abandoning the discernment between the God of the Bible and other gods that made its tolerance possible.

### What a sound refugee policy requires

Scripture gives the model of the stranger rightly received. Ruth the Moabitess did not merely reside in Israel; she joined it: *"thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God"* **(Ruth 1:16)**. And Nehemiah grieved when the children of Judah *"spake half in the speech of Ashdod, and could not speak in the Jews' language"* **(Nehemiah 13:24)**. A nation may welcome the stranger, but it must welcome him into its own life, not beside it. From that principle I draw six requirements.


 	- **Temporary protection by default.** Refuge is granted for as long as the danger lasts, and reviewed regularly. Britain has now adopted the Danish model: refugee status is reviewed every 30 months, and a refugee waits 20 years rather than five before he can apply to settle permanently.

 	- **Allegiance before permanence.** No one should receive permanent status who will not affirm that the Constitution and the laws of the land rank above any religious legal code, including sharia. Those who work to overturn that order should lose their status. In America, the First Amendment makes a religious test for entry the wrong instrument; a test of civic allegiance is the right one. It judges a man by what he holds and does, not by his ancestry.

 	- **Integration required, not merely hoped for.** A common language, honest work, and self-support are the conditions of staying. Denmark has set out to end its "parallel societies," neighborhoods that live apart from the nation, and the number of areas on its official list fell from 26 in 2018 to five in 2025. I share that goal. But Denmark sorts residents by national origin, and I would measure people by their conduct and allegiance instead.

 	- **Family reunification that is limited and conditional.** Bringing relatives should follow integration and continued need for protection, not precede them.

 	- **Enforced return to genuine safety.** Those with no right to stay must leave. In October 2026, the European Union gave final approval to rules allowing member states to send people ordered to leave to "return hubs" in third countries, on the condition that no one is sent back to persecution. That condition is the right one, and it must be honored in fact, not only on paper.

 	- **Count all migration, not only refugees.** Most Muslim migration to Europe has come through ordinary channels: work, study, and family. In Pew Research Center's projections, even with no further refugees at all, continued regular migration would roughly double Europe's Muslim share, from about 5 percent in 2016 to about 11 percent by 2050. A refugee policy alone cannot address the concern; the whole immigration system must be governed by the same principle of joining the common life.


America is not where Britain and Europe are. The same Pew work projected the Muslim share of the United States at about 2 percent by 2050. That is not a reason for complacency. It is a reason to choose wisely now, while the choice is still ours to make.

### The size of the take

When Israel asked for a king, Samuel warned them what the king would cost: *"He will take the tenth of your seed, and of your vineyards… He will take the tenth of your sheep: and ye shall be his servants"* **(1 Samuel 8:15, 17)**. A tenth was the warning. Denmark takes more than four tenths of everything its people produce, and spends it on their behalf. This is where Denmark fails my third question. I would not want four of every ten dollars I earn taken from me to be spent by others for my supposed good, even if the services were excellent. The care of the poor, the sick, and the old belongs first to the family, then to the church, then to the neighbor, and to the state only as a last resort. A government that takes over all three first duties becomes, in Samuel's word, a master, however kind. I grant the Danes this: they chose their system freely, at the ballot box, and they keep choosing it. A free people may decide to pay that price. I will not urge Americans to make the same choice.

## The question under the question

Why do so many Americans point to Denmark? I do not think most of them are reaching for a 25 percent sales tax or a 60 percent income-tax rate. They are reaching for a society where nobody falls through the floor, where people trust one another, where work is expected and honored, and where the public accounts balance. That longing is good. It is close to what the Bible calls a righteous nation: *"Righteousness exalteth a nation: but sin is a reproach to any people"* **(Proverbs 14:34)**. Denmark's system runs on something its budget cannot buy: trust. It is a small, long-settled nation shaped by centuries of shared faith and a common work ethic. Its welfare state works partly because most Danes do not cheat it. That trust is moral capital, accumulated over generations. A government can spend moral capital; it cannot create it. And no tax rate can transplant it into a nation of 340 million people that has been spending down its own. So the lesson I take from Denmark is not "build a bigger state" and not "Denmark is secretly libertarian." The lesson is that the parts of Denmark that work are moral disciplines: pay what you owe, expect work from those who can work, guard the common life, honor free agreement. Those disciplines are within America's reach without Denmark's tax burden, and they are what I want my country to recover.

## What I would urge

As a candidate, my role is to lead by voice: to take a position, give my reasons, and stay open to a better argument. Here is where I stand.


 	- **Pay for what we spend.** Treat a balanced federal budget, and then a falling national debt, as a moral obligation, not an accounting preference. If a program is worth having, it is worth paying for now.

 	- **Help that expects effort.** Every able-bodied adult receiving public aid should actively seek work and accept suitable work offered. Help the person who falls; expect him to rise.

 	- **An immigration system that asks for integration and allegiance.** Legal entry, a common language, honest work, an affirmed allegiance to the Constitution above any religious legal code, and a long, earned path to permanent standing, with protection for those in real danger until real safety returns.

 	- **Keep the American tax burden low.** No national value-added tax and no federal wealth tax. A competitive corporate rate. Keep the share of our economy taken by government well below the European level.

 	- **Restore the first welfare system.** Encourage the family, the church, and voluntary associations to take back the work of caring for neighbors, so the state is the last resort rather than the first.


## A word on health care

Mr. Woods's email went on to health costs, and the same principle applies there. Danish care is described as free, but it is paid for through the taxes above, and nearly half of Danes buy private insurance on top of it. An honest comparison with America looks at the total cost a family bears, in taxes and premiums together, not at the price at the counter. (His email closed by promoting a health cost-sharing service he uses and is paid to recommend. I make no judgment on that product here; this essay is about policy.)

## Why I believe the concern is real

The worry behind Mr. Woods's email is that Americans are being sold Denmark with the price tag removed. I think that worry is well founded, for three reasons. First, the benefits of a welfare state are visible, and the costs are spread thin across every purchase and paycheck, so you can always make the case by naming the benefits alone. Second, there is a political motive to keep it that way: a politician who promises Danish benefits wins votes, and one who also proposes Danish taxes loses them, which is how a nation ends up with the benefits and the borrowing but not the taxes. That is, in fact, the path America has taken. Third, the things that make Denmark's system bearable, its small size, its high trust, and its habit of paying its bills, are the very things the sales pitch never mentions.

## Sources and their limits

My figures come mostly from official and mainstream sources: Eurostat, the European Commission, the OECD, Statistics Denmark, Danish government and unemployment-fund guidance, the Commonwealth Fund, the Tax Foundation, and the Heritage Foundation. That is the record most likely to be accurate on numbers, but it is also the curated record, and I want to be plain about where it is thin.


 	- **Unemployment** depends on the measure. Denmark's register count (about 3 percent) and the international survey method (about 6 percent) are both official.

 	- **Tax-to-GDP comparisons** depend on how pensions and social contributions are counted. Denmark funds much of its social spending through general taxes, whereas other countries use payroll contributions, which raises its ratio somewhat. The ranking holds, but the gap is smaller than it looks.

 	- **The Heritage Foundation, a conservative think tank, publishes the economic-freedom index**. Its rankings are widely used, but its weighting reflects its philosophy.

 	- **I could not confirm the list's claim that Denmark ranks 4th in the OECD for ease of doing business, with France second to last**. The World Bank discontinued its well-known "Doing Business" ranking in 2021, so the figure may come from an older edition or another index.

 	- **The immigration effects** are disputed. Some researchers argue the 2019 shift did little to change asylum or return numbers compared with neighboring countries; the government argues otherwise. The British government's statement that Denmark cut asylum claims by more than 90 percent in a decade is the claim of a government adopting the same policy, and I treat it as such. I have stated the policy, not its measured results.

 	- **The population figures** have limits. The British census is from 2021, and Pew's European projections were made in 2017; both are now dated, and projections are scenarios, not predictions. Census religion is self-reported. Denmark's count of "parallel societies" depends on criteria the government itself sets, and its critics call the law discriminatory because it classifies residents by national origin.

 	- **The history** of the Crusades and the Islamic conquests is summarized from the standard historical record, which is contested in emphasis, though not in its main dates.

 	- **The source email** was Tom Woods's daily email of 13 November 2026, quoting a French-language post on X that I have not seen in the original.
