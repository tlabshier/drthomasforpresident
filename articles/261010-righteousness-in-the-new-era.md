# Righteousness: The R in the New ERA


## A Position Paper on the Standard Beneath Every Policy


*Dr. Thomas | October 10, 2026*


---


## Why This Paper


In 2004, I ran for the Oregon Senate under the slogan “A New ERA”: Energy, Righteousness, and Accountability. My platform today is built on the same frame. Most of what I have written since has focused on the outer two letters: abundant energy as the foundation of prosperity and accountability as the check on power. This paper is about the middle letter, because everything else rests on it.


In recent position papers, I have used a single test for every act in the public square: *Would a neighbor feel loved by this act?* I still believe in that test. But I have come to see that it carried a weight it couldn’t carry alone. Each time I used it, I had to add a qualification: “not flattered, not indulged, not necessarily pleased, because correction is not always pleasant.” That qualification is the tell. If a neighbor can be truly loved by an act he dislikes, then his feelings don’t decide whether he was loved. Something outside him decides it. That something is God’s judgment about what is right.


So I am correcting my own standard in public, as I have asked every leader to do. The center of the moral law is not the neighbor’s feeling. It is righteousness: what God judges and establishes as right. Love of neighbor is how righteousness is lived toward people, and it is never less than righteousness. This paper lays out that order, shows where it comes from, and proposes how it should govern public life.


## What Righteousness Is


Righteousness is that which God judges to be right. It is not what the majority approves, what the courts have most recently ruled, or what feels kind in the moment. Abraham asked the defining question when he pleaded for Sodom: *“Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?”*** (Genesis 18:25)**. The question assumes that right exists, that God knows it, and that He does it.


God does not consult a standard outside Himself. His nature is the standard. *“Righteousness and judgment are the habitation of thy throne”* **(Psalm 89:14)**. *“A God of truth and without iniquity, just and right is he”* **(Deuteronomy 32:4)**. When we call something good, we are saying that it agrees with who He is. When we call something evil, we say it departs from Him.


God has made His righteousness known in three ways. He wrote it into the conscience, so that even those without the written law *“shew the work of the law written in their hearts”*** (Romans 2:15)**. He gave it in the written law and the prophets. And He showed it perfectly in a life, the life of Jesus Christ.


Righteousness is not a checklist to be quoted. The Bible teaches it through commandments, stories, proverbs, and parables, so that a man who plants those words in his heart develops a sense for the pattern of Godly action and can recognize it in situations the text never names. Righteous character is learned the way any skill is: by trying, falling, being corrected, and trying again until the right response becomes habit.


I also developed another way to say what righteousness is in *Transforming America*. If God experiences His creation from the inside, as I believe He does, then every human life is an experience God is having. A righteous life is the life He enjoys living most. His commandments are not arbitrary restrictions on our happiness. They describe the shape of the fullest life, the one in which He and we find the most joy. Righteousness, then, is not opposed to joy. It is the map to real joy.


## Love of God Comes First


When Jesus was asked which commandment is greatest, He answered in order: *“Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind. This is the first and great commandment. And the second is like unto it, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets”* **(Matthew 22:37–40)**.


The first commandment is first. That order is not an accident of phrasing.


Scripture also tells us what loving God means, and it is not primarily a feeling. *“If ye love me, keep my commandments”* **(John 14:15)**. *“For this is the love of God, that we keep his commandments”* **(1 John 5:3)**. To love God is to choose His way over our own, His law over the pull of the flesh. That choice is the purpose for which we were made. God could have made beings who obeyed Him automatically. He made us able to refuse Him, because love that cannot refuse is not love. Every day, in matters large and small, a man chooses whether to please God or to please himself. When he chooses God’s way, God receives what He created us to give: freely chosen love.


When pleasing God and pleasing men conflict, God comes first. Peter and the apostles told the council, *“We ought to obey God rather than men”* **(Acts 5:29)**. Paul wrote, *“If I yet pleased men, I should not be the servant of Christ”* **(Galatians 1:10)**. A leader who governs by what will please the most people, rather than by what is right, has inverted the order. He may be popular, and he may even be kind, but he is not righteous, and in the end he will not have loved the people he pleased.


## Love of Neighbor Is Righteousness Toward People


The second commandment is *“like unto”* the first. It is not a different standard. It is the same righteousness applied to the people God has placed around us.


Paul makes this explicit.* “Thou shalt not commit adultery, Thou shalt not kill, Thou shalt not steal, Thou shalt not bear false witness, Thou shalt not covet; and if there be any other commandment, it is briefly comprehended in this saying, namely, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. Love worketh no ill to his neighbour: therefore love is the fulfilling of the law”* **(Romans 13:9–10)**.


Notice what this means. The adulterer, the murderer, the thief, the perjurer, and the covetous man are not merely breaking rules. Each fails to love. The merchant with two sets of weights, a heavy one for buying and a light one for selling, is an abomination to the Lord **(Deuteronomy 25:13–16; Proverbs 11:1)**, and he is also cheating his neighbor. God’s law and love of neighbor point to the same act every time, because they come from the same nature.


Paul’s great chapter on love, **1 Corinthians 13**, describes what that love looks like in conduct: it is patient and kind; it does not envy, boast, or behave rudely; it does not seek its own, is not easily provoked, and keeps no ledger of wrongs; it *“rejoiceth not in iniquity, but rejoiceth in the truth.”* Notice the last clause. Love does not celebrate wrongdoing in the name of acceptance. It rejoices in truth.


The commandment also includes the self: “as thyself.” Rightly ordered, love of self is not selfishness. It is a man seeking for himself what God seeks for him, and caring for his own body, mind, and soul as a steward of something entrusted to him. *“No man ever yet hated his own flesh; but nourisheth and cherisheth it”* **(Ephesians 5:29)**. God, neighbor, and self are not three competing loyalties. They are one righteousness, held in the right order.


## Christ Is the Calibration


Jesus gave His disciples a commandment that sharpened the old one: *“A new commandment I give unto you, That ye love one another; as I have loved you”* **(John 13:34)**. We do not have to guess what love looks like. We have seen how He loved.


He kept the order perfectly. *“I do always those things that please him,”* He said of the Father (John 8:29). In Gethsemane, facing the cross, He prayed, *“not my will, but thine, be done”* **(Luke 22:42)**. Pleasing the Father came before everything else, including His own life.


Yet this never made Him cold toward people. He knew men are flesh, that they hunger, tire, and grieve. He fed the multitude because they had been with Him for three days with nothing to eat (Mark 8:2). “He knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust” (Psalm 103:14). He also knew that men’s egos bruise easily, and He usually spoke gently.


When a rich young ruler asked how to inherit eternal life, *“Jesus beholding him loved him,”* and then told him the one hard thing he lacked, and let him walk away sorrowful **(Mark 10:21–22)**. He did not soften the truth to keep him, and He did not chase him with threats. When a woman caught in adultery was brought to Him, He refused to condemn her and refused to excuse her: *“Go, and sin no more”* **(John 8:11)**. When He faced the scribes and Pharisees, men whose hearts had hardened behind their religion, He used the harshest words in the Gospels **(Matthew 23)**, and He drove the money changers from the temple **(John 2:15–16)**.


That range is the calibration. The same Lord was gentle with the broken and severe with the hard-hearted, and in both cases He was loving and righteous at once. Scripture teaches the same balance elsewhere: *“speaking the truth in love”* **(Ephesians 4:15)**;* “a soft answer turneth away wrath”* **(Proverbs 15:1)**; restore the fallen *“in the spirit of meekness”* **(Galatians 6:1)**; *“of some have compassion, making a difference: and others save with fear, pulling them out of the fire”* **(Jude 22–23)**. Soft words are more endearing than harsh ones, and should be the first resort. Harsh words are sometimes needed for the hard heart. The question is never which tone is more comfortable for the speaker, but which will serve the hearer’s good.


## The Three Questions


From these principles I draw a test in three ordered questions. I will use it in place of the single neighbor test in my future papers.


**1. Is it righteous?** Does God establish this act, forbid it, or leave it free? This question comes first and governs the others. If God’s law forbids an act, no amount of kindness in its execution makes it good.


**2. If it is righteous, is it loving?** Among the acts God permits or requires, which one actually seeks my neighbor’s good, as Christ would seek it? This asks me to know my neighbor as he is: a man of flesh with real needs, an ego, a history, and a soul.


**3. Is it done in love?** Is the truth being spoken and the act being done with the gentleness his flesh needs, and with firmness only where his hard heart requires it?


The original neighbor test, *Would a neighbor feel loved by this act?*, now has a defined place. It belongs to the second and third questions. It asks whether a righteous act is also being carried out in a way that a man of good will, on the receiving end, could recognize as care for him. It checks how we apply righteousness. It is not the definition of righteousness.


## Why Both Are Needed


Righteousness and love, rightly understood, are one thing seen from two sides. In practice, men separate them, and each, on its own, goes wrong.


**Righteousness without love becomes cruelty in God’s name.** In *Transforming America*, I wrote about the juries in the American South that acquitted lynchers for the better part of a century. Nearly every man on them was a churchgoing Christian who would have said the Bible was his moral standard. The Inquisition’s defenders believed they were defending God’s truth. In both cases, men claimed righteousness and showed no love, and that lack of love proved they had misread righteousness. Paul is direct about this: *“Though I give my body to be burned, and have not charity, it profiteth me nothing”* **(1 Corinthians 13:3)**. Jesus said, *“By their fruits ye shall know them”* **(Matthew 7:20)**. Love is the fruit by which a claim of righteousness is tested.


**Love without righteousness becomes a destructive indulgence.** The parent who never says no does not love his child more than the parent who disciplines; he loves him less. *“Whom the Lord loveth he chasteneth”* **(Hebrews 12:6)**. Eli the priest lost his house because his sons *“made themselves vile, and he restrained them not”* **(1 Samuel 3:13)**. A compassion that refuses to name sin, or that protects the wrongdoer at the expense of his victims, is not Christ’s love. It is a counterfeit that feels kind and does harm.


So the two tests check each other. Righteousness tells us what is good. Love shows whether we have understood it. When they seem to conflict, it signals that we have misread one or the other, and we must look again.


## Righteousness in the Public Square


A fair objection arises at once. Who decides what God judges righteous? A candidate who says “righteousness first” might seem to be saying “my interpretation first,” and history is full of men who governed that way.


My answer has several parts, and I develop them further in Chapter 6 of *Transforming America*.


First, I do not claim to speak for God in every position I take, but I do try to stand for the position that I believe would please God and be His will. I take positions, give my reasons, and hold them open to a better argument. A leader who claims to be God’s infallible mouthpiece has put himself beyond correction, and that is the root of religious tyranny.


Second, Christ has already shown what righteousness looks like, and the Bible states it. Every citizen must read it and judge for himself, with the humility to be corrected by his neighbors and by the facts.


Third, where Christians disagree on doctrine, the state should be silent. Where they agree, on the moral law in the spirit of the commandments and the two great commandments on which they hang, *“Love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, soul, and mind”* **(Deuteronomy 6:5)**. Jesus called this “the first and great commandment.” *“Love thy neighbour as thyself”* **(Leviticus 19:18)**, which He said is “like unto it.” The nation with these laws at its center has a conscience. John Adams wrote that Americans won their independence on “the general principles of Christianity.” A Baptist and a Catholic can both stand under those principles. Neither should be made to stand under the other’s catechism.


Fourth, the sword has a limited job. The state punishes the sins that make victims: violence, theft, fraud, and the taking of innocent life. Most sin is the business of the sinner, his family, his church, and his God. The nation may name what falls short of righteousness without punishing it, as a father names what he hopes his children will avoid.


Fifth, here is where the neighbor test does its most important public work. In a nation where not everyone shares my faith, I owe my fellow citizens a reason they can examine. Asking whether a law would feel like love to the neighbor on the receiving end translates a righteous position into terms an unbeliever can weigh. It also lets him check me. If a policy I call righteous cannot be shown to serve the real good of the people it touches, that is evidence I have misread God’s law.


*“Righteousness exalteth a nation: but sin is a reproach to any people”* **(Proverbs 14:34)**. That is a claim about consequences that anyone can test against history, not only a claim of faith.


## The Three Questions Applied


A few brief examples show how the ordered test works in practice.


**Taking a neighbor’s property by vote.** In *Transforming America*, I argued that much of what government transfers from one citizen to another is taken without consent, and that a man who votes himself his neighbor’s property has only found a respectable way to rob him. The first question settles the matter: *“Thou shalt not steal”* does not contain an exception for majorities. The second question then opens a better road. The text commands care for the widow, the orphan, and the poor, and giving freely to them is both righteous and loving.


- **James 1:27 ***“Pure religion and undefiled before God and the Father is this, To visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction, and to keep himself unspotted from the world.”*

- **Deuteronomy 10:18** – God *“doth execute the judgment of the fatherless and widow.”*

- **Deuteronomy 24:19-21** – Farmers are commanded to leave part of the harvest for the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow.

- **Psalm 68:5** – God is described as *“a father of the fatherless, and a judge of the widows.”*

- **Isaiah 1:17** – *“Seek judgment, relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow.”*

- **Matthew 25:35-40** – Jesus teaches that caring for those in need is service to Him.


The test does not abolish compassion. It moves compassion from compulsion to charity, where it can actually be love.


**Deceiving the public.** In my paper on indoctrination, I described a fabricated story of murdered infants used to sway a nation toward war, and an industry that manufactured doubt to keep selling a product it knew was killing its customers. The first question condemns both: they are false witness. The second explains why: a man manipulated into a decision has been treated as a mechanism, not as a neighbor. The third adds a duty for those who expose such things: to tell the truth plainly, but without the same manipulation in reverse.


**Correcting a neighbor’s sin.** When a friend is going wrong, the first question tells me what is right and wrong in his conduct. The second tells me that silence is not love; warning him is advocating for his soul. The third tells me how: privately first, gently first, with firmness only if his heart is hard **(Matthew 18:15–17; Galatians 6:1)**.


## Why the R Is in the Middle


Energy, Righteousness, and Accountability were never three separate planks. The middle letter holds the other two together.


Abundant energy without righteousness becomes appetite. In *Transforming America,* I described the hunger that says, “just a little more”, the fire that never says it is enough** (Proverbs 30:16)**. More power and more wealth, in the hands of people who have not learned to say “enough,” only feed that fire faster.


Accountability without righteousness becomes power checking power. We hire the sheriff to stop the robber, and the sheriff learns to take a cut. We appoint a judge to watch the sheriff, and the judge can be bought. Every new guard is a man, and every man has the same heart as the one he guards. Accountability works only when a standard of right stands above all the guards, and men fear the One who set it.


Righteousness is the internal governor that makes abundance safe and accountability real. That is why the founders said our form of government suited only a moral and religious people. A nation does not become righteous by law. It becomes righteous one heart at a time, as men choose God’s way over their own. A government can refuse to stand in the way, tell the truth about what is good, punish the wrongs that harm victims, and support the families, churches, and neighbors who do the real work of forming character.


## Conclusion


The prophet Micah summarized the whole matter in one sentence: *“He hath shewed thee, O man, what is good; and what doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?”* **(Micah 6:8)**. Justice, mercy, and humility before God: righteousness, love, and the right order between them.


I offer this standard as the foundation of my platform, and I invite every reader to test it and to test me by it. Is what I propose righteous? If it is, is it loving? And am I proposing it in love? Where I fail any of the three, I want to be told.
