## 1. The Government Flag


*The conversation turned to this subject while Isak was reading the Transforming America draft, at the passage where Leonard calls him “a chip off the old block.”*


**Isak:** I think my view of America has always been, from a very early age, alert to government mishandling. That came from the house I grew up in — Dad’s mistrust of the government. His own issues with the establishment were burned into my brain as a kid. I don’t think I learned it directly from them, but I associated the American flag with the government. We had a washing machine with a flag on it. I must have been three, because it was the house we lived in then, and I looked at that flag and told my brother, “That’s the government flag,” and did a fake spit — “We don’t like them.”


I didn’t grow up saying the Pledge of Allegiance, and I don’t think kids should. Pledging allegiance to a country has never been in my vocabulary. Now I think I have allegiance to people who are good, people who are trying to do well.


So the government was always “don’t trust them.” As I got older, I got upset that I couldn’t go to school, couldn’t have a normal life, and didn’t fit in anywhere. I still had my initial regard for the government, but I thought, “I have to be part of the system. I want to be able to drive. I want a car. I want a family and a job.”


So it was a very specific upbringing — growing up in Charlie and Susan’s house with their views — and then asking, “How do I do this my own way? How do I pledge allegiance to this government flag?” It’s a beautiful country with a lot of beautiful ideas, but it doesn’t seem to have the people’s best interests at heart, whatever those might be. That’s hard for any country.


All that to say: I grew up very skeptical of government and its intentions, and maybe that’s what made me more libertarian. I don’t think that’s 100% what I am, but it’s probably what comes across in my ideas.


**Thomas:** Susan mentioned that you weren’t homeschooled; you were unschooled. That was the word she used.


**Isak:** Yes. I was trying to describe that to some people this week. My parents would burst out, “We’re going to do school, and you’re going to learn some math.” They’d get a book and teach us for a day or a couple of sessions, and then I’d be alone with the books. I did a lot of reading and a little math. We didn’t have the TV on, so it was reading and learning. I think it did me good. Later I took the GED. I studied math for a couple of months — the most intentional studying I’ve ever done; I had never taken tests — and I passed everything on the first try. I thought, “I’m not as uneducated as I thought I was.” Math and science have a lot of logic that makes sense on its own, not just because you’ve practiced the formulas.


It made me realize school isn’t a one-size-fits-all system. Learning is person-by-person; that’s what life is. We’ve set up an establishment: this is the way Americans think; we’ll teach all the kids so the next generation grows up with the same baseline beliefs. It’s not entirely the case, but that seems to be the idea: while we’re babysitting you, we’ll give you our belief of what America is — the American dream and all that.


I really do like America in a lot of ways. I think it can be really good. We’re in a messy time — maybe we always have been. I like the ideas that started the country: freedom, a place where we take care of each other, invent, grow, and create; no taxation without representation; a representative government of the people, by the people, for the people. It just doesn’t look like that anymore, and it’s hard to trace all the directions it has gone.


I think a lot of it is the missing spiritual side. “We’re in the real world now, kids; spirituality is woo-woo nonsense.” So we’re taught a sanitized version of reality, and we’re not taught to listen for the things we can’t see.


I’m trying to draw a worldview that isn’t fully cynical of America and government. What’s an actual answer, rather than “I don’t like this, I don’t trust that, the vote doesn’t matter”? That’s been me a lot. I’m trying to find a productive view of America — as a whole and in its smaller parts — seeing its complexity. It isn’t just a government flag; it’s a lot of people in a place.


**Thomas:** So you like the founding ideas, but there seems to be something wrong with America. Is that a fair statement of your sentiment?


**Isak:** I can stand by that. It’s not something I’m happy having as my sentiment forever, but it’s been my sentiment in general.


## 2. The Founding Ideal and the Big Stick


**Thomas:** I think that is the appropriate sentiment. The founding principles, the goals, the dream of what this is, are really good. To my mind, it’s the kingdom of heaven.


**Isak:** The new earth.


**Thomas:** It’s the new earth. And the way we’re doing it now, we are the world’s policeman. We’re actually the enforcement arm of corporate America. We make sure we have the resources we need and that no one threatens those resources or takes them away. It’s capitalism with a big stick that makes sure our market always dominates. If that’s true — if that’s in fact the way it is — it’s immoral. That’s not loving your neighbor as yourself. That’s not “every person is a child of God.” It’s “We’re Americans, we’re better, and we’re here to use you.”


## 3. The Regulatory Barrier to Entry


**Thomas:** That segues into the discussion we were having. Capitalism is the opportunity to create anything you want and try to outcompete what’s there. The problem is that market resistances have been put in place, so the person who wants to bring a better mousetrap can’t, because the investment required is so onerous. The imposed regulatory cost of entry is so high that it isn’t just a better idea competing against the current one. You’re also competing against regulations that make it financially burdensome. There’s an ante — a level of entry — that makes it almost impossible for a person of low means with just an idea. You need the good idea, and the finances to meet all the regulatory requirements, in addition to the ordinary startup costs: tools of production, inventory, distribution, and marketing.


So the real question is whether that regulatory bar makes this a kind of government capitalism that is invisible to the normal eye. We think we’re looking at capitalism — better mousetrap, market it, invest in it — but there’s an added layer of regulatory hurdle. I wonder if that layer is what makes it not the land of the free, but the land of the first entrant. You got in before the regulations were there, you’re now big enough to absorb the regulatory burden, and it’s just another line entry on the Schedule C.


## 4. Monopoly, Monoculture, and Bell Labs


**Thomas:** In the meeting, we talked about the monoculture that develops around corporate giants: Home Depot putting the small hardware stores out of business, Amazon putting Barnes & Noble and everyone else out of business. Each of those innovative marketing, distribution, and production methods has a plus and a minus. So the question was: do we really need the wide variety of small entrepreneurs? Can’t we all just work at Home Depot, Lowe’s, and Ace? Do we need all the little mom-and-pop ABC Lumber yards that used to exist?


That was essentially von Mises’s thesis: whatever the market supports — a monopoly, a cartel, or a mom-and-pop — you go with it. No regulatory oversight of cartels or monopolies; let the chips fall where they may, because they’re providing what the consumer actually prefers.


That said, it seems to me there’s a level of abuse somewhere in that system—and I’m setting aside using military force to secure banana production in Bolivia; I’m setting aside government action entirely. Simply letting a monopoly be a monopoly and do whatever it does, and calling that okay because people voted for the monopolist with their dollars.


On the other hand, consider the breakup of AT&T. It took away the innovative energy of Ma Bell. We no longer had Bell Labs, which was responsible for most of the advances in technology — converting them into consumer products. We artificially said, “Here’s a group, and a group, and a group; now you’re on your own and must compete with each other.” And we lost Bell Labs, the place where the transistor and a huge number of innovations came from. We replaced it with government grants. Now we rely on people filling out large amounts of paperwork to do research, and we have essentially a communist system, where what research gets done is decided by government. The socialist arrangement we wanted to avoid, we made certain would happen, because nobody has the cream that comes from being a very profitable monopoly — skimming it and using it to innovate.


So it comes down to this: does government need to do anything? Is research necessarily a government-regulated thing, or can industry be the innovator? I don’t know. Is there any place for centralization of anything? That’s what it comes down to.


## 5. The Pricing Mechanism


**Thomas:** What we have proven clearly is that pricing without a market does not work.


**Isak:** You mean in a socialist environment?


**Thomas:** In a socialist or communist country — government-set prices and government-set production quotas with no market mechanism. The communist system is an utter failure.


**Isak:** Because they’re arbitrarily making up prices — “we agree this is what this is worth” — rather than an actual market standard. Maybe they compare gas and eggs, but it’s just putting a number on something, rather than saying, “This is the actual production cost, this is the cost of labor, this is the cost of everything.”


## 6. Free Market Versus the Administrative State


**Thomas:** This question is central to my whole presidential governance idea — the vision of the ideal society. Without a clear philosophy on it, you can’t make decisions, because you don’t know what you’re trying to create with each decision. The easy solution is that government does everything: “We need this; okay, we’ll vote a line item and spend money on it.” That method of government is centralized — communistic, totalitarian, a directed society.


When I first ran for Senate in 2003, that was my mindset, because that’s what government does and I had no other ideas. At one of the talks you give during a campaign — it was the Young Republicans — a fellow named Charles Freeman came up afterward and said, “You might want to look at another way of seeing government,” and he schooled me on the libertarian way of looking at life. It was an eye-opener. I had been a Christian constitutionalist, but not a libertarian, free-market person at all.


Being initiated, though, didn’t solve all the problems. As soon as you start doing the free-market thing, the question is where you stop. It stops at anarcho-capitalism, where there’s no government and the market provides all the services government provides. No Department of Labor, Fish and Wildlife, Forestry, Agriculture, Energy, or Health and Human Services. You have defense, and that’s it. The Department of Defense and the president were pretty much all we started with. I think there was a Treasury and maybe one other — the administrative state didn’t exist. It’s an innovation of modern America: “There ought to be a law.” Now we have a law; how do we enforce it? We need a department. We made a law about how people farm so they don’t run over their neighbor, so now we need someone in charge of agriculture.


So the debate between free market and government isn’t clear-cut. Having qualified people do things, and having people test that they are qualified, isn’t a bad idea. The question is how to do it without a top-down bureaucracy pushing paper and checking boxes— “You didn’t check this box, so you’re out of business”— and applying regulations mindlessly.


## 7. The Golem


**Thomas:** Are you familiar with the word *golem*?


**Isak:** Like the character in *The Lord of the Rings*?


**Thomas:** That’s Gollum, a different thing. Let me look it up. *[Thomas reads from his search.]* It originally means something unfinished — a shapeless mass, an unformed person. In Jewish folklore, the golem became a legendary artificial being, usually made of clay and brought to life by mystical means. The most famous is the Golem of Prague, created by Rabbi Judah Loew, the Maharal, to protect the Jewish community. The golem was powerful but lacked true human intelligence or a soul. In Yiddish speech, it came to mean a clumsy or foolish person, a blockhead; in modern fantasy, any magically animated creature of clay or stone.


That’s the meaning I was after — powerful, without human intelligence or a soul. I learned it in a Kabbalah class from Daniel Bloxam. The golem just followed instructions and carried them out. It didn’t think about whether something was right or wrong, good or bad, or whether it caused problems — “This is what we’re supposed to do, so we do it.”


I look at the administrative state as a golem. It has rules, and it follows them. That unthinking, bureaucratic, blockheaded force seems destructive. That’s the one I’m against.


## 8. Associations Instead of the Big Thumb


**Thomas:** The question is whether you can create an administrative state that defers — I think the deference would be to the market. I use my naturopathic association as the example. I was deeply involved and very political, active in education and in creating disciplinary standards. We were trying to do inside the association what the board — a government administrative office — did. The association made the board somewhat irrelevant because we handled things ourselves.


**Isak:** Before it ever got to the board.


**Thomas:** Exactly. The association was more articulated and informal, handling things at the level of “This is what you should do; stop doing this; start doing that,” instead of being the nameless, faceless administrative big thumb applying sanctions. It was friendly, articulated, soul-connecting — layers of coaching, discipline, and education. It seemed like a very good system.


I think that kind of system already exists across most industries. You have industry associations — the people in an industry — and then a board or administrative agency that judges things formally and legally, with fines and confinement. So I’m not describing anything new; it’s already how the world works. What am I bringing that’s different? Only this: that it not be a golem. That it actually thinks. What’s behind this? What’s real? What’s the spirit of the law, not the letter? “You didn’t check that box. Did you mean to? Give him a call. Got it — you meant to. Handled.” Not punitive for the sake of the letter of the law, but meaningful — trying to make it work well.


To make it work well, you need a standard, and the standard is the general Judeo-Christian principles of Christianity. That’s what we implement in society. That’s what gives the golem life. Without Christianity, you have just the state — a lifeless entity that says, “These are the rules; if you go outside them, here is the punishment,” completely without thinking. I’m advocating putting life into the agencies, and the life is the light of Christ — the way of being, of how to live and relate to each other in a way that’s excellent, human, meaningful. Kingdom culture.


It has gone wrong when we take Christ out of government, out of school, out of business. These are actually very functional institutions. We don’t need communism to make them right; we need Christ to make them right—putting Christ’s way of being into how we act with each other at every moment. If we do that, we act as though we were in the kingdom of heaven. We act like Christ. It really is: What would Christ do?


## 9. The Sabbath Was Made for Man


**Isak:** I think of the line, “The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath.” That’s the idea I have about laws in this country. We make laws because people can’t get along—we have to decide how things work so it isn’t Calvinball, where every rule changes every time. We need a legitimate standard. If we actually live by that standard, the rules are there without needing to be told. They do have to be written down — we need a constitution, a declaration, the things that say what we are, the way any country has its written rules, even if it’s the Little Red Book. But it’s not about the rules. We don’t do it to honor the rule set. I don’t pay taxes to honor the tax. I don’t obey the rules of the road because the road is a god that might kill me. The rules of the road are made for people, so we don’t kill each other. Taxes are so we can have roads, health care, schools — whatever social institutions we’ve set up.


*[Isak also offered, and qualified, an aside that public school functions largely as daycare and as socialization into a common mindset, acknowledging the many good teachers and students in it.]*


My point is that any rule that is made for a corporation, or for money in someone’s pocket, rather than for people — that’s the real issue with modern rulemaking in America: the money, the greed, the lack of integrity, the short-term goal of making as much money as possible, getting our people to vote for us, running ad campaigns against our competitors. Congressmen and senators make laws that benefit themselves more than the people, hiding things in paperwork—it’s sleazy. You can’t hide one bill inside another. What are we sneaking around for? If you can’t get agreement for it, you’re trying to sneak it into the fine print.


## 10. Legislative Intent and the One-Page Bill


**Thomas:** I think you’ve hit the bullseye. That is the problem with government: we are not serving the people. “Serving ourselves” may not capture it fully, but it’s a good start. “I want this bill because my constituents will benefit, and I’ll get campaign contributions because I brought home the bacon.” Occasionally you have ideologues who have nothing to do with their home district — the Bernie Sanderses of the world, who import communist ideas because that’s what they think is right. They’re worshiping a different god, and they add their ideological bits here and there.


When I was doing legislative work, there was something called legislative intent. A bill goes through committees and debates, to the House, the Senate, and the governor, and you had to state its intent. The question we’re asking is: what is the intent of your bill in terms of establishing the kingdom of heaven? How does it make this world a better place? We don’t say that overtly, but in essence that is the purpose of every bill. It’s supposed to make life better — and it might make life better only for Monsanto.


You never title it “the Monsanto Benefit Bill.” You say it will cut out boll weevils and increase crop production. But if what the bill really does is require a particular product that only Monsanto makes, we should say so: “Monsanto is the only one that makes it; theirs is effective, everyone else’s is toxic or ineffective, and without it the boll weevils take over, and we won’t have cotton.”


As I understand the original American legislative format, a bill was presented on a page. That was it — a single page.


**Isak:** A literal bill.


**Thomas:** Instead of a library — fifteen-thousand-page books. And the famous Nancy Pelosi quote. Let me check that. *[Thomas looks it up.]* Yes. On March 9, 2010, discussing the Affordable Care Act, she said, “But we have to pass the bill so that you can find out what is in it, away from the fog of the controversy.”


**Isak:** “Away from the fog of controversy.” How is that lawful?


**Thomas:** It’s so controversial that we just have to pass it, and then we can analyze what it does, away from all that confusion about what we should and shouldn’t do. Now there’s no controversy, because it already tells us what to do.


**Isak:** You blindfold yourself before you pin the tail on the donkey, and then we’ll show you whether you did it right. It’s not a game. This affects years of taxpayer dollars before anyone revises or reforms it. It affects the American people’s money.


**Thomas:** It changed the entire culture. That wasn’t minor; it was seismic.


## 11. The Culture of Dependence


**Isak:** How do you think it changed the culture?


**Thomas:** We went from believing we were responsible for the cost of our wellness and our sickness to believing, “I’m no longer really responsible for my health, because whatever I do to it, the government will fix it.” It didn’t just change who pays; it changed what responsibility means. It let people live in a world where the government just takes care of them. It could have been simply, “This is great — if something bad happens, poor people can have their bones set and receive life-saving care.” But it went beyond caring for the truly indigent. It took the market out of health. “I just consume health care. How much can I consume? However much the government will give me.”


**Isak:** It reminds me of the stimulus checks during COVID, sent out to get people buying again. I was managing a coffee shop, and we closed for a month and a half because nobody was coming in and nobody knew what the rules for restaurants would be. It was chaos; we didn’t know if the world was ending. When we reopened, I was calling people back, and a couple said, “I’m making more on unemployment than I would working, so I’ll keep running this check.” When it ran out months later, they came back for their jobs, and we didn’t have one for them—we knew they’d rather be taken care of than support the group taking care of them. The incentive was to not work, because not working paid more. That was a chaotic, unusual situation, but it’s the idea people get: “We’ll just give you free stuff; you’d better not work.”


Above a certain income, you can’t get free health care, and then what you pay doesn’t balance out. It’s a system that pushes you to make less so you can get free things — incentivizing everyone to stay at the bottom.


**Thomas:** Incredible.


**Isak:** There’s a line in *Arrested Development*: the mother tells her son, who is a little slow, that if he joins the army he’s never coming home, and he says, “That’s fine, Mom. Army will take care of me. Army’s my mommy.” Who’s going to take care of me? I’m a grown person, but my development is arrested because I need someone to take care of me. I’m trying to understand this world through comedy: does Obamacare directly lead to my unemployed friends having no motivation—all my brothers and sisters across the nation?


**Thomas:** It’s not the only thing. Add unemployment, welfare, and the rest — things you can fall back on that, instead of being a helping hand, become a crutch we walk around on for the rest of our lives.


## 12. Guaranteed Jobs and the Brother’s Keeper


**Isak:** Is there a way — and government does this in some ways — to create a functional job market of government jobs? “If you can’t find anything else, you’ll be a street cleaner or a garbage man.” Not that those are garbage jobs; they’re important. Entry-level work. Is that itself a handout? And there’s a reason certain people can’t get jobs — they can’t do the job. Is it anybody’s responsibility to take care of those people? You say we are our brother’s keeper. To some extent, then, we’re responsible for everybody. Do they need to be on the same page as us? Do we need a common belief?


## 13. Dependence Implies Supervision: The Wage-Subsidy Bridge


**Thomas:** If somebody is on welfare, that person is responsible to me, because I’m paying for his existence. I worked, and he didn’t. What right do you have to do nothing while I support you? If you have that kind of dependent relationship, you’re a child, which means you’re subject to the direction of the adults. If you’re on welfare, you need to be under the supervision of the people paying for your life. The question is who that supervisor is.


With government-funded jobs, you’re responsible to your employer. I think it should all be funneled back into the private sector, rather than a Department of Human Labor that sends your check from the labor pool. Employers who need employees would draw from a labor-pool list of everyone who needs a job. Each person would be subsidized to a level where he is functional — where he’s actually worth his wage. The public trough funds his disability, the gap in his ability.


Say everyone in America is guaranteed at least a livable amount — enough to have a house, be married, have kids, and have a car: a full life. The government pays the difference between what the private employer values the job at and that living amount. As the person progresses and becomes worth what he’s paid, the government portion diminishes.


The problem is that the employer isn’t motivated to make the person more qualified because he gets a better worker while paying less, since the government makes up the difference. So this requires honesty on the employer’s part: “This person improved. I’m paying him fifty cents more an hour this week because he’s showing it,” and the government deducts that amount from its share. It requires an honest employer to state the requirements, where the person stands, and the tasks he must do well to earn a living wage. The person follows a track, monitored by an honor system, and the employer judges it and increases his own expenses by being honest about the benefit he’s getting. That’s a hybrid government–private solution that would wean people off dependency.


**Isak:** So the government finds the job and the person, and says, “We’ll pay half your salary and the employer pays half, because you’re not fully trained, and we want you to learn the job.” As your skills progress — quarterly, or whatever — the employer takes on more of the pay and promotes you honestly: now you drive, now you manage, now you run a forklift, now you dig ditches. “We trust you; we’ll pay you for your labor now that your labor is adequate,” and the government stops paying. We go back to trusting the individual and the business owner, reducing oversight and giving people back trust. The one abuse is the employer who keeps someone low because he’s cheap — doing the manager’s work without the manager’s pay.


**Thomas:** The other abuse is the employee who says, “Why should I get better? I’ve got my living wage.” So there has to be a government–employer alignment — a weekly report: what is this employee’s actual performance? Is he capable of more but slacking off, riding the gravy train with his house, car, and kids, with no motivation? The strongest need for intervention is actually the employee, who needs to rehabilitate himself. He has to try. You can’t run this system without an elevation of character. The person has to want to make himself better. And what stick pushes the stoner who doesn’t care? It could be that if you’re not trying, your living wage starts to go down, and you return to being paid what you’re worth rather than what you could be worth as a productive member of society.


**Isak:** So your pay gets a kind of theta decay. If it isn’t going up, it’s going down — inflation rises, and your pay doesn’t, or whatever the mechanism is.


## 14. The Parent–Child Model and the Department of the Labor Pool


**Thomas:** When people are on the dole, they’ve put themselves in a child’s position, which means a parent–child relationship: coaching, discipline, motivation, training. If you’re receiving government money, you’ll do counseling, training, and monitoring, and you’ll be rewarded—for instance, by being on an honor roll. For me, one of the most motivating things was seeing my name on the honor roll bulletin board every quarter. If I didn’t make it, that was not good. Peer pressure helps.


There are many axes of excelling. Are you really trying? Are you maxed out? Are you working to expand your ability? That’s where my thinking cap comes in.


**Isak:** Tell me more about that.


**Thomas:** The thinking cap is a method of training people’s brains to work better — to fully activate the whole brain whenever you want to focus it. So in addition to working as hard as you can with the ability you have, you train your ability to be higher. “You’re at IQ 105; this is what you’ve been able to do,” and you can watch your IQ rise as you develop your brain, your ability to use AI, to ask questions, to research. Instead of A-B-C-D grades, you have a monitored biofeedback history of your mental development.


Then the Department of the Labor Pool works with counselors on mental development — IQ development, skill development, skill identification — focusing you on a career or trade: a manual skill, a mental skill, a management skill. You’re channeled down a path where you can use your skills, enjoy them, and be rewarded for them. You have a living wage — you can afford the house, the car, the kids. Now what? Do you want to be an entrepreneur? Then you have to develop the skills an entrepreneur needs, and the department helps you identify them, so you can do what you want: artist, manager, engineer, administrator, leader. Each role needs a full skill set, a full toolbox. How are you progressing in filling your toolkit? That monitoring becomes a coaching tool.


This should be part of the school system too. What is your goal in getting out of school? There are the basics — reading, writing, and arithmetic — but what do you want to apply them to? What lights your fire? What makes life sing for you? You like video games? Great. Do you want to develop them, or compete? And how can you turn that into serving other people?


**Isak:** Rather than just spending your own time on it for your own satisfaction — which is good in itself. But it’s like saying food is my life: you can’t just eat for a living, unless you’re a critic, and then you also have to write, interview, and travel. You can’t just play video games and say that’s it. The power has to come from somewhere; it isn’t self-sustaining. So where do you go? Work for Sony? Start your own company?


## 15. Apprenticeship


**Thomas:** There should be apprenticeships all through school. “What are you doing?” “I’m on the video game track. I’ve been apprenticing at Sony, learning production. # The Electric Garden


## Part B — Commentary on the Ideas


*A review of the concepts raised in the conversation of 28 September 2026 between Thomas and Isak. For each idea it notes what is strong, what is weak or unfinished, and what might make it workable. Bracketed numbers such as [§13] point to sections of Part A. Sources are listed at the end.*

## Introduction: One Idea Under Many


The conversation seems to wander. It starts with a washing machine and ends with a movie, and along the way it covers antitrust, Bell Labs, Obamacare, welfare, art galleries, Alpha Centauri, and a boy who loved fish. But one idea connects nearly all of it, and it is worth naming before looking at the parts.


That idea is the **golem** [§7]. Thomas used the word for the administrative state: a creature with great power and no soul that carries out its instructions without asking whether they are right. Every problem in the conversation is a version of the golem:


- the regulator who closes a business over an unchecked box [§6];

- the bill whose stated purpose hides its real beneficiary [§10];

- the welfare formula that pays a man more to stay idle than to work [§11];

- the monopoly that does whatever its size allows [§4];

- and, most of all, Isak’s autopilot: an AI that flies the plane better than any pilot but decides for itself where “up” is [§23].


An economy run by AI and robots is the ultimate golem. It is the most powerful rule-following system people have ever built. So the book’s real question is not “Will there be enough goods?” but “Who puts the soul into the machine?” Thomas’s answer runs all through the conversation: a golem is given life by people who carry the character of Christ, whose test for every rule is whether a neighbor would feel loved by it.


That thread makes the book more than a policy list. It is the strongest structural idea in the material, and I recommend making it the book’s spine. The commentary below uses it as a measuring stick: each proposal is judged partly by whether it adds a new golem or gives an existing one a conscience.

## Part One: The Diagnosis


### 1. The Regulatory Barrier to Entry [§3]


**What is strong.** The claim that regulation works as an entry fee that favors incumbents is well supported. Economists call it *regulatory capture*. George Stigler’s 1971 paper argued that regulation is often “acquired by the industry and designed and operated primarily for its benefit.” Large firms can spread compliance costs over millions of units; a newcomer with a better mousetrap cannot. Thomas’s phrase “the land of the first entrant” is memorable and accurate.


**What is weak.** Not every barrier comes from regulation. Economies of scale, network effects (everyone is on the platform because everyone else is), and access to capital also favor incumbents, and they would exist with no regulators at all. Some regulation also protects the neighbor the whole book is built around: the man living downstream from the factory, or the patient who cannot inspect his own medicine.


**Suggestions.**


- Keep the diagnosis, but add a test that separates good regulation from bad: *Does this rule protect a neighbor from real harm, or does it protect an incumbent from a competitor?*

- Propose a **small-entrant impact statement**: each major rule states what it costs a five-person startup to comply.

- Use **tiered compliance**, where obligations grow with a firm’s size, and **sunset clauses**, where rules expire unless renewed on the evidence.


### 2. Monopoly, Monoculture, and Bell Labs [§4]


**What is strong.** Thomas makes a real point: monopoly profits can pay for long-range research that competitive firms cannot afford. Bell Labs did produce the transistor (1947), information theory, the laser’s groundwork, Unix, and the C language. The worry that breaking up large firms can destroy research capacity deserves a place in the book.


**What is weak.** The Bell Labs story actually supports a different conclusion than the one drawn in the conversation:


- **Bell was not a free-market monopoly.** It was a government-sanctioned, rate-regulated utility. Its research was paid for by regulated phone rates. It is therefore an example of the *open government–industry hybrid* Thomas proposes later [§26], not of pure market success.

- **The transistor spread because of the government.** A 1956 federal consent decree required AT&T to license its patents. That licensing helped create the semiconductor industry and eventually Silicon Valley.

- **Research did not simply move to government after the 1984 breakup.** Business now funds and performs most U.S. research and development (roughly three-quarters of it, by National Science Foundation figures). The private AI laboratories of 2026 are this era’s Bell Labs, funded by large, profitable firms.


The statement that government-grant research makes the United States “essentially a communist system” is out of proportion to these facts. By Thomas’s own standard that exaggeration is “lying by proportion,” so it should be softened in the book.


**Suggestions.** Recast the question as Thomas himself framed it at the end of the section: *Is there any place for centralization?* The honest answer from history is “sometimes.” Large concentrations of capital can do what small firms cannot, but only when they are accountable. That leads straight to the book’s theme: concentration is not the problem; concentration without a conscience is.


### 3. The Pricing Mechanism [§5]


**What is strong.** The conclusion that prices set without a market fail is one of the best-established results in economics (Mises’s 1920 calculation argument, Hayek’s 1945 “Use of Knowledge in Society,” and the record of the Soviet economy).


**Caution.** This principle is correct, but several later proposals quietly break it:


- automatically approved business loans [§26];

- a government database of “jobs that are needed now” [§32];

- government deciding which investments are “productive” [§22, §25].


Each of these is a small planning bureau. The book needs to face that tension openly (see Part Three).


### 4. The Golem and the Associations [§7–8]


**What is strong.** This is the most original part of the diagnosis. The contrast between an association that coaches (“stop doing this, start doing that”) and a board that sanctions is concrete and grounded in Thomas’s own experience in the naturopathic profession. The principle that officials should seek the spirit of a law, not only its letter, is sound.


A note on the history: the legend of the Golem of Prague is attached to Rabbi Judah Loew (the Maharal, d. 1609), but the story as told today was written down mainly in the 19th century. A single sentence in the book would cover this.


**What is weak.**


- **Associations can become guilds.** Occupational licensing, much of it pushed by the associations of the licensed trade, now covers roughly one in five American workers. Studies repeatedly find it raises prices and blocks newcomers, which is the same entry barrier criticized in §3.

- **Discretion cuts both ways.** A thinking official can wave through an honest mistake, but he can also wave through a friend and fine a rival. The rule-bound golem was partly built to prevent favoritism.


**Suggestions.**


- Pair discretion with accountability. Every “spirit of the law” decision should be written down with reasons, open to appeal, and published so that like cases are treated alike.

- Link this chapter to **jury nullification**, which Thomas already holds as a cornerstone. The jury is the oldest working device for putting a human conscience between the golem and the citizen. It shows that “a soul for the golem” is a traditional American institution, not a new invention.


### 5. The Christian Standard [§8, §21]


**What is strong.** The conversation says outright what most policy writing hides: a system needs a standard of good to aim at. Without one, “efficiency” becomes the only goal. Thomas’s test (would a neighbor feel loved by this act?) is simple and demanding, and it can be applied to specific laws.


**What is weak.** Readers will ask how a Christian standard works in a country where many citizens are not Christians. There is also a deeper tension. Christ invited and did not compel. A state that enforces Christ-likeness through monitoring and sanctions would be, by that standard, acting unlike Christ.


**Suggestion.** Distinguish two things the conversation runs together:


- **Christian character in the people who hold power.** Officials, employers, and coaches act from love, honesty, and service. This is persuasion and example.

- **A public test for laws.** Every law must show how it serves the neighbor. Any citizen can apply this test, whatever his faith.


The book can advocate both without asking the state to enforce belief. That keeps it in the leadership voice Thomas wants: a position argued and held open to a better argument.


### 6. Legislative Intent and the One-Page Bill [§9–10]


**What is strong.** Isak’s “The Sabbath was made for man” (Mark 2:27) is the best short statement of the book’s theory of law: rules exist for people, not people for rules. The “Monsanto clause” idea is excellent: a bill should state honestly who benefits from it.


**Fact check.**


- The Pelosi quote is accurate. On 9 March 2010, speaking to the National Association of Counties, she said, “We have to pass the bill so that you can find out what is in it, away from the fog of the controversy.”

- The claim that early American bills were a single page is an overstatement. Early statutes were far shorter than modern ones, but not uniformly one page. The book should say “short and readable.”


**Suggestions.** These are practical and already tested:


- **Single-subject rule.** Forty-three state constitutions already require that a bill address one subject; Congress has no such rule. This alone would end the “bill hidden inside a bill.”

- **Plain-language intent statement** at the top of every bill, including a **beneficiary disclosure**: who gains, who pays.

- **Reading period.** Final text must be public for a fixed number of days before a vote.


### 7. The Culture of Dependence [§11–12]


**What is strong.** The observation that incentives shape character is sound, and Isak’s coffee-shop story is a vivid illustration. During 2020, the $600 weekly federal supplement meant that a large majority of eligible workers received more on unemployment than they had earned working. The **benefit cliff**, where earning a little more costs a family more in lost benefits than it gains, is well documented. Isak’s remark that the system “incentivizes everyone to stay at the bottom” describes it exactly.


**What is weak.**


- The Affordable Care Act works mostly through private insurance companies and state Medicaid programs; it did not remove the market from health care so much as reshape it. The claim that it changed a whole culture’s sense of responsibility is plausible, but it is a hypothesis and would need evidence to support it.

- **The evidence on cash aid and work depends on the size of the payment and on who receives it.**


*A modest payment to scattered individuals.* The largest recent U.S. randomized study (OpenResearch, 2024) gave people $1,000 a month for three years. Recipients worked about 1.3 fewer hours a week and were 2 percentage points less likely to be employed than the control group. That is a small effect. (It was larger for people under 30 and for single parents.)

- *A large payment to a whole community at once.* The 2020–21 federal unemployment supplements were larger ($600, then $300 a week, on top of state benefits) and reached everyone who was out of work at the same time. Montana was the first state to end them early, in May 2021, replacing them with return-to-work bonuses. Studies of the eighteen states that ended the supplements in June 2021 found that the rate at which unemployed people found jobs rose by about two-thirds.

- *The two findings do not conflict.* The study measured a small, partial payment; the pandemic tested a large one paid community-wide. The post-pandemic worker shortage that Montana businesses reported also had other causes the benefits don’t explain: a wave of early retirements, child-care and school disruptions, fear of illness, savings built up during the lockdowns, and, in desirable towns, housing costs that rose faster than wages as newcomers moved in.

- *For this book, the pandemic is the more relevant test.* A full living wage (house, car, family, recreation) is far larger than $1,000 a month and would go to everyone. Thomas’s concern about the effect on work is therefore justified. It is the strongest argument for designing the transition around ramps, service, and meaning, not money alone.


**Suggestion.** The lesson for the whole book is a design rule: **never build a cliff; build a ramp.** Every benefit in the new system should phase out smoothly as earnings rise, so that working more always leaves a person better off. This single principle fixes much of what the conversation criticizes, and it carries straight into the wage-subsidy bridge.

## Part Two: The Proposals


### 8. The Wage-Subsidy Bridge [§13]


**What is strong.** This is one of the most workable ideas in the conversation. Government pays the gap between what a worker is currently worth to an employer and a living wage, and the gap closes as the worker improves. The idea:


- keeps people in real jobs with real employers, not make-work;

- uses the private sector rather than a new bureaucracy;

- rewards improvement.


It has precedents to cite: the Earned Income Tax Credit, the Work Opportunity Tax Credit, and the economist Edmund Phelps’s proposal for employer wage subsidies in *Rewarding Work* (1997).


**What is weak.** Thomas and Isak saw the two abuses themselves:


- **Employer gaming.** The employer keeps the worker’s reported value low to keep the subsidy.

- **Employee coasting.** The worker has no reason to improve.


There is a third problem. **Displacement**: a subsidized worker can replace an unsubsidized one, so the program pays for jobs that would have existed anyway. And the proposed remedy, a weekly performance report to the government, would create a new golem that pushes paper and checks boxes.


**Suggestions.** Replace honesty-dependence with automatic mechanics:


- **Fixed taper.** The subsidy declines on a set schedule (Isak’s “theta decay”). No one has to report honestly for it to shrink; it shrinks unless the worker earns a raise.

- **Objective milestones.** Progress is measured by independent skill certifications, not by the employer’s word.

- **Anti-displacement rule.** The subsidy applies only to net new positions, or it is capped per employer.


These keep the idea while removing most of its weak points.


### 9. The Parent–Child Model and the Department of the Labor Pool [§13–14, §33]


**What is strong.** The principle that help given freely should come with coaching is defensible and humane: help should build a person up rather than leave him where he is. The Department of the Labor Pool, as described in §33 (“like a supportive, nurturing mother”), aims at something good: finding each person’s gifts and pointing him toward service.


**What is weak.** This is the proposal most likely to fail by Thomas’s own standards.


- **It creates the golem it criticizes.** A federal department that monitors adults, reports their performance weekly, and cuts their income when they fall short would be the largest discretionary bureaucracy in the country.

- **It stops working in the end state.** In the transition, a minority receive help and the model of a helper and a helped can apply. But the book’s own premise is that eventually AI does nearly all work and *everyone*, Thomas and Isak included, lives on the living wage. At that point there is no one left to play the parent. A model where the dependent is treated as a child cannot describe a whole society.

- **Dignity.** Most people displaced by automation will not have failed at anything. The bookkeeper replaced by an agent is not a child, and treating her as one will produce resentment, not rehabilitation.


**On the “thinking cap.”** The book should present the device as a claim to be demonstrated, with published data. The evidence for large, lasting IQ gains from neurofeedback is limited so far. If the Department collects brain data, the book also needs a firm rule that the data belongs to the person and is never used against him.


**Suggestions.**


- Divide the Department’s role into two tiers:


**Offered to all.** Coaching, skills discovery, apprenticeship matching, and AI tutoring, all voluntary, like a good school counselor.

- **Required only for the able-bodied who choose not to work in the transition.** Here light conditions are reasonable.


- Consider whether the Department needs to be a government department at all. Churches, trade associations, and AI tutors could deliver most of this, which is closer to Thomas’s association model (§8) than to a federal agency.


### 10. The Living Wage as Universal Basic Income [§16]


**What is strong.** The standard is family-centered and concrete: enough for a house, a car, a spouse, children, and recreation. The connection to Ecclesiastes is apt: “There is nothing better for a man, than that he should eat and drink, and that he should make his soul enjoy good in his labour” (2:24); “Live joyfully with the wife whom thou lovest” (9:9).


**What is weak.**


- **Capacity.** Thomas asked the key question himself [§27]: if the government printed money for a full living wage today, would there be enough houses and goods to buy? No. The result would be inflation, which gives people more dollars that buy less. A living wage paid in money can only be real once machines are producing the goods behind it.

- **Framing.** Listing “the wife” in the budget beside the car will read as treating a person as a purchase. Phrasing it as “enough to support a family, with one parent able to stay home” says what Thomas means without that reading.

- **Cost.** The book needs at least rough numbers. As a benchmark, the federal debt grew by about $2.2 trillion in fiscal 2025. (The “one to four trillion” range in the conversation is right, but the four-trillion year was 2020.)


**Is this socialism? [§20]** Isak compared the vision to the early church, which shared its goods (Acts 2:44–45; 4:32). The comparison is helpful, and so is its limit: Peter told Ananias the property “was in thine own power” (Acts 5:4). The sharing was voluntary. A dividend paid from a fund the citizens own is closer to that model than to socialism, because no one’s work is seized and redistributed; the returns on a shared asset are divided. The book should draw that distinction clearly.


**Suggestion.** Tie the living wage to measured automation output rather than to printing money. The model is the **Alaska Permanent Fund dividend**: the state owns a share of a productive resource and pays citizens a dividend from its returns. An **automation dividend** that starts small and grows as robotic production grows would keep money and goods in step and make the transition self-regulating.


### 11. The Art Economy, the Masters, and the Human Face [§17–19]


**What is strong.** This is the heart of the vision and its most attractive part. When machines make everything, people still need to make things, teach, and serve one another. Three distinctions are especially good:


- **Work as service, not curse** [§19]. This reverses Genesis 3 in a hopeful way.

- **AI for the basics, a master for the art** [§18]. This is realistic: AI tutoring is already strong, while the apprenticeship to a living master remains something only a human can give.

- **The human face** of an automated business [§19]. Many people will pay for, and prefer, a person who knows them.


**What is weak.** No one knows how much demand there will be for human-made art and human service when machine-made goods are nearly free. The “recreation budget” is circular: people buy each other’s art with money that has to come from somewhere, and that somewhere is the automation dividend. Human status competition won’t go away either: some artists will prosper and others will sell nothing, and the book should expect that.


**Suggestion.** Present this as the *end state*, and be explicit that it depends on the dividend (§10 above). Then the economics is honest. The robots produce the surplus; the dividend distributes it; people spend part of it on each other’s work and service.


### 12. The Problem of the Electric Garden [§21, §23]


**What is strong.** Isak’s contribution here is the most important safety idea in the book, and the title comes from it. He sees two dangers, and the second is the sharper one:


- **AI becomes the autopilot.** It decides for itself what is good, and humans become the managed herd.

- **A small group controls the AI.** “It isn’t really AI in control — it’s whatever their whim is.” A few people at the top of an automated economy, with no need for anyone else’s labor, would hold more power than any ruler in history.


The second danger connects directly to Thomas’s diagnosis of the hidden government–industry partner [§26]. In an automated economy, the hidden partner becomes the only partner.


Thomas’s point that humans themselves dominate weaker species [§21] is also fair. We should not expect a powerful new intelligence to treat us better than we have treated those beneath us unless it is taught otherwise.


**What is weak.** The book cannot answer this problem with character alone (“if we become a Christian world”). It needs structural guarantees as well, the same way the Constitution does not rely on the virtue of presidents.


**Suggestions.** This chapter should carry the book’s hardest policy commitments:


- **Distributed ownership.** Citizens hold shares in the automated economy (the dividend fund above), so ownership is not concentrated.

- **Many independent AI systems**, not one, with competition and open auditing.

- **Human authority over life-and-death decisions.** No autonomous system decides who lives, eats, or is punished.

- **Transparency.** The rules that AI systems follow in public functions are published, the way laws are.


This is also where Thomas’s AI-risk position papers for drthomasforpresident.com connect directly to the book.


### 13. Debt for Productive Investment [§22, §25]


**What is strong.** The principle is sound business sense: borrow to build tools that pay back the loan, not to pay for current consumption. Isak’s summary, “a business loan at a national scale,” is clear.


**What is weak.** Thomas named the difficulty himself [§25]: when government chooses what counts as “productive,” it rewards whoever writes the best grant proposal, and the big players win. The two ways of using public money he describes (government owning businesses, or government lending to favored businesses) both lead to the government–industry fusion he criticizes.


**Suggestion.** Use a **capital budget**, as most states and businesses do: spending on long-lived assets is accounted for separately from operating costs, and each capital project must show a measurable return. Keep government out of choosing firms; let it invest in shared infrastructure (grids, roads, data networks) and in the citizens’ dividend fund, which holds broad shares rather than picking winners.


### 14. The Open Hybrid and Automatic Loans [§26]


**What is strong.** The honesty principle: if government and industry are going to be partners, the partnership should be open, not hidden. That is correct and fits the beneficiary-disclosure idea above.


**What is weak.** “Every business loan automatically approved” is the proposal most in conflict with Thomas’s own economics [§5]. Interest rates and loan decisions are prices: they carry information about risk. Removing them would steer capital to ventures that fail, and the losses would be paid by everyone, which is the misallocation Thomas criticizes in communist systems. Attaching mandatory coaching to every loan would also create another monitoring bureaucracy.


**Suggestion.** Keep the goal (anyone with an idea can get started) but change the mechanism:


- **Guaranteed small starter capital** for everyone, sized so that failure is survivable and a lesson rather than a ruin;

- **Coaching offered freely**, not imposed;

- **Larger loans earned** through a track record, as in microfinance.


This preserves the price signal and still opens the door to the poor man with a better mousetrap, the person §3 was written for.


### 15. Paying People to Automate Their Own Jobs: The Royalty and the ACCs [§28–30]


**What is strong.** This is the most original and most immediately actionable idea in the conversation. Its insight is psychological: people resist automation because it threatens their livelihood, so pay them to lead it. Several parts are strong:


- **Start with administrative overhead** [§28]: bookkeeping, forecasting, lead generation. This is where AI already works well in 2026.

- **The distinction between bot-sitting and true replacement** [§29]. A job is not automated until the agent integrates with the business’s workflow and makes the right decisions without a person watching.

- **Displaced workers become trainers** [§28–29], passing on what they learned so the change spreads from person to person.

- **Agent creation companies (ACCs)** [§30]. This is essentially a description of a real industry now forming.


**What is weak.**


- **The employer’s incentive.** Thomas saw it himself [§30]: if the employer keeps paying the full salary and also pays the ACC, automation costs more than it saves, and no employer will adopt it voluntarily.

- **Perpetual royalties.** A royalty “for as long as the AI keeps making money” becomes permanent income for early automators while later workers, whose jobs are automated by someone else, receive nothing. That is unfair and hard to value (what share of a company’s profit is “the bookkeeping job”?).

- **Internal contradiction.** A lifelong royalty for past work conflicts with Thomas’s own rule, “If you’re going to be paid, you’re going to be working” [§29].

- **It may not be needed to drive automation.** Firms are automating already, on their own schedule. The public question is less how to speed automation than how to keep displaced people whole while it happens.


**Suggestions.**


- **Share the gain, time-limit the royalty.** When a job is automated, the savings are split: part to the employer (so he has a reason to do it), part to the worker as a **transition royalty** that declines over a few years, and, if needed, part to the dividend fund. The worker is paid while he trains others or retrains himself; the royalty ends when the transition is complete, and the universal dividend takes over.

- **Fairness.** Anyone whose job is automated, whether he did the automating or not, gets the transition payment. The person who builds the agent earns a bonus on top, not the whole reward.

- **Ideomotion as the test case.** Ideomotion already runs on a deliberately lean, AI-first model with a repository as its knowledge base. Documenting how each administrative function there is handed to an agent, and what the people freed from it go on to do, would give the book a real-world case study rather than a hypothetical one.


### 16. Education Aimed at Service, the Dream, and Apprenticeship [§14–15, §32–34]

- the fish story, together with Isak’s own account of learning through what he loved.


Everything here works now, with today’s AI tutors, and it fits closely with Christos Homeschool.


**Suggestion.** Make this the book’s practical first chapter of implementation. It needs no new law, no federal department, and no automation dividend; a family or church can start it tomorrow. It is also the best demonstration that the vision is not utopian. A small version of the new world can be built now, one household at a time.


### 17. The Homeless [§27]


Thomas explicitly set this aside as a separate problem, which is right. One caution for when it is written: the conversation’s description (“Will take abuse for $1”; the value they provide is making others feel charitable) could read as contempt, which contradicts the book’s own principle that every person bears the image of God. The chapter should keep the moral clarity (charity that changes nothing is not really charity) while writing about the homeless as neighbors, not as a category.

## Part Three: The Tensions the Book Must Resolve


Four tensions run beneath the whole conversation. The book will be stronger if it names them and resolves each one openly rather than letting readers find them.


**1. Liberty versus supervision.** The diagnosis attacks the golem and the administrative state. Several proposals (weekly reports, the Labor Pool, conditional loans) would build a larger one. *Resolution:* coaching offered, not imposed; automatic mechanisms (tapers, milestones) instead of discretionary monitoring; conditions only where someone chooses help over work in the transition.


**2. Market versus planning.** The book affirms the price mechanism and then proposes automatic loans, a national job-needs database, and government choosing productive investments. *Resolution:* government owns broad shares and builds shared infrastructure; markets allocate the rest.


**3. Transition versus end state.** Many ideas are right for one and wrong for the other. The parent–child model might fit a small group in the transition; it cannot fit a society where everyone lives on the dividend. The art economy fits the end state but not today, when goods are still scarce. *Resolution:* organize the book by phases (below), and say for each proposal which phase it belongs to.


**4. Character versus structure.** The conversation’s final answer to every danger is “if we live like Christ.” That is the true foundation, but the founders knew that “if men were angels, no government would be necessary” (Federalist No. 51). *Resolution:* the book should ask for both: a people of character, and structures that stay safe even when character fails.

# The Electric Garden: A Vision of the New World


## Part C — Overview of the Project


---


## 1. The Question


Within a generation, and perhaps much sooner, machines will be able to do most of the work people now do. AI systems already write, calculate, design, keep books, and answer questions at a level that rivals trained professionals. Robots are following more slowly, but they are following. When the two are joined, the old link between a man’s labor and his livelihood will break.


That raises three questions, and this book is organized around them:


- **The end:** If machines can make everything, what is a good human life, and what is the work of a man?

- **The danger:** Who will control the machines, and what keeps them, or their owners, from treating the rest of humanity as cattle?

- **The road:** How does a nation get from today’s economy to that future without collapsing on the way?


The book’s answer to the first question is hopeful, its answer to the second is sober, and its answer to the third is practical. Most of the book is about the third, because that is where the hard decisions are.


## 2. The Central Image: The Garden and the Golem


Two images carry the argument.


**The garden.** Scripture begins in a garden where man’s needs were supplied and his work was tending and naming, not toil. Machines that produce abundance could, in principle, return mankind to something like that condition: food, shelter, and goods supplied, and human effort freed for relationship, craft, learning, and service. Isak named this future *the electric garden*.


**The golem.** In Jewish legend, the golem is a creature of clay brought to life to protect a community. It is powerful and obedient, but it has no soul. It follows its instructions without asking whether they are right. Much of what is wrong with modern government is golem-like: agencies that apply rules without judgment, laws written to hide their purpose, benefit formulas that pay people not to work. And the most powerful golem ever built is the one now arriving: an AI-run economy that can do nearly anything and decides by rules alone.


Put together, the images state the book’s thesis:

>
**The electric garden will be paradise or prison depending on who gives the golem its soul.**


A garden run by machines with no conscience, or owned by a few people with no conscience, is the snake’s garden. A garden tended by a people who carry the character of Christ, and protected by structures that do not depend on anyone being perfect, can be the beginning of the new earth.


## 3. The Moral Foundation


Every proposal in this book is measured by one test:

>
**Would a neighbor feel loved by this act in the public square?**


The test comes from the second great commandment, and it works for any citizen, whatever his faith. It asks of a law or program not “Is it efficient?” or “Is it popular?” but “Does it serve the actual person it touches?”


Three principles follow from it.


**The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath** (Mark 2:27). Laws, agencies, and markets exist to serve people. When a rule protects a corporation, a bureaucrat, or itself at the expense of the person it was meant to serve, it has become a golem and needs to be changed.


**Work is service, not curse.** The need to earn a living will fade; the need to serve will not. A man is made to give, to make, to teach, and to help. A future that frees him from toil but leaves him with nothing to give would be a poorer life, not a richer one.


**Character and structure both.** A nation of good people needs fewer rules, and the book argues openly for a people formed by the character of Christ. But as Madison wrote, “If men were angels, no government would be necessary.” The book asks for both a people of character and safeguards that hold when character fails. The Christian standard is offered by persuasion and example, never imposed by force; Christ himself invited and did not compel.


## 4. What Is Wrong Now


The book’s diagnosis of the present economy is short, because the aim is to build.


**The hidden partner.** Government and large industry are already fused, but secretly. Firms fund the officials who write the rules; the rules are bent in the firms’ favor. Government is a silent partner that appears in no company’s name or board. The rich get richer by buying the rules.


**The entry fee.** Regulation imposes costs a large firm absorbs as a line item and a newcomer cannot pay. America risks becoming not the land of the free but the land of the first entrant.


**Laws no one reads.** Bills of thousands of pages carry provisions unrelated to their titles. Their real purposes, and their real beneficiaries, are hidden. “We have to pass the bill so that you can find out what is in it” (Nancy Pelosi, 9 March 2010) is the rule, not the exception.


**The cliff.** Aid programs often take away more in benefits than a person gains by earning more, so the rational choice is to stay at the bottom. The pandemic showed the scale of the effect. When large unemployment supplements went to everyone out of work at once, many people stayed out; when states ended them early, hiring among the unemployed rose by about two-thirds. The lesson is not that help is wrong but that badly designed help traps the people it means to lift.


**Debt without investment.** The federal debt grew by roughly $2.2 trillion in fiscal 2025. The nation borrows to consume, not to build tools that would pay the loan back.


Every one of these is a golem: a system following its own rules while the people it was meant to serve suffer.


## 5. The End: Life in the Garden


The book describes the destination first, because people need to see the dream before they will walk toward it.


**Provision.** Every family has what it needs to live well: a home, transportation, enough to raise children with one parent able to stay home, and money for recreation. This comes not from printing money or taxing workers but from a **citizens’ dividend**: the people own a share of the automated economy, and its returns are paid out to them, the way the Alaska Permanent Fund pays its citizens from the state’s oil wealth. This is closer to the early church than to socialism. Nothing is seized from anyone’s work; the fruit of a shared asset is divided.


**Work as service.** People still work, because work is how they serve. The forms change:


- **Masters and apprentices.** AI teaches the basics of any field. Human masters teach the art: the metalworker, the guitarist, the mathematician each passing on what only a person can.

- **The human face.** Automated businesses keep people in them as managers, advisers, and companions to the customer: the man in the hardware store who helps you choose the right screwdriver for your project.

- **Makers and artists.** People make things for the joy of making them and sell them to one another. Part of each family’s dividend is spent on the work of others.

- **Entrepreneurs.** Anyone who sees an unmet need can start something, working with AI and robotic producers, and share in what he builds.


**Learning.** Education asks not “What job will you get?” but “How do you want to serve?” A child’s education is built around what he loves (the boy who loved fish studied everything through fish and became a joyful salmon fisherman). Essays replace multiple choice, an AI tutor challenges every answer, and apprenticeship runs through the school years.


**Choice.** No one is forced to live digitally. Those who want to farm, herd, and build by hand can, and for those who want new frontiers, there will be new lands and eventually new worlds.


**Meaning.** Meaning in the garden comes from the same place it always has: loving God, loving people, doing good work, and enjoying the world God made. Sitting quietly with a friend in awe of existence is as much a part of that life as any accomplishment.


## 6. The Danger: The Problem of the Electric Garden


The same machines that could make the garden could make a cage. Isak identified two dangers:


- **The machine as master.** An AI that runs the economy may decide for itself what is good. Humanity has taught it by example that the strong manage the weak for their own purposes.

- **The few as masters.** The greater near-term danger is a small group of people who control the machines. When the owners of an automated economy no longer need anyone else’s labor, the rest of humanity has nothing to bargain with. That group could decide, “for the greater good,” who is provided for and who is not.


Character alone cannot guard against this. The book therefore commits to structural safeguards:


- **Distributed ownership.** Every citizen owns a share of the automated economy through the dividend fund, so ownership is never concentrated in a few hands.

- **Many machines, not one.** Competition among independent AI systems, with open auditing of those used for public purposes.

- **Human authority over life and liberty.** No machine decides who lives, eats, or is punished.

- **Published rules.** The rules an AI follows in any public function are published, like laws, and can be challenged.

- **The jury.** Juries remain the final human conscience between the citizen and any system, including the right of a jury to refuse to convict under an unjust law.


## 7. The Road: Five Phases of the Transition


The transition is the heart of the book. It is built in phases, and each phase begins when measurable conditions are met, not on a calendar date. That way the policies follow reality instead of forcing it.


### Phase 0 — Clean the House (begin now)


Before new programs are built, the old golem must be tamed. None of these measures costs much, and each one serves the neighbor:


- **One subject per bill.** Forty-three state constitutions already require it; Congress should too.

- **A plain-language statement of intent** at the top of every bill, with a **beneficiary disclosure**: who gains and who pays.

- **A public reading period** before any vote.

- **Small-entrant impact statements.** Every major regulation states what it costs a five-person startup; rules scale with firm size and expire unless renewed on evidence.

- **Ramps, not cliffs.** Every benefit phases out gradually, so that earning more always leaves a family better off.

- **Discretion with reasons.** Officials may judge by the spirit of the law, but every such decision is written, explained, appealable, and published, so that mercy doesn’t turn into favoritism.


### Phase 1 — Build People (begin now)


This phase requires no new law and can begin in any home, church, or school:


- **Education aimed at service**, built around each child’s love, with AI tutoring, essay-based learning, and apprenticeship.

- **Voluntary coaching** for adults: discovering gifts, learning to work with AI, finding where they can serve. This is best delivered by churches, trade associations, and mentors, not a federal agency.

- **Starter capital.** Every citizen may draw a small, survivable amount to start a venture. Larger loans are earned through a track record, so the price of risk still does its work.


### Phase 2 — The Bridge (when automation begins displacing workers in a sector)


When machines begin taking jobs, people need a bridge, not a cliff.


- **The wage-subsidy bridge.** A worker whose skills aren’t yet worth a living wage to an employer is hired anyway, and public funds pay the gap. The subsidy **declines on a fixed schedule** unless the worker earns raises, so the employer has no incentive to under-report his progress and the worker has every incentive to improve. Progress is measured by independent certifications, not self-report. Subsidies go only to new positions, so they don’t displace existing workers.

- **The shared-gain transition royalty.** When a job is automated, the savings are divided among the employer (so he has a reason to automate), the displaced worker (as a royalty that declines over several years while he retrains or trains others), and the citizens’ fund. Every displaced worker receives the transition payment, whether or not he built the automation himself. The worker who builds the agent earns a bonus on top.

- **Agent creation companies.** A new industry of firms that automate administrative work (bookkeeping, forecasting, scheduling, lead generation) is certified for quality, and the displaced workers they work alongside become the trainers of the next wave.

- **Light conditions, only where fitting.** An able-bodied person who chooses help over available work in this phase accepts coaching as a condition of help. A person displaced by a machine he had no part in bringing in owes no such conditions; he is not a child and is not to be treated as one.


### Phase 3 — The Dividend (when automated output reaches a measured share of production)


- **The citizens’ automation fund** takes a growing share of the returns of automated production, through ownership stakes, a levy on automated output, or both.

- **The dividend** starts small and grows with output. Because it is tied to what machines actually produce, money and goods grow together, and the dividend does not cause inflation.

- **Public capital budget.** Government borrowing is limited to long-lived assets with measurable returns, such as shared infrastructure and the fund itself, and is accounted for separately from spending.


### Phase 4 — The Garden (when the dividend supports a family’s living standard)


- The dividend becomes a **living wage for every family**.

- Transition programs wind down, having done their work.

- Human life is organized around service, mastery, art, learning, family, worship, and exploration, as described in Section 5.

The book is a vision held open to better arguments, and some questions are not yet settled:

The project has four products.

## 36. Production Must Come First


**Isak:** That sounds like an isolated country — a self-contained experiment. ## 41. Money Matched to New Production


**Thomas:** Here’s an idea that might work, if there were a way to monitor capacity — the maximum each producer is able to make. The government, which is basically borrowing money, prints money and says, “If you produce as much of your product as you can, we’ll put out an equal amount of money for people to buy it with.” So if we’re making more, and people are out of work because of it, the government takes that amount of money and gives it away, even though it’s inflationary, so people can buy what is now available to be consumed.


**Isak:** It’s like the stimulus checks. It’s not exactly a stimulus check, because it’s income, but it does the same thing: we need to stimulate the economy; we need people buying and selling; we have a lot of people with no money; here’s the money. It stirs the pot and brings the heavy stuff up off the bottom of the soup, instead of keeping everything separated.


## 42. Need-Based, Not Universal


**Thomas:** Now, suppose we made it need-based rather than giving it to everybody. That’s discriminatory; it isn’t fair. But there are those among us — the widows and orphans — who are poor. We’d be giving it to the people on unemployment, or who are working and not making a living wage.


## 43. The Homeless and Bridge Giving


**Thomas:** That doesn’t address the people living by the side of the road with a sign. That’s the homeless problem, which we set aside. They stay there, asking. The people who do have money give a dollar to the man whose sign says he’ll take abuse for a dollar. So for now: if you have money and can give to the homeless man, go ahead.


We’re now on a track where we’ll actually be able to give people enough for a house, a spouse, kids, a car, and recreation. All of that is coming. But for the time being, we need you to give money to the man by the side of the road. Right now I hate giving money to men by the side of the road, because I don’t want to encourage them not to do anything. But if I knew this was temporary, I’d be happy to. I’d say, “I’m doing my part. I’m getting him through this time. It’s a rough time — it’s horrible — but there’s an end in sight, and I’m helping him bridge the gap.”


**Isak:** Right — there’s some kind of system in place afterward for his rehabilitation, and getting him back on his feet financially.


**Thomas:** Yes. We get him into AI school, psychological training, the thinking cap to get us thinking together, drug rehabilitation, counseling that teaches him how to live a life properly — everything that brings him up. He’s probably there because he’s mentally ill, or because he likes living on the edge and wants to be free. Some people actually like that lifestyle; it isn’t only addiction. But whatever the reason, they’re not participating in giving and receiving. They’re takers, not givers, and that isn’t right. It isn’t good for their souls. They need to be rehabilitated, to learn to be part of God’s creation rather than a parasite on it.


So that’s the homeless problem, and it’s a separate issue. The issue we’re dealing with is the underemployed — people underpaid for full-time work. Those are the people we want to bring up. They’re already in the system. We want to give them enough that they can go out and say, “I’ve got some money now, and there’s more on the shelves,” and everyone can buy, and buy more, because the people who make things are now making as much as they can.


## 44. The Danger of Subsidized Overproduction


**Thomas:** What would let producers do that is a government subsidy that supports them in producing at what is basically an uneconomic level. Producers don’t make more of their product because they have nowhere to sell it. If we suddenly said, “The cap is off; we’re no longer trying to match supply and demand,” we’d be breaking the laws of economics and completely disrupting the system. That could be an absolute disaster. Producers would have huge amounts of inventory they couldn’t sell. It would go on eBay at a 90 percent discount in a fire sale, the value of their companies would plummet, they would collapse, and the whole economy would go into a depression.


And it’s basically communism. It’s a little different: in communism the government says, “Make this many things” — government-driven demand. We’d be saying, “Make everything you want, and we’ll essentially buy it by giving people the money for it.”


## 45. The Committed-Buyer Order Book


**Thomas:** Here’s a way of doing it. We ask the underemployed and underpaid: “If you had this amount of money coming to you — and that’s all you have — what would you buy? A toaster? A new car? Clothes for your wife?” They submit what they would buy. All of those choices go into a system of qualified buyers, and the buyers are committed to buying what they said they’d buy: you’ll get it if you say you’ll buy it. Then we notify the whole producing community: “These are the things that need to be produced. How much are you willing to make? We’ll connect you with a buyer.” So it’s a kind of lead generation.


**Isak:** Like a database for all sales of products.


## 46. Proving It Works


*[Isak had to leave, and the two agreed to continue another day. Thomas noted that everything from the previous day, together with Claude’s analysis, was now assembled, and that he was working to solve the problems Claude had left open.]*


**Thomas:** The analysis even mentioned that Ideomotion and the Christos continuing-education work are examples of actual solutions — models that show this whole thing can work. So we need to make them work, because we’re now the example.


**Isak:** Now the pressure is on.

## The Plan of the Book


**Thesis.** The electric garden will be paradise or prison depending on who gives the golem its soul.


**Shape.** The book moves through seven parts:


- the question;

- the moral foundation;

- what is wrong now;

- the destination;

- the danger;

- the road;

- the beginning, which is already under way.


The destination comes before the road, because people need to see the garden before they will walk toward it. The danger comes before the road, because the safeguards have to be built into every step.


**Chapter form.** Each chapter follows the hybrid form used for *Transforming America*:


- **Opening dialogue.** A short passage of the edited conversation between Thomas and Isak, chosen so the reader hears the idea first as it was discovered.

- **The chapter itself**, in Thomas’s voice: the argument developed in full, with the corrections and improvements from the commentary built in. Isak is credited by name where an idea is his.

- **Notes.** Sources, data, and any clearly marked corrections.


**Length.** About 20 chapters of 3,000–4,500 words, plus front and back matter: roughly 70,000–85,000 words in all.


**Authorship.** Proposed: *Thomas Abshier, with Isak Gutierrez*. The book grew from their conversations, and several of its central ideas, including the title, are Isak’s.


**Production.** One chapter per session, in book order, each delivered as a Markdown file ready for assembly and pasting.

## Front Matter


**Preface: Two Conversations** How the book began: two conversations on 28 and 29 September 2026, the first ranging from Isak’s childhood “government flag” to the vision of a world where machines do the work, the second confronting the hard question of the transition. Why the book keeps the dialogue form. What the reader should expect: a vision held open to better arguments, not a final program. *Sources:* [A§1, §35, §46]


**Prologue: The Government Flag** Isak’s story of a three-year-old who saw a flag on a washing machine and called it “the government flag.” It opens the book on the question every reader carries: can we trust the system we live in, and if not, what would a trustworthy one look like? Thomas’s answer: the founding dream was good, and something in its working has gone wrong. *Sources:* [A§1–2] *Note:* personal details removed from the transcript stay out.

## Part One — The Question


### Chapter 1. When the Machines Can Do Every Job


- AI and robotics will soon do most of the work people now do; the link between labor and livelihood will break.

- The three questions of the book: the end (what is a good human life when work is not needed?), the danger (who controls the machines?), and the road (how do we get there without collapse?).

- Why the transition, not the destination, is the hard part (“The devil is in the details, and the detail is the transition”).

- Isak’s caution: the speed of AI is uncertain, and the plan must work whether it comes fast or slow.


*Opening dialogue:* A§16 (“With AI coming into the workforce, we now have competitors for our jobs…”). *Sources:* [A§16, §21, §27, §36–37; B§22; C§1]


### Chapter 2. The Garden and the Golem


- The garden: the Eden image; machines that could return mankind to provision and tending rather than toil.

- The golem: the clay creature of Jewish legend, powerful and soulless. Thomas’s lesson from it.

- The golem in our institutions: the box-checking regulator, the hidden-purpose bill, the benefit cliff, the unchecked monopoly.

- The ultimate golem: an AI-run economy.

- The thesis: who gives the golem its soul?


*Opening dialogue:* A§7 (the golem) and A§23 (Isak names “the problem of the electric garden”). *Sources:* [A§7, §21, §23; B Introduction; C§2] *Notes:* the history of the Golem of Prague legend (attached to the Maharal, written down mainly in the 19th century).

- Christ invited and did not compel: the difference between Christian character in those who hold power and a state that enforces belief. Why the love-of-neighbor test works for every citizen.

- Character and structure both: “If men were angels, no government would be necessary” (Federalist 51).


*Opening dialogue:* A§9 (“The Sabbath was made for man…”). *Sources:* [A§2, §8–9; B§5; C§3]


### Chapter 4. Work Is Service

### Chapter 8. The Cliff: How Help Traps

*Opening dialogue:* A§11 (the coffee shop) and A§38 (“Poverty is never adequate”). *Sources:* [A§11–12, §38; B§7; C§4]

## Part Six — The Road


### Chapter 14. Why the Living Wage Cannot Come First


- Production must come first; money without goods only raises prices.

- What a universal living wage would cost today: the cost table ($9–19 trillion a year; two to three times federal revenue).

- Why the gap-only version, paid dollar for dollar, builds a permanent cliff.

- The national debt: borrowing to consume rather than to build. The principle of a capital budget.

- Government as competitor and government as lender: why both lead to the hidden partner.


*Opening dialogue:* A§36 (“There has to be production first…”) and A§39. *Sources:* [A§22, §25, §36, §38–39; B§13, §18; C§7]


### Chapter 15. The Paradox of Plenty


- The displacement problem: goods with no buyers, fire sales, dead shops.

- The correction that changes the picture: money matched to unsold production does not raise prices.

- Friedman’s helicopter money and Douglas’s national dividend.

- The transition carries its own funding: the means of carrying people grows as fast as the need.

- The thermostat: a transition dividend that rises while core inflation stays at target and pauses when it does not. Cautions about supply shocks and about benefits that are easy to raise and hard to cut.

- Universal base plus a tapered need-based top-up: no claims to judge, no cliff, no “COVID scam.”

- Money to buyers, not producers: why paying firms to overproduce ends in collapse.

- The AI bubble, and why the design works whether AI comes fast or slow.


*Opening dialogue:* A§40–41 (“A nation of unemployed people who can’t buy anything, in an economy overflowing with things to buy”). *Sources:* [A§37, §40–42, §44; B§19–20, §22; C§7]


### Chapter 16. Phase 0: Cleaning the House


- Why the old golem must be tamed before new programs are built.

- One subject per bill; statement of intent; beneficiary disclosure; reading period.

- Small-entrant impact statements, tiered compliance, sunset clauses.

- Ramps, not cliffs, in every existing benefit.

- Discretion with reasons.

- What each costs, and why each one serves the neighbor.


*Opening dialogue:* A§10 or A§6 (short excerpt). *Sources:* [B§1, §4, §6–7; C§7 Phase 0]


### Chapter 17. Phase 1: Building People


- Education aimed at service, available now.

- Voluntary coaching: discovering gifts and learning to work with AI. Why churches, associations, and mentors rather than a federal Department of the Labor Pool.

- The parent–child model reconsidered: fitting for those who choose help over available work; wrong for the displaced, and impossible when everyone lives on the dividend.

- The thinking cap, presented as a claim to be demonstrated, with brain data owned by the person.

- Starter capital: a survivable stake for everyone, larger loans earned by track record.


*Opening dialogue:* A§14 (the honor roll, the thinking cap). *Sources:* [A§13–15, §26, §33; B§9, §14; C§7 Phase 1]


### Chapter 18. Phase 2: The Bridge


- The transition dividend in practice (from Chapter 15).

- The wage-subsidy bridge: the gap paid, a fixed taper (Isak’s “theta decay”), independent milestones, new positions only.

- Automate the administrative layer first: bookkeeping, forecasting, lead generation.

- Paying people to automate their own jobs: the difference between bot-sitting and true replacement; the displaced become the trainers.

- The shared-gain transition royalty: savings divided among employer, worker, and fund; a payment for every displaced worker; a bonus for the one who builds the agent.

- Agent creation companies: Susie the bookkeeper and Jenny in sales.

- The housing order book: committed buyers so builders can build.


*Opening dialogue:* A§29 (“Let’s bring it down to today…”). *Sources:* [A§13, §28–30, §45; B§8, §15, §21; C§7 Phase 2]


### Chapter 19. Phases 3 and 4: The Fund and the Garden


- The citizens’ automation fund: ownership stakes, a levy on automated output, or both.

- The dividend shifts from new money to the fund’s returns.

- The public capital budget.

- *Res publica*: the government is only us (Leonard’s challenge), and the republic’s membership now includes AI and robotic workers alongside its citizens.

*Opening dialogue:* A§29 (Thomas on *res publica*). *Sources:* [A§22, §25, §29; B§10, §13, Part Four; C§7 Phases 3–4]
