# The Prophet and the Attrition: Assessing "China's Nostradamus" on the US–Iran War

*An analysis essay · July 19, 2026*

[Source Article: ](https://www.themirror.com/news/weird-news/chinas-nostradamus-terrifying-warning-how-1803773)

---

## The claim and why it deserves scrutiny rather than dismissal

Professor Xueqin Jiang — a Chinese-Canadian history and philosophy teacher whose YouTube channel *Predictive History* has built a large following on the premise that Isaac Asimov's fictional "psycho-history" might be made real — has become a minor sensation for three predictions he made in 2024. He forecast that Donald Trump would win the 2024 election, that a second Trump term would very likely bring war with Iran driven substantially by Israeli strategic interests, and that the United States would *lose* that war in a way that "will forever change the global order."

The tabloid framing ("China's Nostradamus," "terrifying warning") invites easy dismissal, and the Nostradamus label is worth setting aside at once: Jiang is not claiming mystical foresight but rather structured geopolitical forecasting grounded in motive, terrain, logistics, and game theory. That is a respectable method, and it is the method his third prediction should be judged by — not by whether it sounds ominous, but by whether the reasoning holds against military history and the actual course of the war now underway.

Two clarifications matter before assessing the forecast. First, the first two predictions are less impressive than the framing suggests. Predicting a Trump win in 2024 was a coin-flip-to-favorable call that roughly half of serious analysts shared, and predicting an Iran war under Trump — given a decade of Israeli lobbying for strikes, Iran's advancing enrichment, and the collapse of the 2015 nuclear deal — was a high-probability bet, not a bolt from the blue. Forecasting the likely thing and being right does not validate a method; it is the third, contrarian prediction that carries the real epistemic weight.

Second, and decisively, we are no longer speculating. The war Jiang predicted exists. It is worth stating plainly what has actually happened so the forecast can be graded against events rather than against fear.
## What the war has actually been

The 2026 Iran war opened on February 28, 2026, when the United States (Operation Epic Fury) and Israel (Operation Roaring Lion) launched coordinated strikes on Iranian nuclear sites, military installations, and leadership. Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei was killed in the opening hours, along with the IRGC commander-in-chief, the armed forces chief of staff, and dozens of senior officers — an act of strategic decapitation almost without precedent in scale. Within hours, Iran retaliated with drones and ballistic missiles across the region and closed the Strait of Hormuz to US- and Israel-allied shipping, triggering a global oil-price spike and fuel shortages reaching into Asia.

The months since have settled into a distinctive shape. A two-week ceasefire brokered by Pakistan in early April collapsed into renewed strikes; a June memorandum of understanding intended to end the war has likewise failed to hold, with Trump declaring the truce over on July 7 after Iranian attacks on shipping. As of mid-July 2026, the fighting continues largely in the air and at sea. The Strait remains effectively closed: daily transits have fallen from roughly 110 ships before the war to a trickle, and Iran continues to mine the waterway, board vessels, and conduct GPS spoofing. Iran has struck GCC energy and water infrastructure — including a confirmed strike on a Kuwaiti desalination plant — exactly the vulnerability Jiang highlighted. And the human cost, though real, has been strikingly asymmetric in a particular way: seventeen US service members have died over nearly five months, reflecting a conflict fought overwhelmingly from the air, while civilian and economic damage across Iran, the Gulf, and the wider region has been heavy.

Against this record, several of Jiang's specific structural claims have held up well, and one central prediction remains genuinely unresolved.
## Where his reasoning is strong

The most impressive feature of Jiang's analysis is that he correctly located the war's center of gravity. He argued in 2024 that Iran's advantages were not in winning battles but in imposing intolerable costs on the global economy, that the Strait of Hormuz and Gulf infrastructure were the decisive levers, and that Iran had spent two decades preparing precisely this kind of asymmetric, attritional fight. Every one of those judgments has been vindicated by events. This is the heart of his case, and it is analytically sound.

The underlying logic is old and well tested. Iran cannot defeat the US Navy or Air Force in a symmetrical exchange, and it has never needed to. Its strategy is what strategists call *cost imposition*: convert its geography astride the world's most important oil chokepoint into leverage that the adversary's own allies and economy cannot sustain. The vulnerability of desalination plants — on which Gulf states depend for drinking water — and of export terminals is real, cheap to exploit with drones and missiles, and nearly impossible to defend comprehensively. Vice President Vance conceded the core of this in July: you can degrade Iran's radars, drones, and missiles, but "it's just too easy to fire at ships in the straits," which is why he insists the war cannot be won by force alone. That is Jiang's thesis stated by the sitting US vice president.

His historical analogy to the Athenian Sicilian Expedition (415–413 BC) is apt in one important respect and misleading in another. Apt: Sicily is the archetype of a great power launching an ambitious expeditionary war of choice, winning early, then discovering that distance, hubris, and an underestimated enemy converted initial success into catastrophe. The warning against extrapolating from opening victories is exactly right, and the opening decapitation of Iran's leadership is precisely the kind of early "success" that can seduce a power into deeper commitment. Where the analogy strains is that Athens was annihilated because it committed a huge land and naval force it could not extract; the United States, so far, has done the opposite — it has deliberately *withheld* the ground army whose destruction would make a Sicily-scale disaster possible.
## Where the prediction is weaker or unproven

The forecast's vulnerability lies in the word "lose," which Jiang uses without defining, and in the mechanism he says will produce defeat: a ground war.

Jiang's stated path to American defeat runs through boots on the ground. He argues that regime change cannot be accomplished from the air, that pressure will build — from Israel and the Gulf states — to send in ground troops, and that Iran's 90-million population, mountainous terrain, and long logistics tails will then turn early victory into strategic ruin. The first half of that chain is correct and is the near-unanimous view of military analysts: no air campaign has ever produced regime change against an intact security apparatus (Libya 2011 required rebels on the ground; the sole clean air-and-decapitation surrender, Japan 1945, required nuclear weapons and the threat of Soviet invasion). Estimates for a serious invasion of Iran run from several hundred thousand troops to, in one analysis, roughly 1.6 million — nearly ten times the peak Iraq deployment. On the "a ground invasion would be a disaster" point, Jiang is almost certainly right, and he is in the company of the Cato Institute, the Atlantic Council, and the Arab Center.

But the prediction's load-bearing assumption is that the United States *will take that path*, and here the evidence to date cuts against him. Five months in, Washington has not mobilized conventional ground forces, and the political constraints against doing so are severe: the war polls at roughly one-quarter public support, Trump's own MAGA base is hostile to another Middle Eastern land war, and the administration's stated objectives have been framed precisely to be achievable — or at least attemptable — through air and sea power. The likelier American failure mode is not a Sicilian catastrophe of a shattered invasion force but something quieter and, for Jiang's dramatic framing, less satisfying: a *stalemate*. An open-ended air and naval campaign that degrades Iran without toppling it, cannot reopen Hormuz at acceptable cost, drains expensive interceptor stockpiles against cheap drones, and grinds on until a face-saving settlement leaves the regime — now under harder-line IRGC leadership than before — battered but in power.

That outcome would arguably vindicate the *spirit* of Jiang's prediction while falsifying its *mechanism*. If "losing" means failing to achieve the war's objectives — denuclearization, reopening the Strait, regime change, deterrence restored — then the current trajectory already looks like a loss, and the consensus of CSIS, the Council on Foreign Relations, and the Soufan Center is that the US achieved tactical damage but could not reach Iran's deeply buried infrastructure, end the Strait threat, or produce its desired political result. But if "losing" means the vivid, order-shattering military defeat his rhetoric implies — an American army broken in the Zagros, a superpower humbled the way Athens was — that requires an escalation the United States has so far conspicuously refused, and betting on it is a bet on Washington making the single worst available choice.
## The methodological caveats

Two cautions temper any credit assigned to Jiang. The first is selection and framing bias in the presentation of his record. His easy predictions are counted alongside his hard ones; his forecasts are stated with enough elasticity ("very likely," "in the next two to four years," "no way America can win") that a wide range of outcomes can be claimed as confirmation. A stalemate, a negotiated exit, a bloody quasi-victory, or an actual rout could each be narrated afterward as "he was basically right." Forecasts that cannot be clearly falsified are weaker than they appear, however prescient the analyst.

The second is that Jiang is not a neutral instrument. He is a compelling communicator with a channel whose brand is bold, contrarian, anti-establishment prediction, and whose thesis — American imperial decline — is congenial to both his platform's incentives and a Chinese nationalist frame. None of this makes him wrong. It does mean his confidence should be discounted toward the underlying structural analysis, which is strong, and away from the theatrical certainty of "no way America can win," which outruns what anyone can know.
## Verdict

On the balance of evidence, Jiang's third prediction is *directionally credible and probably closer to right than the pre-war Washington consensus*, but for reasons partly different from the ones he gave, and with a confidence level his own rhetoric overstates.

He correctly identified the war's asymmetric logic, the centrality of Hormuz and Gulf infrastructure, Iran's long preparation, and the futility of air power for regime change — and each has been borne out. He is very likely right that a ground invasion would be catastrophic. He is on weaker ground in assuming the United States will actually launch one, and thus in tying "defeat" to a Sicilian-style land disaster rather than to the more probable outcome of costly, inconclusive stalemate. The most defensible reading is this: the United States is unlikely to *win* this war in any sense that matches its stated objectives, which is a meaningful vindication of Jiang's core insight; but "the United States will lose, forever changing the global order" is a larger and more dramatic claim that current events support only in their milder, attritional form and that could still be falsified by a negotiated settlement that lets all sides declare victory.

The honest assessment, in short, is that the analyst got the physics of the war right and the theater of it wrong — and that a stalemate which quietly erodes American credibility, rather than a battlefield catastrophe that spectacularly ends it, is the outcome to watch for.
