# 261112 - Viral Rant Against Government Commentary

# They Get Another Election:


### On a Viral Rant and the Limits of "Just Do Your Jobs"

Thomas Lee Abshier, ND · 12 November 2026

## The Video

A friend sent me a clip of [Benny Johnson](https://youtube.com/shorts/J7e7ZzE20Ic?si=L64OecJK7OCieV-K), billed as "the most viral political video on the internet right now." It is three minutes of a profanity-laced rant by a 33-year-old man, filmed as he drives.

His argument runs like this:


 	- Every day brings a new crisis, a new accusation, a new televised fight. He is worn out by it.

 	- Markets lurch on words. A threat to bomb Iran sends oil up; talk of a peace deal sends it down; the talks stall, and it goes up again. "Are we running a country or a day trading group chat?"

 	- Someone is profiting from those swings, and almost nobody is asking who.

 	- Politics has become a team sport. We defend politicians we have never met more fiercely than the neighbors, coworkers, and family we actually live with, and we have convinced ourselves that half the country is the enemy.

 	- Government is supposed to work for us. He wants officials to do their jobs with his tax dollars, work together, fix things, be transparent, and stop treating the people as an audience.

 	- Your life is the one thing you can't replace. "They get another election. We don't get another life."


There is a good deal here I agree with. There is also a worldview underneath it that will not hold the weight he wants to put on it. I want to give him his due first, and then say plainly where I think he goes wrong.

## Is He a Libertarian?

My first impression was that he sounds like a libertarian who thinks the world would run fine if the government would simply get out of the way. On a careful reading, that is not quite what he says. He does not ask for government to disappear. He asks it to *perform*: "do their jobs with my tax-paying dollars," "work together," "fix things," "be transparent." He wants "a country where I can afford to live." That is not anarchism. It is the wish of the exhausted independent for a quiet, competent, nonpartisan government that delivers results without demanding his attention.

The libertarian note is real, though, and it comes down to one sentence: "We are not supposed to spend our entire lives worrying about what the government is gonna do next." That is the instinct for a government small enough to be ignored, one whose decisions do not reach into a man's paycheck, savings, and peace of mind every morning.

So his view has two layers, and I will address both. The surface layer is: government without the drama, run by grown-ups who cooperate. The deeper layer is: a life lived free of government's shadow. I stand on this side: his instinct is right, and his remedy is wrong. He is right that politics has swallowed too much of American life. He is wrong that the cure is for the people to look away while the politicians quiet down.

## What He Gets Right

**The markets really do swing on words.** This is not a figure of speech. Through the spring of 2026, oil moved by five, six, and seven percent in a day on headlines about U.S.–Iran talks: down on reports of a deal, back up when a proposal was rejected or talks stalled. Analysts said openly that diplomatic signals, not supply and demand, were driving prices. His "day trading group chat" line fairly describes what the oil market looked like.

**His question about who profits is not paranoia.** The Commodity Futures Trading Commission opened an investigation into oil futures trades placed shortly before major presidential announcements on the Iran war, including roughly $950 million bet on lower prices hours before the April ceasefire was announced. Later reporting put the profits from well-timed trades around several announcements in the billions, with the Justice Department working alongside the CFTC. The White House itself warned staff against using their positions to bet on futures. When a man on YouTube asks "Who's buying? Who's selling? Who actually knows what's happening behind closed doors?", he is asking the same question federal investigators are asking. Whatever those probes conclude, the concern is plausible on its face: when a handful of people know what will be announced, and billions ride on the announcement, the temptation to trade on that knowledge is enormous, and the money motive is obvious.

**He is right about the neighbor.** The sharpest thing he says is moral, not economic: we have learned to love distant politicians and despise the people next door. That is a disorder of the heart before it is a disorder of the republic. Christ did not command us to love our party as ourselves; He commanded us to love our neighbor, and the neighbor is the man across the fence who votes the other way.

**He is right about proportion.** A man who cannot get through a day without a new national emergency has let something trivial occupy the place of something eternal. *"Take therefore no thought for the morrow: for the morrow shall take thought for the things of itself. Sufficient unto the day is the evil thereof."* **(Matthew 6:34)** He is not wrong to want his life back.

**He is right that government is the servant.** The Declaration says governments are instituted to secure our rights and derive their just powers from the consent of the governed. A government that treats the people as an audience for its own drama has inverted that order.

## Where the Worldview Breaks Down


### 1. The noise is not separate from the news

He talks as if politicians who cannot keep their mouths shut caused the oil swings. They are not. The United States was in a shooting war with Iran, and the Strait of Hormuz, which carried about a fifth of the world's oil and liquefied natural gas, was closed or threatened. Of course the price of oil moved when the prospects for war or peace moved. The market was not reacting to chatter; it was pricing real danger to real supply.

Asking the people running the country to "shut up for five minutes" in a war is asking reality to shut up. A government at war must speak to its enemy, its allies, its markets, and its own people, and every word matters because the stakes are real. The honest complaint is not that they talk, but that we are exposed to so much risk that every word reaches our wallets. That exposure can be reduced through fewer wars of choice and an energy supply at home that doesn't depend on a strait half a world away, but silence can't wish it away.

### 2. "Work together and fix things" assumes we agree on what fixed means

This is the deepest flaw in the rant, and it is common to nearly every "I don't care about red or blue" appeal. It assumes the problems are technical and the disagreements are theater. Some of the theater is real, and I despise it as much as he does. But much of what looks like drama is the noise of genuine moral conflict: over life and death, war and peace, borders, marriage, money, what children are taught, and what the government may take from one man to give to another. These are not misunderstandings that will dissolve if everyone sits at the same table. They are questions on which serious people hold incompatible convictions.

Politics is how a free people settle those conflicts without violence. To wish for politics without disagreement is to wish for a people without convictions, and such a people would not be free for long.

Even his own wish is a political position. "A country where I can afford to live" requires choices about energy, housing, spending, debt, and taxes, and each choice has winners and losers and partisans on each side. Declaring himself above red and blue does not exempt him from those choices. It only means that other people will make them for him.

### 3. Tuning out hands the country to the people who profit from the chaos

Here his rant contradicts itself. In the middle, he asks, "Why aren't we asking this more often?" and complains that "we're barely even paying attention to who's benefiting." At the end, he concludes that the biggest problem is that "we've let politics take up so much of the one life we're supposed to be living."

But the question "who benefits?" can only be asked by people who are paying attention. The suspicious oil trades came to light because reporters, regulators, and ordinary people watching the tape noticed the timing. The man who withdraws from politics to protect his peace of mind does not stop the insider; he gives the insider cover. Disengagement is the condition under which a few people quietly profit from the many. The price of self-government is that the governed must keep governing.

### 4. The libertarian dream needs government to get there

If the deeper wish is a life free of government's shadow, I am sympathetic. I want a smaller federal government than we have. But the world does not run well without government, and he knows it: the very market he complains about exists within a legal framework. Contracts are enforced by courts. Regulators police fraud and insider trading. The CFTC investigating those oil trades is the government doing exactly what he asked it to do, which is its job.

The strongest point on his side, and the one he does not quite make, is this: every syllable out of Washington moves billions of dollars because Washington holds so much power. When one man's post can start or stop a war, close or open a shipping lane, or impose or lift a tariff, his words become market events. Concentrated power makes words dangerous. Shrink the power, and you shrink the swings.

But shrinking power is political work. It is slow, unglamorous, and requires sustained attention from citizens over decades. A government small enough to ignore has never been won by people who ignored it. Liberty is not achieved by walking away; it is defended by people who stay.

### 5. Washington is a mirror

He blames the people running the country for treating Americans like an audience. But politicians perform drama because we reward drama. Outrage gets the clicks, the clicks get the money, and the money gets the votes. The video itself proves the point: a rant against the political circus, reposted as "the most viral political video on the internet," ending with an appeal for comments and looping back to the start. It is rage content about rage content, and it spreads for the same reason the politicians' drama spreads.

The problem is upstream of Washington. A nation's leaders reflect the character of its people. *"Righteousness exalteth a nation: but sin is a reproach to any people."* **(Proverbs 14:34)** If we want leaders who do their jobs quietly and honestly, we must become a people who value quiet, honest work over performance, and who vote, give, and click accordingly.

## What a Realistic Version of His Wish Looks Like

His wish is a good one: to live his life, enjoy it, and be free of the daily whiplash of the news. Here is what I think it looks like when it is made practical rather than merely angry.


 	- **Proportion, not withdrawal.** Take the news once a day, not once a minute. A citizen needs to be informed, not marinated. If he trades, trade a plan rather than a headline; the man who sells on every threat and buys on every rumor has chosen to ride the whipsaw.

 	- **Act where your hand reaches.** The school board, county commission, city council, and state legislature govern more of daily life than the cable-news fights do, and one engaged citizen can actually move them.

 	- **Vote on character and principle, not team.** He is right that party loyalty has become tribal. The remedy is to judge candidates by what they do and what they stand for, not by the color of their jersey.

 	- **Demand transparency with teeth.** If officials or their circles are trading on advance knowledge of war decisions, that is not drama; it is corruption. Strict prohibition and real enforcement against trading by anyone with access to nonpublic policy decisions is a concrete demand he and nearly every American could unite behind.

 	- **Love the neighbor in front of you.** He said it himself: we are neighbors, coworkers, families. The healing of the republic starts at the kitchen table and the fence line, not in Washington.


The prophet's counsel to the exiles in Babylon still fits a man who feels like a stranger in his own country: *"And seek the peace of the city whither I have caused you to be carried away captives, and pray unto the LORD for it: for in the peace thereof shall ye have peace."* **(Jeremiah 29:7)** Peace of mind is not something the government can give him. But the peace of the city, which he does need, is partly his responsibility.

## Conclusion

He is right that they get another election and we do not get another life. But the life we get is lived among neighbors, in a nation, under laws that someone will write. Part of loving those neighbors is the small, steady, unexciting work of self-government. The answer to a circus is not to stop paying attention. It is to stop buying tickets, and to help rebuild the town around it.
