# 261024 - Healthcare, the Independent Physician, and Competition

# Restoring the Independent Physician: A Moral Case for Competition in Medicine


*By Dr. Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | 24 October 2026*


*A response to “Restoring Independent Medicine,” by Drs. Jill Glasspool Malone and Robert W. Malone (Malone News, October 24, 2026). Link in Resources below.*


---


## Where I Stand


I stand with the independent physician. I believe American medicine should once again be practiced mostly by doctors who own their practices, answer directly to their patients, post their prices, and live in the communities they serve. I believe the present drift toward hospital corporations, insurance-company control, and private-equity ownership is not a neutral market outcome but, in large part, the product of government rules that reward size over service. And I believe a president can do a great deal to reverse it without building another federal empire.


The Malones have written a careful, well-sourced essay making much of this case, and I commend it to every reader. I want to add what a position paper on this site must add: the moral reason for the policy. Economics tells us what works. Morality tells us what we owe one another. A policy can be efficient and still be wrong, and a policy that is right will usually prove efficient in the long run, because it aligns human incentives with human goodness.


As a naturopathic physician, I write from inside the profession. Naturopathic and integrative medicine has long lived largely outside the insurance system, paid for directly by patients who chose it. That arrangement has costs, but it also preserves something the rest of medicine is losing: a doctor and a patient who decide together, with nobody else at the table.


## The Problem in Brief


The numbers are not in dispute. According to the American Medical Association, the share of American physicians working in private practice fell from 60.1 percent in 2012 to 42.2 percent in 2024, and the share holding any ownership stake fell from 53.2 percent to 35.4 percent. In twelve years, the independent physician went from the norm to the minority.


Meanwhile prices have risen. A RAND Corporation study found that in 2022, private insurers and employers paid hospitals on average 254 percent of what Medicare would have paid for the same services at the same facilities, and RAND found that the degree of local competition influenced those prices more than the hospital’s share of Medicare or Medicaid patients. Where competition disappears, prices rise. That is not ideology; it is arithmetic.


The causes are several, and they reinforce one another:


- **Unequal payment for the same work.** Medicare and private insurers frequently pay more when a service is delivered in a hospital-owned outpatient department than when the same service is delivered in an independent office. A practice becomes more “valuable” the moment a hospital buys it, not because the care improved but because the billing changed.

- **Regulation that small practices cannot absorb.** Electronic-record mandates and incentives, quality-reporting systems, prior authorization, credentialing, and laboratory certification each carry fixed compliance costs. A hospital system spreads those costs across thousands of employees; a three-doctor practice cannot. The 1988 laboratory rules (CLIA) are a clear example: in a 1998 study, nearly two-thirds of surveyed physicians said the rules led them to reduce or eliminate in-office testing, with rural and pediatric practices hit hardest.

- **Third-party payment for routine care.** When an insurance company stands between patient and doctor for every visit, the patient cannot learn the price, the doctor must hire staff to fight for payment, and neither controls the transaction.

- **Training for employment, not ownership.** Medical schools teach a young doctor to diagnose disease but rarely teach him to read a lease, make a payroll, or price a service. He graduates with heavy debt, no business training, and a hospital recruiter waiting with a salary offer.


## The Moral Test


On this site I measure policy by three questions, asked in order:


- **Is it righteous?** Does it conform to what God establishes as just, whatever its convenience?

- **If so, is it loving?** Does it serve the real good of my neighbor, not merely his momentary wishes?

- **Would I want it done to me?** If I stood in my neighbor’s place, feeling his pain in my own body and mind, measured by the self I am committed to being and not by passing appetite, would I welcome it?


Apply these to the present system.


**Righteousness.** Scripture is direct about prices and measures: “Thou shalt not have in thy bag divers weights, a great and a small” (Deuteronomy 25:13), and “A false balance is abomination to the Lord: but a just weight is his delight” (Proverbs 11:1). A payment system that pays one owner more than another for the same service, to the same patient, with the same outcome, is a system of divers weights. A pricing system in which the patient cannot learn the cost until weeks after the service is a balance hidden under the counter. These are not technicalities. They are the kind of dealing God names and condemns. Righteousness requires one price for one service, honestly posted.


Righteousness also concerns who is accountable to whom. A physician owes his first duty to the patient before him. When a corporate administrator, an insurance reviewer, or a federal reporting metric stands between that physician and his judgment, the line of accountability is bent. “The labourer is worthy of his hire” (Luke 10:7), and the patient is worthy of a doctor whose hire comes from him.


**Love.** Love for my neighbor means caring whether he can find a doctor who knows his name, who has time to listen, and who will still be there next year. The closure of all 51 Walmart Health centers in 2024, the retreat of Walgreens’ VillageMD clinics, and the bankruptcy of Steward Health Care, which closed two Massachusetts hospitals, show what happens when care is treated as a portfolio position. Communities are left stranded when a distant board decides the returns were disappointing. A system of thousands of independent practices cannot fail all at once. Love chooses resilience over scale.


Love also means not humoring the neighbor. Some will say that consolidation is fine because patients like the convenience of a large system’s app and its single sign-on. Convenience is real, but love asks what the neighbor is paying for it, in money, in privacy, and in the relationship with his doctor. The 2024 Change Healthcare breach exposed the records of roughly 193 million Americans in a single attack. Patients were never asked whether they wished to accept that risk.


**Would I want it done to me?** I would not want to learn the price of my treatment from a bill that arrives a month later. I would not want my doctor’s recommendation shaped by a utilization reviewer I will never meet. I would not want to be the young physician who wanted to buy his retiring mentor’s practice but could not find the financing, and so watched it sold to a hospital chain. And I would not want to be the patient in a small town whose only clinic closed because a corporation in another state revised its strategy.


The present system fails all three questions. The direction of reform is therefore not a matter of taste. It is a matter of justice.


## A New ERA Applied to Medicine


My platform rests on three words: Energy, Righteousness, and Accountability. The first is the foundation of material abundance. The second and third govern this question directly. Righteousness asks for just weights and honest dealing. Accountability asks that every actor answer to the person he serves: the physician to his patient, the insurer to his policyholder, and the regulator to the citizen, not to the industry he regulates.


## What I Would Do


What follows is advocacy, not decree. Each proposal is my best present judgment, and I hold each open to a better argument.


### 1. One service, one price


I would direct the Department of Health and Human Services to pursue site-neutral payment in Medicare: when a service does not require hospital-level resources, Medicare pays the same amount whether it is delivered in a hospital outpatient department or an independent office. Where hospital resources are genuinely required, they should be paid for openly as such, not hidden in a facility fee attached to an office visit. This is the “just weight” principle in federal payment policy, and it removes one of the strongest financial incentives for hospitals to buy physician practices.


### 2. Posted prices, in plain language


HHS finalized stronger price-transparency rules in October 2026. I support them and would go further in one respect: every provider who accepts federal payment should be able to give a patient, before a non-emergency service, a single all-in cash price. A free market cannot work where the buyer is not allowed to know the price.


### 3. Lift the regulatory weight off small practices


- **Revisit CLIA** so that simple, well-established in-office tests can again be run in a family physician’s office without a compliance burden designed for commercial laboratories, while preserving genuine quality standards.

- **Restore choice in record-keeping.** Federal law does not strictly require electronic records, but payment rules make opting out almost impossible. I would remove the penalties attached to the choice, require that any electronic system export a patient’s complete record in an open, standard format, and affirm that the record belongs to the patient, who may take it to any doctor, on paper or electronically.

- **Cut prior authorization and reporting** to what demonstrably protects patients. Every requirement should have to show a benefit proportionate to its cost, and the cost borne by small practices should be counted separately, because it is that cost which drives them to sell.


### 4. Open the door to ownership


The Malones propose a “Beginning Physician Practice Program” modeled on the Department of Agriculture’s Beginning Farmer and Rancher loans and on existing Small Business Administration lending. I support the idea, with a caution I want to state plainly. It is a government program designed to undo distortions created by other government programs. My first preference is to remove the distortions, which would make independent practice viable on its own merits. But the damage has compounded for decades, and a young doctor carrying medical-school debt cannot wait for every rule to be repealed. So I would support repayable, time-limited lending through existing SBA channels and community banks, with eligibility based on credentials, independent ownership, and a sound business plan, and with no federal conditions on which patients a doctor sees or what he charges. Borrowers repay; lenders still judge viability. The purpose is to open a door, not to create a new dependent.


The same logic supports helping younger physicians buy practices from retiring colleagues. Every practice that passes from one independent doctor to another, rather than to a hospital chain, keeps a community’s medical memory intact.


### 5. Teach doctors to own, not only to obey


Medical schools should teach practice management, accounting, contracts, and the economics of medicine, and residency programs should give young doctors time in successful independent practices. A president cannot dictate curriculum, but the federal government funds much of graduate medical education and can ask, at minimum, that the young physicians it helps to train be equipped to choose independence.


### 6. Enforce competition honestly


I would direct the Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice to scrutinize hospital mergers and physician-practice roll-ups with the same seriousness applied to any other industry where a few firms dominate a regional market. I would also support state efforts to limit noncompete clauses that prevent a physician from continuing to care for his own patients when he leaves corporate employment. A patient is not an asset of the corporation that employed his doctor.


### 7. Return routine care to the patient’s own hands


Health Savings Accounts, direct primary care, and catastrophic coverage already exist, and 2026 changes allow qualifying direct primary care arrangements to coexist with HSA eligibility. I would build on that: insurance for catastrophe, which is what insurance is for, and direct payment for ordinary care, which is what a doctor’s office is for. No American should be pushed to abandon insurance; every American should be free to step around it for the routine visit.


### 8. Restore charity as a calling


The objection every reformer must answer is this: what about the man who cannot pay? The Good Samaritan answers it in part. He tended the wounded man himself, paid the innkeeper directly, and promised to pay whatever more was needed (Luke 10:34–35). Before the third-party system, physicians, churches, and religious hospitals carried much of the charity load, imperfectly but personally. That tradition had real gaps, and any honest reform must keep public protection for catastrophic illness, disability, and poverty. But I would also make charity easier rather than harder: allow physicians to deduct the fair value of documented free care, simplify the rules for church-sponsored and volunteer clinics, and protect volunteer physicians from liability exposure that discourages them from serving. Charity given personally carries love in a way a reimbursement form never can.


## The Strongest Objections, and Why the Concern Stands


Fairness requires facing the best arguments for consolidation.


**“Large systems coordinate care and invest in technology small practices cannot afford.”** Sometimes they do. But the research reviewed by the Government Accountability Office and others has repeatedly associated consolidation with higher prices, while improvements in quality have been inconsistent. If integration delivered better care at lower cost, its prices would show it. They show the opposite.


**“Some rural hospitals survive only because a system acquired them.”** This is true in particular cases, and I would not force any working arrangement apart. But Steward Health Care shows that the system which rescues a hospital can also be the system that drags it under. Resilience comes from many owners, not from a single larger one.


**“Independent practice is nostalgia; young doctors want salaries and predictable hours.”** Many do, and they should be free to choose employment. The question is whether that choice is free. A young physician with large debt, no business training, no access to capital, and a payment system that pays his employer more than it would pay him is not choosing employment; he is being herded into it.


**“The statistics can be read other ways.”** Some can. The decline in private practice may partly reflect generational preference; price comparisons across facilities involve real differences in patient mix; and studies of private-equity ownership show varied results. I grant every one of these areas of uncertainty. They do not change the center of the matter.


Why does the concern remain plausible, even granting these uncertainties? First, the intuitive sense that something is unnatural: medicine has always been a personal relationship, and a patient knows when his doctor is hurried, constrained, and answering to someone else. That intuition is widely shared by patients and physicians alike, and it deserves weight. Second, the money motive is visible: a practice is worth more to a hospital the day after purchase because the billing rules make it so, and private-equity firms are explicit that they acquire practices for return on investment. Third, the links between policy and outcome are direct and documented: payment differentials, compliance costs, and record-keeping incentives each fall more heavily on small practices, and each was written in Washington. When the rules reward size, size wins. Change the rules, and the independent physician can compete again.


## The Choice Before Us


We are told that health care reform means choosing between more government and more corporate control. That is a false choice. Both concentrate power far from the examining room, and the two increasingly reinforce each other: corporations write the rules they can afford to obey, and government grows dependent on the corporations it is supposed to regulate. The Malones rightly call this corporatism, and it is not free enterprise.


The alternative is older and better: a doctor who owns his practice, posts his prices, answers to his patients, and gives charity as a matter of conscience. Every man under his own vine and fig tree (Micah 4:4) applies to the physician as much as to the farmer. A nation of independent doctors is harder to capture, harder to bankrupt, and easier to trust.


I offer these proposals as a starting point. If someone shows me a better path to the same end, a medicine that is righteous, loving, and accountable, I will take it gladly.
