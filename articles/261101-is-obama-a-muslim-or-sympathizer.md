# 261101 - Is Obama a Muslim or Sympathizer

# Obama’s Religious Affiliation:


### A Critique of Evidence of Obama as a Muslim


*Dr. Thomas | 1 November 2026*


A friend whose faith and love of country I respect recently shared a post on Facebook titled “Obama: It Was You.” It lists some thirty charges against President Obama, each beginning “It was you who…,” and introduces it as an essay by Mike Gallagher, the radio host heard by more than two million listeners a week. The post has circulated by email and social media since at least 2015, and versions of its individual items go back to 2008. Every few years it comes around again.


Many people in the conservative and Christian community sense that something was wrong in how the Obama administration treated the Christian faith that formed this nation, and in how it spoke about the ideology that attacked us on September 11. I share that sense. But when I checked the claims on the list, I found that many items were false or only partially true.


How can any of us know what to trust? I used an AI research assistant to check each claim, and I questioned it closely, because its training leans on the mainstream press, which largely defended this President. I asked it to show its sources, to seek out the strongest case from conservative and alternative outlets, and to call a claim “false” only where primary evidence anyone can check — a statute, a school register, a transcript, a video — contradicts it, not merely because a fact-checker said so. Several times my own memory and judgment pushed back, and the findings changed. The “Sources and their limits” section at the end tells you what was used and where the picture may still be incomplete.


Most people don’t have time to do this for every post. Pilate asked, “What is truth?” (John 18:38), and walked away without waiting for an answer. The commandment does not require us to know everything. It requires honest witness: testify only to what you know, with the confidence you actually have. I want this site to be credible, and I want its plans to move the nation toward real solutions. A movement that claims to stand for righteousness cannot build its case on untruth.


So this essay does five things. It sets out what the record actually shows about the list. It explains why we should not pass along charges we have not verified. It explains why the underlying concern is nonetheless plausible. It answers the question the list was really asking — who Barack Obama was, religiously — because disproving the particulars without answering that question would leave a false impression of its own. And it closes with a discipline for checking what we share that anyone can practice in a few minutes.


## Who wrote it


Mike Gallagher did not write it. He posted it on his Facebook page in January 2015, noting that someone had sent it to him and that listeners had asked about it. The attribution to him grew out of that post. The actual author is unknown. This is the first sign of the problem: the list borrows the credibility of a known name it did not earn.


## What the record shows


I have placed a full item-by-item table at the end of this essay. In summary:


**Plainly false.** About a dozen items have no basis at all, or are contradicted by the record.


- Muslims were not exempted from the Affordable Care Act’s mandate. The law’s religious exemption applied only to groups that reject all insurance, including Social Security — in practice, the Amish and a few similar communities. No Muslim group qualified.

- The United States did not “funnel $900 million to Hamas.” In 2009 the administration pledged $900 million for the Palestinian territories: about $300 million in humanitarian aid for Gaza, routed through the UN and aid organizations specifically to keep it away from Hamas, and the rest to the Palestinian Authority, which is Hamas’s rival. One may fairly argue that aid into Gaza inevitably benefits Hamas — serious analysts made that argument at the time — but that is a different claim from the one in the list.

- The Eid postage stamp was first issued on September 1, 2001, under President Bush, and a citizens’ advisory committee, not the President, chooses stamp subjects.

- Valerie Jarrett is not Iranian, and there is no evidence she is Muslim, let alone a member of a “Muslim Sisterhood.” She was born in Shiraz, Iran, to American parents who were working there.

- President Obama attended the National Prayer Breakfast every year of his presidency. The White House iftar dinners he hosted continued a practice of Presidents Clinton and Bush.

- He issued Christmas greetings every year. He vacationed in Hawaii at Christmas, after attending the White House’s own Christmas events.

- No Muslim custom requires removing all jewelry for Ramadan, and he did not.

- Arabic was never made mandatory in American grammar schools. A federal foreign-language grant program that predated his administration funded optional Arabic in some districts, alongside Chinese and other languages.

- I found no record that he told Egypt’s foreign minister “I am a Muslim” or spent $100 million rebuilding foreign mosques. Nor did I find the words “I am one of you” at any Islamic dinner. The claim names no date, place, or witness, so it cannot be tested. It may be a compression of things he did say: at the 2011 White House iftar he told his guests that in America “there is no ‘them’ or ‘us’; it’s just us,” and in Jakarta in 2010, in a speech addressed to the Muslim world, he opened with “Indonesia is part of me.” In context, the first meant Americans of every faith, and the second meant his childhood home. But a man who speaks to Muslim audiences in the language of belonging should not be surprised when listeners hear more than he meant.


**Real words or events, wrongly framed.** Roughly another dozen items take something that happened and present it as something it was not.


- “My Muslim faith.” On ABC’s *This Week* in September 2008, Obama was complaining that opponents were spreading the rumor that he was a Muslim. George Stephanopoulos pointed out that the McCain campaign had not suggested it. Obama replied that McCain “has not talked about my Muslim faith.” Stephanopoulos interjected, “Christian faith”; Obama repeated “my Christian faith,” then clarified that what he meant was that McCain had not suggested he was a Muslim. If he meant the faith rumored to be his, the interjection was unnecessary — even Snopes concluded Stephanopoulos had misunderstood the context — and the haste of both men to fix it shows how well they knew what the phrase would do in a clip. It did exactly that: edited clips circulated within an hour. Some critics read the phrase as a Freudian slip. That reading can be neither proven nor disproven, and this essay does not rest its case on it. Stronger evidence lies in what Obama said deliberately, as discussed below.

- “I will stand with the Muslims.” This distorts a passage in *The Audacity of Hope* in which he wrote that he would stand with Arab and Pakistani immigrants if the political climate turned against their civil liberties. It said nothing about siding with Muslims in a conflict against the United States.

- The Georgetown speech. In April 2009, the White House asked Georgetown to cover all signage and symbols behind the stage so the President could speak before a backdrop of flags, as at other policy speeches. That included the IHS monogram, the ancient Christian symbol of the name of Jesus, which was covered with black plywood. Other religious symbols remained visible elsewhere in the hall. The request was real and, in my judgment, careless toward a Christian institution. But it was not an order to “shroud all vestiges of Jesus Christ,” and nothing similar happened at Notre Dame.

- The Sermon on the Mount. In a 2006 speech, he asked which passages of Scripture should guide public policy, contrasting Leviticus and Deuteronomy with the Sermon on the Mount, which he called so radical that the Defense Department could not survive its application. That is a serious theological error about how Scripture bears on public life, and Christians are right to answer it. But it was not mockery of Jesus. He did use the phrase “Holy Qur’an” in Cairo and in Ramadan greetings; that is customary diplomatic courtesy.

- The “Ground Zero mosque.” The Park51 project was two blocks from the site, not overlooking it. He defended the legal right to build it and the next day said he was not commenting on the wisdom of doing so. Reasonable people objected to the project on grounds of prudence and respect for the dead. “Victory mosque” is a critic’s label, not a fact.

- The bow. He did bow, or something close to it, to King Abdullah in April 2009. American presidents customarily do not bow to monarchs, and criticism was fair. “In submission” is a reading of his heart that no one can verify.

- Trinity United Church of Christ. He was a member for about twenty years, and its pastor, Jeremiah Wright, preached sermons condemning America in terms that should trouble any Christian. The church’s black liberation theology draws on Marxist categories. But the congregation did not “profess Marxism,” and he left the church in 2008.

- The Mao ornament. One volunteer-decorated ornament on a 2009 White House tree carried Mao’s image among other collage pictures. It was a poor choice. It was not the President “hanging photos of Chairman Mao on the tree.”

- Military tribunals were suspended in 2009 and resumed in 2011 under revised rules. A 2010 U.S. report to the UN Human Rights Council mentioned the Arizona immigration law. NASA Administrator Charles Bolden did tell Al Jazeera in 2010 that, of three charges the President gave him, “perhaps foremost” was to engage Muslim-majority nations and help them feel good about their historic contribution to science, math, and engineering. NASA then restated that its primary missions are science, aeronautics, and exploration, and the White House said that was not the charge it gave him. The remark was Bolden’s, not the President’s, and it was about NASA’s outreach, not its mission — though it was still a strange priority to announce for a space agency.


**Substantially true.** A few items rest on solid ground, and they are serious.


- As an Illinois state senator, Barack Obama opposed the Born-Alive Infants Protection bills in 2001, 2002, and 2003, including — as FactCheck.org confirmed — a 2003 version in his committee after it had been amended to match the federal law word for word. He later said he would have supported the federal version. He never argued that surviving infants should be killed, and the phrase “in favor of infanticide” goes beyond his words. But his record here is real, it is grave, and his own account of it was inaccurate.

- The Fort Hood shooting of 2009, carried out by a man who had corresponded with an al-Qaeda cleric and shouted “Allahu Akbar” as he killed thirteen people, was classified by the Defense Department as “workplace violence.” The victims were denied Purple Hearts until Congress changed the law in 2015.


## Why this is a moral question, not merely a tactical one


Some will say: the list may have errors, but the overall picture is right, so what is the harm? The harm is that God forbids it.


“Thou shalt not bear false witness against thy neighbour” (Exodus 20:16). The commandment does not carry an exception for neighbors whose politics we oppose. Among the seven things the Lord hates is “a false witness that speaketh lies” (Proverbs 6:16–19). And Scripture warns the hearer as much as the speaker: “The simple believeth every word: but the prudent man looketh well to his going” (Proverbs 14:15). “He that is first in his own cause seemeth just; but his neighbour cometh and searcheth him” (Proverbs 18:17).


Forwarding is a form of testifying. When a man clicks “share,” he lends his own name to the claims, just as this list borrowed Mike Gallagher’s. If the claims are false, he has borne false witness, even if he did not compose them.


The test I apply to every policy applies here, too. *Is it righteous?* Passing on charges one has not checked is not righteous; it is exactly what the ninth commandment forbids. *Is it loving?* It is not loving to the man accused, and it is not loving to the friends who receive it and repeat it, because it makes them unwitting witnesses to falsehood too. *Would I want it done to me?* No man wants a list of things he never said circulated under his name to two million people.


It also has a practical cost. When nine items are false, the reader who checks them will throw out the tenth, even if the tenth is true. The Born-Alive record is one of the most serious things you can say about any public official. Placing it in a list beside the Eid stamp and the Ramadan jewelry rumor buries it. False charges do not strengthen true ones; they discredit them.


## Why the concern remains plausible


None of this means that the people who share this list are imagining things. Their intuition has real roots, and it deserves to be stated accurately.


First, there was a pattern of reluctance to name the ideology behind Islamist terrorism. The Fort Hood classification is the clearest case, but it was part of a broader policy of avoiding the words “radical Islam,” which many Americans — including me — found evasive about a threat that announces its own religious motive.


Second, the administration showed a pattern of carelessness toward Christian institutions. The Georgetown backdrop was a small thing, but it fit a larger picture: the administration’s contraception mandate pressed Catholic employers, including the Little Sisters of the Poor, to violate their consciences, and that fight went to the Supreme Court. Meanwhile, the administration took evident care in its outreach to Muslim communities. When one faith receives courtesy, and another receives a mandate, people notice.


Third, the President’s own account of his faith left room for the doubts it later attracted, as the next section shows.


Fourth, his Middle East policy did favor the Muslim Brotherhood’s rise in Egypt in 2011, and the Brotherhood-aligned attacks on Coptic churches in 2013 drew from Washington a response many Christians judged far too weak.


Fifth, something did change on the ground. In fiscal year 2016, the United States admitted 38,901 Muslim refugees, the highest number on record and, for the first time in a decade, more than the number of Christian refugees. Of the roughly 12,600 Syrian refugees resettled that year, fewer than one percent were Christian, though Christians were about five percent of Syria’s population and among the most persecuted people in that war. Americans who noticed new faces in their cities were not imagining it.


These are real criticisms. They come from the record, without invention. A Christian who wants to persuade his neighbor should make them in exactly that way.


## Who was he, then?


The list was reaching for a question it never answered well: what did Barack Obama actually believe? The record answers it better than the rumors did.


**His childhood formation included Islam.** The register of the Catholic school he attended in Jakarta records his entry in January 1968 as “Barry Soetoro,” an Indonesian citizen, religion Islam. His teacher explained that the school registered children by the father’s religion, and his stepfather was Muslim. At the public school he attended afterward, he studied Islam and the Koran; one account says his mother objected, but he kept attending because he was interested.


**He carried an affection for Islam into adulthood.** In 2007, speaking with Nicholas Kristof of the *New York Times*, he recited the opening lines of the Arabic call to prayer from memory, with what Kristof called a first-rate accent, and described it as one of the prettiest sounds on Earth at sunset. The opening of that call is the Muslim profession of faith: there is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is his messenger. A man may admire the beauty of another religion’s music. But these are not words a Bible-believing Christian hears as beautiful content.


**By his own account, his Christianity was not the faith of the Bible.** In a 2004 interview with Cathleen Falsani of the *Chicago Sun-Times* — the longest he ever gave on his faith — he called himself a Christian, then said he believes there are many paths to the same place. Asked what sin is, he answered: being out of alignment with my values. On heaven, he described a reward for living well rather than a place secured by Christ. Christian leaders at the time observed that on sin, heaven, and the exclusiveness of the Gospel, he stood outside historic Christianity. Jesus said, “I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me” (John 14:6). Obama said there are many ways.


**He sat for twenty years under a pastor whose theology no Bible-believing Christian would have stayed under that long without sharing much of its worldview.** Black liberation theology reads the Gospel chiefly as a program of political and racial liberation; it borrows Marxist categories of oppressor and oppressed. He left the church in 2008, only after Wright’s sermons became a national controversy during his campaign.


I have found no evidence that he practiced Islam as an adult; a thesis that claims more than the evidence supports hands the critic an easy victory and takes the true points down with it. The rumor that he was secretly Muslim is unproven. But the suspicion behind it was not baseless. It was a crude name for something real.


## Where I stand


Barack Obama was not a Bible-believing Christian. By his own account, his faith held many paths to God and measured sin by his own values. He was registered as a Muslim as a child, studied the Koran, and carried into adulthood an evident affection for Islam that shaped how he spoke and governed: his reluctance to name Islamist ideology, his solicitude toward Muslim communities beside his administration’s pressure on Christian institutions, his refugee policy, and his course in Egypt.


The list has many false statements in its particulars. The suspicion behind it is not. The list was attributed to a man who did not write it; most of its particulars are false, and sharing it violates the commandment a righteous movement must keep above all others. We do not need invented charges. The true record is enough.


I ask those who support this campaign to adopt a simple discipline. Before you forward a charge against anyone — a president, a candidate, a neighbor — check it, using the tests below. If you cannot verify it, do not send it as fact. If you have already sent something false, correct it publicly. That is not weakness. It is the kind of integrity that makes the true things we say believable.


## A discipline for sharing


None of these takes more than a few minutes. Together they would have caught most of the list.


**Before you forward**


- **Look for a source, a date, and a place.** A charge that names none of them cannot be checked. Treat it as rumor until someone supplies them. “I am one of you” names no date, place, or witness.

- **Check the byline.** Search the named author together with the title. If the author never wrote it, the post has borrowed his credibility. The Gallagher attribution fails in one search.

- **Search the exact words.** Put one quotation in quotation marks and search it. If the words appear only in copies of the same post and never in a transcript, speech, or interview, be wary.

- **Test one item.** Pick the claim you can check most easily. If it fails, do not forward the list. A list with one fabrication has forfeited your trust in the rest.

- **Notice the warning signs.** An unknown author, a borrowed famous name, quotations with no context, heavy capitals and exclamation points, “forward this to everyone,” and a list so long and so perfect that every item points the same way.


**When you weigh the evidence**


- **Prefer what anyone can check.** A statute, a record, a transcript, or a video outranks anyone’s summary of it — a fact-checker’s, a pundit’s, or mine.

- **Treat a denial as testimony, not a verdict.** When the only answer to a charge is an official’s word, that is one interested party speaking. “Debunked” sometimes means only “denied.”

- **Hear both sides.** Search the claim with “fact check,” and search it in the outlets that first made it. Read the strongest version of each before you decide.

- **Ask what the rumor is reaching for.** A false charge is often a crude name for a real concern. Find the true version and make that case instead.

- **Treat firsthand reports as leads.** When someone says “I was there,” ask when, where, and who else saw it. Specifics can be checked; a story without them cannot.


**When you share**


- **Say how sure you are.** If you have not verified something but believe it matters, share it as a question — “Has anyone confirmed this?” — not as a fact.

- **Cut the false items.** If a post mixes true charges with false ones, do not forward it whole. Pass on the true ones with their sources. The true charges deserve better company.

- **Use AI as an assistant, not a judge.** Ask it for sources, click them yourself, and push back when your own knowledge disagrees. It reflects what it was trained on, and that record is not complete.

- **Correct yourself publicly.** If you shared something false, say so to the same people who received it. A man who corrects his own errors earns belief when he is right.

## Sources and their limits


This essay drew on fact-checking sites (PolitiFact, FactCheck.org, Snopes, TruthOrFiction), news reporting, and conservative and alternative outlets (Daniel Pipes, TheBlaze, the *Washington Times*, Baptist Press). I gave the most weight to primary evidence anyone can check: statutes, Postal Service records, school registers, interview transcripts, and public video. I labeled a claim “false” only where primary evidence contradicts it; where the only answer is an official denial, I treated that denial as the testimony of an interested party and said so. Fact-checkers choose which claims to check and how to frame them, and the information sphere of those years was curated in ways now well documented. Evidence that never reached the public record could change parts of this picture. Readers with firsthand knowledge are invited to send it.
