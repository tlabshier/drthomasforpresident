# 261114 - A Nation Grounded - A Router Failure

# Four-Hour Grounding of US Air Traffic - Addendum:


# One Router, One Remote Site, One Nation Grounded — What Saturday's Explanation Changes

**By Thomas Lee Abshier, ND** | *November 14, 2026*

*This is an addendum to my essay of November 13, "When the Sky Went Quiet: The FAA Ground Stop, an Aging System, and Who Should Pay to Fix It."*


---


## What We Learned Saturday

Yesterday I wrote that the "aging system" diagnosis was credible as a description of the FAA's condition, but that the specific cause of Friday's nationwide ground stop had not been established. I noted that Secretary Duffy and Secretary Mullin had given two different stories on Friday night: that old equipment had simply worn out under load, or that newer equipment connected to the old system had overwhelmed it. I said I believed the most likely explanation was an engineering failure at the boundary between old and new.

On Saturday morning, at a press conference at the Department of Transportation, Secretary Duffy gave a more specific account. According to his preliminary findings:


 	- The outage began during routine telecommunications maintenance at a remote FAA facility, where aging copper lines were being replaced with fiber-optic service.

 	- The work was being done by L3Harris Technologies, the contractor that has run the FAA's telecommunications network, called the FAA Telecommunications Infrastructure (FTI), for more than twenty years. Duffy said L3Harris "actually owns that network."

 	- During that work, one router began flooding the other routers in the air traffic control network with more than 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds. That disrupted communication with aircraft and some radar feeds.

 	- Investigators have not yet determined whether the cause was human error, an installation mistake, or a defect in the router itself. Duffy put the open question bluntly: was it incompetence on L3Harris's part, or a faulty router?

 	- Officials do not believe it was a cyberattack, though they have not entirely ruled one out. FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford said there was zero evidence of anything nefarious and zero evidence that AI was involved.


About a million travelers were stranded. Thousands more flights were canceled Saturday. As of this writing, L3Harris had not responded publicly.

## How This Changes My Assessment

Saturday's account confirms the judgment I reached Friday and sharpens it.

**I now stand on this conclusion: the failure did not happen because old equipment wore out. It happened because the network was built so that one misbehaving device at one remote site could disrupt the whole country. The age of the system matters, but the deeper fault is that the system has no way to contain a local failure.**

On Friday night we were told, in effect, that the old hardware went down. That was not the whole story. The old copper line was being retired, and the trigger was the act of replacing it. What failed was the modernization work itself, done on a live network, in a way that let one site's problem spread nationwide.

Any engineer who designs critical systems knows this principle. A well-built network assumes that some component will eventually fail or misbehave, and it is designed to isolate that component before the fault spreads. A router that suddenly floods its neighbors with a thousand connection attempts every few seconds should be cut off automatically within moments. That one router could take down the national airspace means that safeguard was missing, disabled, or overwhelmed. Old age alone does not explain that. A system designed without fault containment is fragile whether its routers are thirty years old or new.

This matters because it changes what the money has to buy. A new network will still have routers, and a new router that is misconfigured can flood a network as well as an old one can. If the $50 billion buys new boxes but not a network that contains failures, we will have paid for a newer system with the same weakness.

## This Was Not the First Warning

The record shows the danger was known, and known for a long time.


 	- **2009.** According to a Department of Transportation Inspector General review, a technician for Harris Corporation (L3Harris's predecessor) misconfigured a router while moving the network to fiber. That disrupted 129 air traffic facilities. That was the same kind of work and the same kind of failure, seventeen years ago.

 	- **November 2023.** An FAA-commissioned safety review called the FTI network obsolete. It warned that an unexpected failure could cut off individual facilities, or an entire regional control center, from the network.

 	- **2024, North Texas.** When fiber cables were cut and the Dallas–Fort Worth area lost radar, the FAA blamed Frontier Communications for data failures. It also said L3Harris failed to make sure the system backups actually worked.

 	- **September 2026, the Northeast.** One circuit failed, and the backup fiber had been cut. That produced eight hours of ground stops across the Northeast corridor.

 	- **September 2026, the GAO.** In its review of the FAA's new modernization program, the Government Accountability Office warned that without better cost and schedule planning, the agency could cause air traffic disruptions or prolong its reliance on badly outdated systems.


The last warning deserves attention. The GAO said the rush to modernize could itself cause disruptions. Two months later, modernization work caused the largest disruption of the year. This was not an unforeseeable accident. It was a hazard that had been written down in advance.

*"A prudent man foreseeth the evil, and hideth himself; but the simple pass on, and are punished."* **(Proverbs 22:3)**

## The Contract Problem

A structural problem underlies this. L3Harris has held the FTI contract since 2002. In 2023, the FAA chose Verizon to build its replacement, a new network called FAA Enterprise Network Services (FENS). In August 2026, the FAA moved the lead role to AT&T, with Verizon in support and a target completion date of December 2028. On Saturday, Secretary Duffy said that until he can build the new network, he is "stuck with L3Harris."

So the company maintaining and upgrading the live network is the one that lost the contract for the network that will replace it. I make no accusation against L3Harris. The company reported in May that it had passed the halfway mark in its telecommunications modernization work for the FAA, and it may well have done its work competently. But a contractor on its way out has weak commercial reasons to invest in excellence on a system it will soon hand over. That is a design flaw in how the transition was set up, and the people who designed the transition own it, not the contractor.

The transition itself is also the most dangerous period. Replacing copper with fiber across roughly 4,600 FAA facilities means thousands of changes like the one made Friday. Each is a chance for the same failure. A deadline that encourages speed over care multiplies those chances.

## A Caution Against Rushing to Blame

On Friday night the cause was described as old hardware and the remedy as congressional funding. By Saturday morning the focus had moved to the contractor and the possibility of incompetence. Both may be partly true. But I notice that each explanation came from officials whose own record is under scrutiny, and each points responsibility somewhere other than the agency that oversees the network. Those statements are testimony from interested parties, and the investigation is not finished.

Scripture sets a standard for judging a matter before the facts are in:

*"Thou shalt not raise a false report."* **(Exodus 23:1)**

*"At the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall the matter be established."* **(Deuteronomy 19:15)**

The router's logs, the work orders, the configuration records, and the testimony of the technicians on site are the witnesses. Until they have been examined by someone independent, no one, whether company, agency, or official, should be convicted in the press. If the evidence shows negligence, accountability should follow. If it shows a defective router, the manufacturer and the people who tested it are part of the story. If it shows a network design that let any single fault spread nationwide, the responsibility rests with the FAA and with the Congresses and administrations that left that design in place for decades.

## The Moral Test, Applied to the New Facts

**Is it righteous?** A steward is responsible not only for keeping a system running but for changing it without endangering the people who depend on it. Doing major work on a live national safety network without sound safeguards against a local fault spreading is not righteous stewardship, whoever did the work. Holding the responsible parties to account once the facts are established is righteous. So is restitution where negligence is proven:

*"If fire break out, and catch in thorns, so that the stacks of corn, or the standing corn, or the field, be consumed therewith; he that kindled the fire shall surely make restitution."* **(Exodus 22:6)**

**Is it loving?** Love for the million people stranded on Friday means making sure the next upgrade cannot do this to them again. It also means fairness to the technicians and the company: they deserve a real investigation, not a convenient scapegoat.

**Would I want it done to me?** If I were the technician at that remote site, I would want to be judged by what the evidence shows, not by the needs of a press conference. If I were a traveler sleeping on the floor of Terminal 4 at JFK, I would want to know that someone will answer for it, and that the system will be fixed so it does not happen again.

## Does This Change My Answer on Funding?

It strengthens the case for modernization and sharpens what modernization has to mean. I still hold that users should pay for most of the rebuild through the trust fund, financed up front with revenue bonds, with a stated share from general funds for defense and security. But I would now attach three conditions to any appropriation:


 	- **Pay for architecture, not just equipment.** The first dollars should go to fault containment: automatically isolating any device that floods the network, separating the network into segments so one region's fault stays in that region, and physically separate backup routes that are actually tested.

 	- **Pay for safe transition, not just speed.** Work on the live network should follow strict change-management discipline: staged rollouts, changes made in low-traffic windows, a tested way to undo each change immediately, and no change made at one site that can reach the whole network. If that discipline means the 2028 deadline slips, it should slip. A deadline is not worth another nationwide ground stop.

 	- **Write accountability into the contracts.** Every telecommunications contract, including the bridge arrangement with L3Harris and the new FENS contract with AT&T, should carry clear performance standards, penalties for outages caused by negligence, and the right to recover the costs of such outages. A contractor should not lose less money than his customers when he fails.


## What I Would Do as President, Updated

My earlier list still holds: an independent investigation with a public report, redundancy first, a fully costed plan, honest funding, investment in controllers, and responsibility instead of blame. Saturday's facts add these:


 	- **Ask the investigation the right question.** Not only "What went wrong with that router?" but "Why could one router at one remote site disrupt the national airspace, and why was that lesson not learned after the same kind of failure in 2009?" The Inspector General's 2009 findings and the FAA's 2023 safety review should be part of the record.

 	- **Pause risky changes to the live network until safeguards are verified.** Routine maintenance continues. Resume major cutovers from copper to fiber only after the FAA certifies that a fault at any one site will be contained to that site.

 	- **Make the transition plan public.** The public should know who is responsible for the network during the handover from L3Harris to AT&T, what safeguards apply, and what happens when something goes wrong in between.

 	- **Pursue restitution if negligence is proven.** If the investigation finds negligence, I would direct the Department of Transportation to recover the government's costs under the contract and to support the airlines and passengers who seek theirs.

 	- **No scapegoats.** I would not name a guilty party before the evidence did, and I would ask every official in my administration to hold the same standard.


## Why the Concern Remains Plausible

Saturday's explanation is more specific and more believable than Friday's, but several reasons for public unease remain, and they deserve to be taken seriously.


 	- **The story changed within twenty-four hours.** Friday's explanation pointed to old hardware and the need for funding. Saturday's pointed to a contractor's possible incompetence. Each was offered with confidence. A citizen who watched both press conferences has reason to wait for the final report before fully believing either one.

 	- **A network flood looks similar whether it is accidental or deliberate.** A device firing a thousand connection attempts every few seconds behaves much like a denial-of-service attack. A misbehaving router and a hostile one look alike at first. Only forensic examination of the device, its configuration history, and its logs can tell them apart. Officials say they "don't believe" this was an attack, which is not the same as having proven it was not.

 	- **The contractor has not been heard.** We have one party's preliminary account of what another party did. L3Harris has had no public opportunity to answer.

 	- **The warnings were on record.** When the same kind of failure happened in 2009, the FAA's own review in 2023 flagged it, and the GAO foreshadowed it in September; a reasonable person asks why it was allowed to happen again. That question is not paranoid. It is the question accountability exists to answer.


My conclusion: **Saturday's preliminary finding is the most credible explanation we have, and I accept it as the working account, while leaving it open to the independent investigation. But the lesson is larger than one router or one contractor. A national safety system must be built, maintained, and changed so that no single local mistake can stop the whole country. Until it is, a newer system is not necessarily safer.**

## Sources and Their Limits

This addendum was written the morning after the ground stop, based on reporting from a single press conference. The account of the cause rests on preliminary statements by Transportation Secretary Duffy and FAA Administrator Bedford. They are officials whose agency oversees the network and whose funding request is pending, so their statements are interested-party testimony. L3Harris had not responded when this was written, so the contractor's side is missing. The 2009 Inspector General finding and the 2023 FAA safety review are cited as reported by Yahoo News and CBS News; I have not read the original documents. I found no independent technical analysis of the failure, from mainstream or alternative sources, and the FAA has released no technical report. My statements about fault containment and change management describe standard network engineering practice. They are not findings about what safeguards this particular network had. I will revise this addendum when the investigation reports.
