# 261019 - Conspiracy Movies Compared to Real Life

# When the Paranoid Were Right:


## Concealment, Accountability, and the Duty of a Free People


*Dr. Thomas | 19 October 2026*


## The question behind the movies


WatchMojo recently released a countdown of ten conspiracy films whose plots seemed paranoid when they premiered but now look like previews of what followed: *The Parallax View*, *The Constant Gardener*, *Three Days of the Condor*, *Wag the Dog*, *Michael Clayton*, *Syriana*, *The Conversation*, *The China Syndrome*, *Enemy of the State*, and *Network*.


The video is entertainment, and it does what entertainment does. It mixes proven facts with suggestive timing, and it lets a coincidence stand beside a documented crime as though they were the same kind of thing. Still, the list raises a question every citizen must answer: when should a free people believe the powerful are hiding something, and what should a government do when they sometimes are?


I want to answer that question with proportion. Conspiracy theories may be true or false. The task isn’t to choose between paranoia and trust, but to build a republic where concealment is hard, discovery is protected, and wrongdoers are held to account whether they wear a uniform, a lab coat, or a corporate title.


## What the record actually shows


Strip away the assassins and car chases, and several of these films rest on events that are established beyond reasonable dispute.


**Government agencies surveilled and manipulated citizens.** The FBI’s COINTELPRO program, which ran from 1956 to 1971, infiltrated and disrupted domestic political groups. The CIA’s MKUltra program, from the early 1950s into the 1970s, conducted mind-control experiments, some on people who never consented and never knew. In 1973, the agency’s director ordered most MKUltra records destroyed. Both programs came to light through a burglary, journalists, and the Senate’s Church Committee investigations of 1975 and 1976, not through the agencies’ own candor.


**Drug trials were run on the vulnerable.** In 1996, during a meningitis epidemic in Kano, Nigeria, Pfizer tested its antibiotic Trovan on sick children. Families said they had not given informed consent. Pfizer denied wrongdoing and, after years of litigation, settled with the Kano state government in 2009.


**Companies did hide known hazards.** In 2005, DuPont agreed to pay the largest administrative penalty the EPA had imposed up to that time for failing to report what it knew about the health risks of PFOA, a chemical used to make Teflon. Syngenta, the maker of the herbicide atrazine, planned a campaign to discredit Tyrone Hayes, a Berkeley biologist whose research raised concerns about the chemical, according to its own internal documents.


**The government did collect Americans’ communications in bulk.** After the Patriot Act of 2001, the National Security Agency ran a warrantless wiretapping program, disclosed in 2005, and a bulk collection of Americans’ phone records, disclosed in 2013. In 2015, a federal appeals court held that the phone-records program went beyond what the law authorized.


**News did become a product.** *Network* was satire in 1976. Today, few would deny that outrage holds attention, and that attention is what the business sells.


## What the record does not show


Proportion requires naming what the video overstates.


*The China Syndrome* opened twelve days before the Three Mile Island accident, and the timing was uncanny. But equipment failure, poor instrument design, and operator confusion caused the accident. The federal commission that investigated it found serious training and communication failures. It did not find a murderous cover-up as the film imagined.


*Wag the Dog* opened a month before the Lewinsky scandal broke, and President Clinton did later order strikes abroad while under investigation and impeachment. Critics accused him of wagging the dog. An accusation is not proof.


*Three Days of the Condor* imagined a plan to seize Middle Eastern oil fields. Whether oil drove the 2003 invasion of Iraq is still argued by serious people; the stated reasons were weapons of mass destruction and terrorism, and the weapons were not found. As I write, the United States and Iran are in open conflict over the Strait of Hormuz, with tankers struck and fuel prices disrupted around the world. Oil is plainly at the center of the region’s strategic life. That does not by itself prove any particular decision was made for oil.


And *Network* is not really about a conspiracy at all. No one needs to meet in a dark room for news to drift toward entertainment. Incentives do that on their own. Many of the things we call conspiracies are simply many people following the same bad incentive in the open.


A citizen who treats every suspicion as fact becomes as easy to manipulate as one who trusts everything. *“The simple believeth every word: but the prudent man looketh well to his going”* **(Proverbs 14:15)**. And *“He that is first in his own cause seemeth just; but his neighbour cometh and searcheth him”* **(Proverbs 18:17**). The answer to manipulation is not suspicion of everyone. It is evidence, examined openly, by people free to examine it.


## The common thread is concealment


Every proven case on the list shares one feature. Someone with power knew something that would harm others and hid it because disclosure would cost him money, standing, or authority.


That is not a political observation first. It is a moral one. *“Every one that doeth evil hateth the light, neither cometh to the light, lest his deeds should be reproved”* **(John 3:20)**. Concealment is how wrongdoing protects itself. The films grasped this: in *The Parallax View* the conspiracy’s real strength is not its assassins but its ability to make the official story stick. In *Michael Clayton* the danger is not the weed killer alone but the buried memo.


So the public policy question is not how to stamp out conspiracy theories. It is how to make concealing harm difficult and discovering it safe.


## The moral test


I measure every policy by three questions, asked in order:


- **Is it righteous?** Does it accord with what God has established as right, before any question of convenience or popularity?

- **If so, is it loving?** Does it actually serve the neighbor’s good, not merely humor him?

- **Would I want it done to me?** Not by my momentary appetite, but by my conscience at its best: would I, standing in my neighbor’s place, consent to this?


Apply those questions to the cases above, and they are not hard.


Is it righteous to test a drug on a dying child whose parents do not understand what is being done? No. Would I want my own child treated so? No.


Is it righteous for a company to know that its product is poisoning people’s water and say nothing? No. Would I want to drink that water without being told? No.


Is it righteous for a government to read its citizens’ communications without a warrant, on the theory that it might someday find something? The Fourth Amendment already answered: the people are to be “secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects.” Would I want men who answer to no judge to read my own papers? No.


When the answer is that plain, the remaining question is only whether we will build the laws to match it.


## What I propose


These are positions I hold and will argue for. They are open to a better argument, and I invite one.


**1. Informed consent without exceptions.** Any clinical trial funded by the federal government, or whose data is submitted to the FDA for approval, should meet the same consent standard whether it is conducted in Ohio or in Nigeria. Data from a trial that fails that standard should not be accepted in support of approval. A company should gain nothing from a shortcut it would not dare take at home.


**2. Personal accountability for hiding known hazards.** Federal law already requires companies to report substantial evidence that a chemical poses a serious risk. Fines paid out of shareholders’ money have proven too small to change behavior. An executive who knowingly orders the suppression of hazard data should face personal criminal liability. A corporation cannot repent; a man can.


**3. Real protection for whistleblowers.** Someone on the inside exposed nearly every proven conspiracy on this list. Employees in private companies, federal agencies, and the intelligence community who report wrongdoing through lawful channels, including directly to the relevant committees of Congress, must be protected from firing, prosecution, and retaliation, and the protection must be enforced rather than promised.


**4. A warrant for Americans’ data, whoever holds it.** The government should not be able to buy from a data broker what it would need a warrant to seize. Surveillance authorities should carry firm expiration dates so that Congress must justify them again, in public, at regular intervals.


**5. Records that cannot quietly vanish.** Classification should expire on a fixed schedule unless an official justifies its extension in writing. Destroying government records that are under investigation, or are likely to become so, should be a crime, as the destruction of the MKUltra files should have been.


**6. War only by declaration.** *Wag the Dog* and *Three Days of the Condor* both turn on a president or an agency sending Americans into conflict without the people’s deliberate consent. The Constitution gives Congress the power to declare war. Restoring that requirement removes much of the temptation and suspicion that hang over every foreign strike.


**7. Remove the motive.** If oil is the prize that tempts nations to conceal and to fight, the surest remedy is to stop needing it. Abundant domestic energy, built on thorium molten-salt reactors, is the foundation of my platform because it changes what nations must fight over. A country that makes its own energy is harder to blackmail and less tempted to blackmail others.


**8. Trust the jury.** When government investigates itself, the last independent check is twelve citizens in a jury box. A juror has the right to refuse to convict when the law, or its application, is unjust. That right is a cornerstone of a free people. Jurors are the citizens least likely to be bought.


## A New ERA


My campaign rests on three words: Energy, Righteousness, and Accountability. This subject falls squarely under the last two.


Righteousness tells us what concealment is: a wrong done to the neighbor, hidden so that he cannot defend himself. Accountability is what a free people builds so that the wrong comes to light and the one who did it answers for it.


The films on WatchMojo’s list are fiction, and their villains are exaggerated. The lesson behind them is not. Power that can hide will be tempted to hide. The answer is not to distrust everyone, nor to trust everyone, but to build a government in which nothing that harms the people can stay hidden for long, and in which the man who hides it pays the price himself.


*“For nothing is secret, that shall not be made manifest; neither any thing hid, that shall not be known and come abroad”* **(Luke 8:17)**. A righteous republic does not wait for that day. It hastens it.
