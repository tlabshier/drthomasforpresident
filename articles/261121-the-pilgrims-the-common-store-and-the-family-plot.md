# 261121 - The Pilgrims - the Common Store and the Family Plot

# The Pilgrims' Common Store and the Family Plot


### What Bradford Learned at Plymouth:


### The Common Store, the Family Plot, and the Order God Set Among Men


**By Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | 21 November 2026**


---


## The Occasion


Every Thanksgiving, someone retells what happened at Plymouth between 1620 and 1627. This year, the author M. A. Rothman posted it on Facebook under the headline "The Pilgrims Tried Sharing Everything in Common — It Took Private Family Plots to End Their Hunger." His summary is short and mostly accurate. The colony's 1620 contract with its London investors set up a seven-year joint stock. Everything the colonists grew or caught went into a common store, and everyone drew from it alike. In the spring of 1623, still hungry, the colony's leaders assigned each family its own plot of corn. Governor William Bradford recorded that the change "had very good success." In 1627, the colonists bought out the investors and divided the land into twenty-acre shares.


Rothman's central sentence is the right one: *same people, same soil; the only thing that changed was who kept what they grew.*


I stand with that reading. Bradford was there; he made the decision, and he told us firsthand what he saw and what he concluded. But the story deserves to be told whole, because the parts usually left out make the lesson deeper. The common store was not the Pilgrims' idea. They resisted it before they sailed. And the harvest of 1623 came through two doors at once: men working their own ground, and a colony on its knees asking God for rain.


## What Bradford Actually Wrote


The decision came in the spring of 1623. Bradford wrote that "after much debate of things," he and the chief men "gave way that they should set corn every man for his own particular, and in that regard trust to themselves." Each family got a parcel of land in proportion to its size, and the boys and single young men were placed within families.


Then he reported the result:


>
"This had very good success; for it made all hands very industrious, so as much more corn was planted than otherwise would have been by any means the Governor or any other could use, and saved him a great deal of trouble, and gave far better content. The women now went willingly into the field, and took their little ones with them to set corn; which before would allege weakness and inability; whom to have compelled would have been thought great tyranny and oppression."


Notice what he says about compulsion. Under the common store, the governor could only get labor by command. When he did command it, the people felt it as tyranny. When each family worked for itself, no command was needed. The same women who had pleaded weakness now went out with their children to plant.


Bradford then stepped back and drew the lesson. He noted that this "common course" had been tried for several years among Godly and sober men, people of real faith and good conduct, and still it failed:


>
"...may well evince the vanity of that conceit of Plato's and other ancients applauded by some of later times; that the taking away of property and bringing in community into a commonwealth would make them happy and flourishing; as if they were wiser than God. For this community (so far as it was) was found to breed much confusion and discontent and retard much employment that would have been to their benefit and comfort. For the young men, that were most able and fit for labour and service, did repine that they should spend their time and strength to work for other men's wives and children without any recompense."


He went on. The strong man got no more food or clothing than the man who could not do a quarter of his work, and that was felt as injustice. The older men resented being ranked with the young. Husbands could not bear to have their wives commanded to cook and wash for other men, and the wives counted it a kind of slavery. And when everyone was to have alike and do alike, the respect that God had set between people within families and between generations wore away.


Then Bradford answered the objection that the fault lay in the people, not the system:


>
"Let none object this is men's corruption, and nothing to the course itself. I answer, seeing all men have this corruption in them, God in His wisdom saw another course fitter for them."


That sentence is the center of the whole story. Bradford did not argue that the colonists were unusually lazy. He argued that every man carries the same fallen nature, and that a system which works only if men are not fallen is a system built for a world that does not exist. God's order for human life (the family as the unit of provision, a man's labor tied to his own household, property as the fence that protects both) is not a concession to sin. It is wisdom fitted to men as they are.


## Where the Post Needs Correcting


I hold to the post's thesis, but several details need correcting, and each correction strengthens the argument.


**The common store was imposed on the Pilgrims, not chosen by them.** The original terms the Leiden congregation negotiated would have given the colonists two days a week to work for themselves, and would have let their houses and improved land stay with them when the seven years ended. Thomas Weston, the investors' agent, struck both terms before departure. The Leiden leaders refused to sign the altered conditions, and their objection was blunt: terms fit for thieves and slaves, not honest men. They sailed without signing, and the colony accepted the terms only in late 1621, after Robert Cushman arrived on the *Fortune* and persuaded them that without the investors' support the colony could not go on. So the story is not that a band of idealists tried socialism and learned better. It is that a band of Christians who already understood the principle had compulsion forced on them by a contract, suffered under it, and then changed it as soon as necessity gave them room.


**The 1623 plots were for use, not ownership.** Bradford is precise: the land was assigned "only for present use," with "no division for inheritance." The next year, at the colonists' request, each person was granted one acre to keep near the town. Freehold ownership came with the purchase from the investors and the 1627–28 division. This means Rothman's sentence is exactly right. What changed in 1623 was not the deed to the land. It was who kept the fruit of the labor. That alone was enough to transform the colony.


**The harvest came through prayer as well as work.** Rothman leaves out what Bradford put at the center of 1623. After the families planted their own corn, a drought set in from the third week of May until mid-July. The corn began to wither. The colony set apart "a solemn day of humiliation, to seek the Lord by humble and fervent prayer." Bradford reports that the morning was clear and hot without a cloud, and that toward evening gentle rain began and soaked the ground. Then, at harvest, *"instead of famine, now God gave them plenty, and the face of things was changed, to the rejoicing of the hearts of many, for which they blessed God."* Bradford adds that some of the abler and more industrious families had corn to spare and sell, and that no general want or famine had come upon the colony since. To Bradford, the family plot and the answered prayer were not competing explanations. Both were God's provision: one through the order He set for men's labor, the other through His direct mercy.


**The first hunger had other causes too.** The 1621 harvest was fair. The hardest shortage came in 1622, made worse when the ship *Fortune* arrived in late 1621 carrying thirty-five new settlers and almost no food. The colonists were also townsmen and weavers learning to farm and fish in a strange land. But the change in behavior Bradford describes (women who had pleaded weakness going willingly into the fields) is not explained by bad luck with ships. It is explained by the change in who kept the harvest.


**The temptation is not confined to one party.** Rothman closes by calling this a lesson "the left" refuses to learn. That is partly fair; many on the left do argue that the failures of collectivism were failures of execution, not of design. But I would put it in proportion. The Plymouth common store was a creature of profit-seeking investors, not socialist theorists. As I wrote earlier this week in "The Parable of Catching Hogs," the corn of compelled dependence feeds corporations and industries as well as the poor. The temptation to live from another man's labor without recompense is not partisan. It is human, which was exactly Bradford's point.


## The Moral Test


Everything on this site rests on three questions I ask of any public act. Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? And would I want it done to me, judged by the self committed to righteousness and not by my momentary appetite?


**The compelled common store fails the first question.** It severs a man's labor from his own household. Scripture places the duty of provision squarely on the family: *"But if any provide not for his own, and specially for those of his own house, he hath denied the faith, and is worse than an infidel"* **(1 Timothy 5:8)**. Bradford's young men were not refusing to work. They were refusing to work for "other men's wives and children" while their own duty went unmet. Their complaint was, at bottom, a complaint about a system that made obedience to that command impossible.


**It fails the second question.** Compelled love is not love. Paul told the Corinthians how to give: *"Every man according as he purposeth in his heart, so let him give; not grudgingly, or of necessity: for God loveth a cheerful giver"* **(2 Corinthians 9:7)**. The common store took the harvest by necessity, and it produced exactly what Paul warned against: grudging, resentment, and the slow death of mutual respect. The families who kept their own corn in 1623 were the same families who could then sell their surplus to those in need. Private provision did not end generosity. It made generosity possible.


**And it fails the third question.** I would not want to be the strong young man whose labor feeds every household but his own, or the wife commanded to wash another man's clothes, or the old man ranked as an equal with the boy he taught. Neither would anyone who thinks it through.


The early church in Jerusalem shared all things in common, and that is the strongest Christian objection to everything I have said. But the sharing in Acts was freely given. Peter told Ananias plainly, *"Whiles it remained, was it not thine own? **and after it was sold, was it not in thine own power?"* **(Acts 5:4)**. The difference between Jerusalem and Plymouth's first two years is the difference between a gift and a levy. One is love. The other is a contract enforced on people who had refused it.


## The Question Under the Question


Why does this story come back every November, and why does it provoke such argument? I think people are reaching for something larger than seventeenth-century agriculture. The question under the question is this: *Does God's order for human life still work, or was it a stage we were meant to outgrow?*


Every generation produces men who believe they can design something better than the family, the field, and the fence: a system in which need is met without anyone having to own, earn, or give. Bradford's phrase for that belief was "as if they were wiser than God." The Pilgrims did not reach their conclusion from theory. They reached it by hunger. That is why the story still matters. It is a small, well-documented case in which a Godly people, under a compelled system, did not flourish, and under a system that honored the family and the fruit of labor, did.


## The Strongest Objection, and My Answer


The strongest objection comes from serious historians of Plymouth, most prominently Jeremy Dupertuis Bangs. His case runs like this. The common store was a feature of a joint-stock company formed to earn a return for investors. That is an early form of capitalism, not socialism, so calling it socialism is an anachronism. The 1623 change was not a move to private property; it ended the yearly rotation of fields and gave families stable use of the same plots. Private freehold came only with the buyout of 1627–28. And Bradford wrote his famous passage about Plato in the 1640s, two decades after the fact. Bangs argues that Bradford was then reacting to the radical egalitarians of his own day, such as Samuel Gorton and the English Levellers, and used his memory of 1623 to make a point against them. On this reading, the "Pilgrims rejected socialism" story is a modern political sermon laid over an administrative adjustment.


I take this seriously, and much of it is right. The label "socialism" is anachronistic. The 1623 plots were use rights, not deeds. Bradford did write his reflection later, with the controversies of his own time in mind.


But none of that touches the lesson. Grant every point and three things remain.


First, Bradford's diagnosis does not depend on who did the compelling. The defect he named was not ideological. It was the severing of labor from reward and of provision from the family. That defect does the same damage whether the common store is required by London investors, a commune, or a modern state. If anything, the fact that the compulsion came from capitalists makes the lesson more universal, not less.


Second, Bradford's testimony about behavior is firsthand. He was the governor. He watched the women go into the fields. He saw how much more corn was planted. He is an interested witness, since the decision was his, and I grade his account accordingly. But he is the only eyewitness who described the change in detail, and nothing in the primary record contradicts him. Writing his reflection later does not make his memory of what he saw false; it gave him time to understand it.


Third, the objection that 1623 was "only" a change in use rights actually confirms the core claim. A change that left the land in common ownership but let each family keep what it grew was enough, by itself, to transform the colony's effort and its harvest. That is the point. The essential thing is not the deed. It is whether a man and his family eat the fruit of their own labor.


As for the voluntary communities that do share property (monasteries, the Hutterites, the early church), they make my case rather than refute it. They work where people have freely consented, share a deep faith, live at a small scale, and can leave. Plymouth's settlers had refused the arrangement and were bound to it by debt. Even in Israel's kibbutzim, once the world's best-known voluntary experiment in common life, most communities have since moved toward differential pay and private ownership of homes. Freely chosen common life can flourish for a time among the devoted. Compelled common life has never been shown to work among ordinary men.


## Why the Concern Remains Plausible


Some will say the Plymouth story is too small and too old to carry any weight in a modern argument. I think the concern it raises is more than plausible, for four reasons.


**First, the pattern repeats wherever it is tried.** Jamestown went through the same thing a decade earlier. Ralph Hamor, the colony's secretary, wrote in 1615 that when the men were fed from the common store, a man was glad if he could slip away from his work, and that thirty men had then produced less corn than three or four now raised for themselves. Sir Thomas Dale's remedy was the same as Bradford's: three acres to each man. Three and a half centuries later, in December 1978, eighteen farmers in the village of Xiaogang in Anhui Province, China, signed a secret agreement to divide their commune's land into family plots. They knew it could cost them their lives; the contract pledged that if any of them were imprisoned or executed, the others would raise their children to age eighteen. The next harvest was larger than the previous five combined, and their experiment became the model for China's rural reform. Like Plymouth in 1623, the land in Xiaogang remained under collective title. What changed was who kept what they grew.


**Second, ordinary experience confirms it.** Every parent knows that a chore assigned to everyone is done by no one. Every employer knows the difference between a worker paid for results and one paid for attendance. The intuitive sense that compelled equal sharing breeds idleness is not a prejudice; it is the accumulated observation of people who live and work together.


**Third, there is a money and power motive on the other side.** Every system that takes the harvest into a common store needs administrators to decide who gets what. Those administrators gain power from the arrangement and have every reason to defend it. In Plymouth, the beneficiaries were distant investors. In the twentieth century, they were party officials. The motive does not require bad men, only ordinary self-interest.


**Fourth, the doctrine and the system point the same way.** A philosophy that treats the state as the primary provider, programs that cut a family's benefits faster than its wages rise, and laws that grow in only one direction are the common store in modern clothes. When belief, structure, and incentive line up, the behavior Bradford saw (repining, resentment, and the quiet withdrawal of effort) is the predictable result.


## What I Would Do as President


The president's first tool is his voice. I would use it to tell the whole story of Plymouth in my Thanksgiving proclamation: the investors' contract the Pilgrims resisted, the hunger, the family plots, the drought, the day of prayer, the rain, and the harvest. Our children should know that the first American Thanksgivings came from people who learned both to work their own ground and to fall on their knees.


Beyond the proclamation, the principle Bradford learned guides my policy, as I have laid out in "The Parable of Catching Hogs":


 	- **Keep the fruit of labor with the laborer.** No family should lose more in benefits than it gains from another hour of work. Benefit cliffs should be smoothed so that work always pays.

 	- **Protect the family as the unit of provision.** Tax and benefit rules should never penalize marriage or make a father's earnings a liability to his household.

 	- **Leave room for willing generosity.** Charity belongs first to families, churches, and neighbors. I would use the office to call them back to that work, and I would protect their freedom to do it.

 	- **Keep promises already made.** Those who paid into existing programs arranged their lives around them. Righteousness keeps the promise while fixing the design.

 	- **Rebuild the field.** Plymouth's families could only feed themselves because there was ground to work. In our day, the ground is good work at good wages, and abundant, inexpensive energy is the foundation of the New ERA Vision precisely because it brings that work back.


## Conclusion


The headline version of this story says the Pilgrims tried socialism and it failed. The whole story says more. A Godly people had a compelled common store forced on them by contract. They resisted it, suffered under it, and replaced it as soon as they could with an arrangement that let each family keep what it grew. They worked harder, prayed through a drought, and received a harvest that ended their hunger for good. Bradford's verdict was not partisan. It was theological: all men carry the same corruption, and God in His wisdom saw another course fitter for them.


That course is still before us. It is the picture the prophet gave of a people at peace: *"But they shall sit every man under his vine and under his fig tree; and none shall make them afraid"* **(Micah 4:4)**. Every man under his own vine, working his own ground, free to give from his own harvest. That is what Plymouth found, and it is what I believe America must recover.

## Sources and Their Limits


This essay relies mainly on William Bradford's *Of Plymouth Plantation*, which I quote in modernized spelling from the public-domain text, and on the colony's land records. Bradford is the only detailed eyewitness to the 1623 decision and its effects, and he was the man who made the decision, so his account is interested testimony; I have treated it as firsthand evidence that nothing in the primary record contradicts, not as proof beyond question. His reflection on Plato was written years after the events, and scholars disagree about how much the controversies of the 1640s shaped it. The strongest dissenting reading comes from the Pilgrim historian Jeremy Dupertuis Bangs, whose work I have represented as fairly as I can; he himself notes that his link between Bradford's passage and the Levellers is his own argument, not a consensus. Popular retellings on both sides tend to simplify: conservative versions often skip the investor contract and the drought, and progressive rebuttals often treat the 1623 change as merely administrative and pass over Bradford's direct testimony about behavior. Where the record is thin (the exact terms of each family's use rights, the yields of individual years, and how much of the colony's recovery was owed to trade and new arrivals), no surviving source can settle the question. The parallels from Jamestown and Xiaogang rest on Ralph Hamor's 1615 account and on published reporting of the Xiaogang agreement; the Chinese figures come largely from official and state-media sources and should be read with that in mind.
