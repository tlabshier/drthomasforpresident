# Why Did Putin Go to War? Weighing Bill Browder's Answer Against the Others

**A Position Paper — Dr. Thomas for President** | *September 19, 2026*

**Source interview:** "The Real Reason Putin Invaded Ukraine — Sir Bill Browder," *TRIGGERnometry*, hosted by Konstantin Kisin and Francis Foster, released July 4, 2026. ## 1. Why the motive matters

A war's cause is not an academic question. What a nation believes about why its adversary fights determines what it thinks will end the fighting. If Vladimir Putin invaded Ukraine for a reason that a settlement can address, then negotiation is a duty. If he invaded for a reason no settlement can touch, then negotiation is a waste of time, and the only policy is to defeat him. By Western estimates, more than a million men have been killed or wounded between the two armies. A wrong diagnosis here is paid for in other people's sons.

Sir Bill Browder gives one diagnosis. Mr. Putin, in his February 2024 interview with Tucker Carlson, gave another. Scholars and diplomats have offered several more. This paper sets them side by side, asks what each one predicts, checks those predictions against the record, and then states a position.

The rule of procedure is an old one: "He that is first in his own cause seemeth just; but his neighbour cometh and searcheth him" (Proverbs 18:17). Each witness below sounds persuasive alone. The work is in the searching.
## 2. The moral ground on which this paper stands

Three principles govern what follows, and I state them first so the reader can test the conclusions against them.

**First, explanation is not justification.** To find that Russia had a real grievance is not to find that Russia had a right to invade. A man whose neighbor has wronged him has remedies; burning the neighbor's house is not among them. Nothing in this paper excuses the invasion of Ukraine, the killing of its people, or the breaking of Russia's own written pledge, in the Budapest Memorandum of 1994, to respect Ukraine's borders.[^1]

**Second, truth is kept in proportion.** A statement can be built entirely of true facts and still deceive if it presents one cause as the whole cause. Single-cause accounts of this war are attractive because each licenses a simple policy. "It is all Putin's theft" licenses war without end. "It is all NATO's fault" licenses walking away. Both are errors of proportion.

**Third, a nation's word is a moral act.** The man who may dwell in the Lord's tabernacle is the one who "sweareth to his own hurt, and changeth not" (Psalm 15:4). "Let your communication be, Yea, yea; Nay, nay" (Matthew 5:37). Promises made by the United States — to Russia, and to Ukraine — are to be judged by that standard, and so are Russia's.

The final test for any act in the public square is whether the neighbor affected by it would recognize it as love. That test applies to the Russian conscript, the Ukrainian family in Kharkiv, and the American taxpayer alike.
## 3. What Mr. Browder says, and what he has earned the right to say

Mr. Browder's testimony deserves respect before it receives scrutiny. He was the largest foreign portfolio investor in Russia. He exposed theft in Russian state companies, was expelled in 2005, and saw his investment companies seized and used to extract a fraudulent $230 million tax refund. The man he hired to investigate, Sergei Magnitsky, was arrested by the officials he had named, held for 358 days, denied treatment for pancreatitis and gallstones, and died in custody on November 16, 2009, at thirty-seven. Mr. Browder turned that death into the Magnitsky Act of 2012 and its successors in some thirty-five countries — laws that freeze the assets and bar the travel of individual officials who steal and torture. That is an honorable work. It punishes the guilty rather than a population.

The core of the account has been tested by a court. In 2019, the European Court of Human Rights ruled unanimously — the Russian judge included — that Russia failed to protect Magnitsky's life, that the medical care denied him led to his death, that he was ill-treated by guards shortly before dying, and that his posthumous conviction was unfair. The same court did not accept every part of Mr. Browder's telling: it found the original arrest rested on a reasonable suspicion of tax evasion under an inquiry opened before Magnitsky made his accusations.[^2] A careful reader holds both findings.

On that foundation, Mr. Browder builds a claim about the war. In his words from the interview, Putin "stole so much money that he's afraid of his own people," and the remedy is "Machiavelli 101: create a foreign enemy, start a war." He adds three corollaries: that Putin's stated grievances about NATO, Ukrainian "Nazis," and historic Russian lands are narratives manufactured "for public consumption"; that Putin "needs the war to stay in power" and therefore will never negotiate its end; and that the right policy is to arm Ukraine until Putin is finished, because "nobody should negotiate with Putin."

Here a distinction must be drawn. On Russian corruption, Mr. Browder is a firsthand witness. On Putin's inner motive he is not; he says himself that he has never met the man. His theory of the war is an inference, and an inference is weighed by whether its predictions come true.
## 4. Testing the Browder postulate

The theory that leaders start wars abroad to escape trouble at home is an established idea in political science, called diversionary war. Russia supplies its most quoted example: the remark attributed to Interior Minister Plehve before the 1904 war with Japan, that Russia needed a small victorious war to hold back revolution. The theory is respectable. The question is whether it fits this case. It makes four predictions.

**Prediction one: the stated grievances are inventions for the public.** The record contradicts this. Russian objection to Ukraine in NATO was pressed in private, by the whole Russian establishment, long before Putin had any war to justify. In February 2008 the American ambassador in Moscow, William Burns — later Director of the CIA — cabled Washington under the title "Nyet Means Nyet," and wrote to Secretary Rice that Ukrainian entry into NATO was "the brightest of all redlines for the Russian elite (not just Putin)."[^3] Putin made the complaint openly at Munich in 2007. In December 2021, Russia delivered draft treaties demanding a written end to NATO enlargement, and NATO's own Secretary General later told the European Parliament that Putin had presented that demand as a condition for not invading.[^4] A grievance stated consistently for fifteen years in confidential channels is not a story made up for television. One may judge the grievance unreasonable. One may not call it fake.

**Prediction two: the timing follows domestic danger.** Here the evidence is mixed, and partly favors Mr. Browder. Putin's approval in the independent Levada polls sank to the low sixties after the protests of 2011–2013, then rose to 87 percent following the seizure of Crimea.[^5] The rally was real, and Putin surely noticed. But the trigger in 2014 was an event he did not schedule: the fall of the Yanukovych government in Kyiv on February 22, 2014. Russian forces moved in Crimea within the week. In February 2022, the fit is weaker. The opposition had already been broken — Alexei Navalny imprisoned, Memorial ordered closed, the constitution amended to let Putin stay until 2036 — and his approval stood near seventy percent.[^5] There was discontent after the pandemic, but no visible match near the powder.

**Prediction three: a war chosen for popularity will be short and cheap.** Crimea fits. The 2022 invasion was planned as a campaign of days, which also fits. But when the plan failed, a ruler interested only in his ratings had an exit: in the Istanbul talks of March and April 2022, the two sides drafted terms built on Ukrainian neutrality, which state television could have sold as victory.[^6] Instead the war went on, and in September 2022 Putin ordered a mobilization that cost him the first significant drop in approval since the invasion.[^5] A man governed by polls does not do that.

**Prediction four: no negotiation is possible, on any terms.** This is the corollary with the gravest consequences, and it is the hardest to test, because it is framed so that no evidence can count against it: if Russia refuses terms, the theory is confirmed; if Russia offers terms, the offer is called a trick. What can be said is that Russia has negotiated throughout — the Istanbul draft, the grain corridor, repeated prisoner exchanges, the truces of April and May 2026 — and that its demands have been consistent rather than arbitrary: neutrality, limits on Ukraine's forces, and territory. A ruler who wanted only a war would have no need of consistent demands.

Several of Mr. Browder's supporting figures also fail the test of proportion. He says 141 million Russians live in "destitute poverty"; Russia's official poverty rate before the war was roughly one in nine, and even a skeptic of official figures cannot stretch that to the whole nation. He equates a trillion dollars of capital flight with a trillion dollars stolen from the government; economists do estimate Russian offshore wealth on that order,[^7] but capital flight includes lawful money fleeing an unsafe country as well as loot. He says Russia and Ukraine had "no beef" before 2014, which passes over the Orange Revolution of 2004, the gas cutoffs of 2006 and 2009, and the Bucharest declaration of 2008. And he pronounces Putin "a total sociopath," a diagnosis no one outside a consulting room is equipped to make and on which no nation should rest a policy of war.

**What survives.** A good deal. The kleptocracy is real; the Magnitsky case, the Panama Papers, and the Black Sea palace establish it. The rally effect is real. And Mr. Browder's sharpest observation stands: a ruler who has stolen and killed cannot safely retire, so for him every threat to power is a threat to life. Research on dictatorships finds that personal rulers, surrounded by men who fear them, start more wars and receive worse information than rulers who answer to a party or a council.[^8] That is where corruption belongs in the explanation of this war — not as the motive, but as the condition that removed every brake. No one in Moscow could tell Putin that Kyiv would not fall in three days.
## 5. The NATO explanation, and the promise

The second account is the one Putin gave Mr. Carlson, and the one John Mearsheimer and others argued in the West.[^9] Putin's words were that Russia was promised "not an inch to the East," that the West then said the promise was not on paper, and that five waves of enlargement followed, ending with the 2008 declaration opening NATO's door to Ukraine.[^10]

Accuracy requires care about what was promised, to whom, and about whom.

In February 1990, Secretary of State James Baker told Mikhail Gorbachev that if a united Germany remained in NATO, the alliance's jurisdiction would not move "one inch" eastward. Declassified records show similar assurances from German, British, and French leaders.[^11] The assurance was real. It was also verbal, given during the bargaining over Germany, never written into the treaty that settled the matter, and given to the Soviet Union, a state that ceased to exist twenty-two months later. It said nothing about Ukraine, which was then a Soviet republic. Therefore, it is not correct to say the United States broke a promise not to enroll Ukraine in NATO; no such promise was made. What is correct is that Russian leaders of every stripe, Yeltsin included, understood the spirit of 1990 to have been violated, and that Americans as sober as George Kennan warned at the time that enlargement would be "the most fateful error of American policy in the entire post-Cold War era."[^12] By the Psalm 15 standard, a word given is a word given whether or not the lawyers wrote it down. Against that stands the consent of the Poles, Czechs, and Balts, who asked to join because their experience of Moscow's rule gave them cause, and whose wishes deserve weight equal to Russia's.

The clearer American fault is Bucharest. In April 2008, over German and French objection, President Bush obtained a declaration that Ukraine and Georgia "will become members of NATO," with no date, no plan, and no intention among the allies of fighting for either country.[^13] That was a promise to Ukraine we did not mean to keep, joined to a provocation of Russia we did not mean to back. It gave Ukraine the danger of membership without its protection for fourteen years. That is a wrong done to Ukraine as much as to Russia.

So the grievance is genuine. Is it the cause? Four facts say it cannot be the whole cause.

- **Membership was not near.** In February 2022, Ukraine was no closer to NATO than in 2008. Germany and France still opposed it, and Chancellor Scholz told Putin directly it was not on the agenda.

- **The 2014 crisis was not about NATO.** Ukraine was by its own law a non-aligned state from 2010 until December 2014, and only about one Ukrainian in five favored joining the alliance. The crisis began over a trade and association agreement with the European Union. Russia objected to Ukraine's westward turn.

- **Finland.** When the invasion drove Finland and Sweden into NATO, more than doubling the alliance's land border with Russia, Putin said their membership posed no direct threat. A man for whom NATO's approach was intolerable in itself would not shrug at eight hundred miles of new frontier.

- **The demands exceed neutrality.** Russia has annexed four provinces and Crimea by constitutional amendment. At the talks of September 5, 2026, Putin again required Ukrainian withdrawal from all four provinces, including land Russia does not hold, along with "demilitarization and denazification" and neutrality.[^14] Neutrality is one of the three demands.

## 6. The other explanations

**The national-historical claim.** This is the motive Putin himself puts first. Given an open microphone by Mr. Carlson, he spent the opening half hour not on NATO but on history from the ninth century forward. His July 2021 essay argues that Russians and Ukrainians are one people. His address of February 21, 2022, three days before the invasion, called modern Ukraine a creation of Bolshevik Russia. In June 2022, he compared himself to Peter the Great, who, in his telling, did not seize land but returned it. When a man explains himself the same way in writing, in a speech to his nation, and to a friendly foreign interviewer, you should take the explanation seriously. It also accounts for what the NATO theory cannot: the annexations, the denial of Ukrainian nationhood, and the indifference to Finland.

**Fear of the example.** Michael McFaul and Robert Person argue that what Putin fears is not NATO's tanks but a free, Slavic, Orthodox, prospering Ukraine next door, whose success would put a question to every Russian.[^15] This is the better form of Mr. Browder's thesis. It shares his premise that the regime's survival is the motive, but it explains why the target had to be Ukraine and why the Maidan uprisings of 2004 and 2014 were each followed by Russian escalation.

**The Donbas and the Minsk accords.** Some fourteen thousand people died in eastern Ukraine between 2014 and 2022. The Minsk agreements meant to settle it were never carried out, and Chancellor Merkel's later remark that Minsk had bought Ukraine time is cited by Moscow as proof of Western bad faith. This is a real strand, though Russia armed and directed the separatist side from the beginning.

**Material and strategic interest.** The naval base at Sevastopol, the fresh-water canal that Ukraine closed to Crimea in 2014, the land corridor to the peninsula, and the industry of the east are all things a state might fight for. They help explain the shape of the campaign more than the decision to launch it.

**Miscalculation and the open window.** Putin spent the pandemic in unusual isolation, reading history and seeing few advisers. He had watched the American withdrawal from Kabul in August 2021, Europe's dependence on Russian gas, and a new German government. His intelligence services told him Ukraine would fold. This explains the timing better than any domestic danger does.

My judgment is that Mr. Browder is right about the regime's character and wrong about the cause of the war. Putin went to war because he is convinced that Ukraine belongs within Russia's orbit and was slipping out of it for good; the prospect of NATO was the military form of that loss, and American carelessness at Bucharest made the fear concrete. He went when he did because he thought the West weak and Ukraine brittle. And he was able to make so large a mistake because a government built on theft and fear has no one left in it who will tell the ruler the truth.

This matters for policy because Mr. Browder's conclusion — never negotiate — depends entirely on his being right about motive. If the grievances are even partly real, then some part of this war is open to settlement, and refusing to seek it is a decision to spend lives that might be spared.
## 8. Positions

- **Tell the whole truth about the war's origins.** The United States should say plainly that Russia's invasion was an unjust aggression and a breach of its written word, and say just as plainly that the Bucharest declaration of 2008 was a reckless promise that America never intended to honor. Confessing our own fault costs us nothing we should want to keep, and it is the precondition of being believed.

- **Test the motive rather than presume it.** The competing theories predict different answers to a concrete offer. Put on the table an armistice on the line of contact, binding security guarantees for Ukraine without NATO membership, and an end to strikes on each other's territory. If Mr. Browder is right, Moscow will refuse any terms whatever, and the case for pressure will then rest on evidence rather than diagnosis. Russia's present position, which demands land it has not taken and a change of government in Kyiv, already shows that NATO was never the whole of it, and American negotiators should say so at the table.

- **Peace without ratifying conquest.** An armistice stops the killing; it need not bless the theft. From 1940 to 1991, the United States declined to recognize the Soviet annexation of the Baltic states while living at peace with the Soviet Union. The same course is available here: a settled line, and no legal recognition of territory taken by force.

- **Keep the Magnitsky instrument, and turn it on ourselves.** Personal sanctions against officials who steal and torture are the most just tool in this whole affair, because they fall on the guilty and not on pensioners and conscripts. But Mr. Browder's most uncomfortable testimony concerns the West: the stolen money lives in London, on the Riviera, and in American shell companies, and he describes Britain in those years as "bought and paid for." First cast the beam out of thine own eye (Matthew 7:5). Beneficial-ownership disclosure and enforcement against the lawyers, bankers, and agents who launder such money belong at the head of the list.

- **No security promise without the Senate.** The Constitution gives the President treaty power with the advice and consent of two-thirds of the Senate (Article II, Section 2). A summit communiqué declaring that a nation "will become" a treaty ally commits the honor of the United States without that consent. Future declarations of this kind should be made only after the Senate has voted, so that America's yea means yea.

- **Ukraine decides for Ukraine.** No settlement should be imposed over the heads of the people who have done the dying. America's part is to be an honest broker, to stop supplying a stalemate as though it were a strategy, and to support elections so the nation being defended governs itself.

- **No policy built on a diagnosis.** Calling an adversary a sociopath, a Hitler, or a madman ends thought. It may even be true, and it still tells us nothing about what he will accept. "He that answereth a matter before he heareth it, it is folly and shame unto him" (Proverbs 18:13).

These positions are taken on the evidence set out above. If Russia is offered real neutrality with real guarantees and still refuses every form of peace, I will owe Mr. Browder the acknowledgment that he saw more clearly than I did. I hold the position open to that argument and to any better one.
