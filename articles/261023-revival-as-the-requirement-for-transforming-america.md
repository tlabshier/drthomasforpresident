# 261023 - Revival as the Requirement for Transforming America

# How Does a Revival Come?


*Dr. Thomas — 23 October 2026*


## The Question Behind the Question


Nearly everyone who loves this country and knows its history has said, in some form, that only a revival can save America. Billy Graham said it for sixty years. His grandson Will Graham is saying it now, carrying his Declaration of Dependence Tour through the thirteen original colonies. Cliff Kincaid, reporting on the tour’s stop in Frederick, Maryland, asks in his title whether a revival can save America, and answers by describing how badly the churches need one. Pulpits have stopped preaching the commandments. Men who call themselves Christian run for high office while advocating what Scripture plainly forbids. Congregations sponsor what their fathers would have called sin.


I agree with the diagnosis, and I have made the claim that a revival can save America the center of this campaign. But the claim is not where the work is. The real question is the one almost no one answers: how does a revival actually come? If we cannot answer it, then “only a revival can save us” becomes a sigh rather than a plan, a way of saying the situation is hopeless and we are waiting for a miracle.


My position is this. A revival is God’s work, and no man can manufacture one. But God has told us the conditions under which He sends it, and history shows those conditions being met again and again before every great awakening. We cannot command the fire. We can lay the wood, and we are commanded to do so.


## What a Revival Is


The word is used loosely, so it needs a definition. A revival is not a well-attended meeting, a stirring speech, or a season of good feeling. It is the reviving of something that once lived and has grown cold. The Psalmist prays, *“Wilt thou not revive us again: that thy people may rejoice in thee?”* **(Psalm 85:6)**. The “us” is God’s own people. Revival begins in the church before it reaches the world.


Its marks are consistent across centuries:


- **Conviction of sin.** People see themselves as God sees them, and it breaks them. At Pentecost, when Peter preached, the hearers *“were pricked in their heart”* and asked what they must do **(Acts 2:37)**.

- **Repentance that changes conduct.** Not regret, but a turning: debts repaid, wrongs confessed, habits abandoned.

- **A hunger for God’s Word and for prayer.** People who once endured an hour of church now cannot get enough.

- **Fruit in the public square.** What begins in hearts reaches the tavern, the courthouse, the marketplace, and eventually the law.


That last mark matters for anyone who cares about the nation. Revival is the root; societal reformation is the fruit. Every lasting moral reform I know of in Anglo-American history grew out of a prior awakening of hearts. You cannot get fruit from a dead tree.


## What Scripture Says Brings It


The clearest promise is one most Christians can quote:

>
*“If my people, which are called by my name, shall humble themselves, and pray, and seek my face, and turn from their wicked ways; then will I hear from heaven, and will forgive their sin, and will heal their land.”* **(2 Chronicles 7:14)**


Notice who is addressed: *my people*. Not the pagans, not the government, not the culture. The healing of the land depends on the church’s repentance. Peter says the same thing: *“judgment must begin at the house of God”* **(1 Peter 4:17)**. This is why Kincaid’s diagnosis matters. If the house of God is preaching another gospel, the first condition of revival is not met, and no amount of political organizing will replace it.


The Bible also shows the pattern in action. Under King Josiah, the priests found the Book of the Law, which had been lost for a generation in the Temple itself. When they read it to the king, he tore his clothes. He gathered the whole nation, read the Book aloud to them, and led them in renewing the covenant, and only then did he tear down the idols** (2 Kings 22–23)**. Under Nehemiah, Ezra read the Law from morning until midday, and the priests *“gave the sense, and caused them to understand the reading.”* The people wept **(Nehemiah 8:8–9)**. In both cases the sequence is the same: the Word recovered, the Word understood, hearts broken, conduct changed, society reformed.


Hosea gives the farmer’s version: *“Break up your fallow ground: for it is time to seek the LORD, till he come and rain righteousness upon you”*** (Hosea 10:12)**. The farmer cannot make rain. But he can plow, and the rain does nothing for a field that was never plowed.


## What History Shows


The same pattern appears in every great awakening I have studied.


**The Great Awakening (1730s–1740s).** Jonathan Edwards called what happened in Northampton “a surprising work of God,” and it was. But it came to a town where a faithful pastor had been preaching the plain doctrines of sin, judgment, and grace, and where people had begun to pray. George Whitefield then carried the same message across the colonies, preaching in open fields to crowds no building could hold. The awakening shaped the generation that went on to declare independence.


**The Second Great Awakening (1790s–1840s).** It began with the Cane Ridge camp meeting in Kentucky in 1801 and spread through the preaching of Charles Finney, who argued that revival comes through the right use of the means God has given: prayer, plain preaching, and calling for a decision. It gave rise to the abolition movement, the temperance movement, Sunday schools, and a wave of missionary and benevolent societies. Its moral energy played a major role in what eventually ended slavery.


**The Prayer Meeting Revival (1857–1858).** In September 1857, a lay missionary named Jeremiah Lanphier began a noon prayer meeting for businessmen in lower Manhattan. Six men came the first week. Within months, thousands gathered daily in cities across the country to pray, with no famous preacher at the center. Conversions in the next two years are commonly estimated at hundreds of thousands, possibly as high as a million.


**Wales (1904–1905).** A young former coal miner named Evan Roberts began asking congregations to confess every known sin, put away every doubtful habit, obey the Spirit promptly, and confess Christ publicly. Some hundred thousand people came to faith in about a year. Contemporary reports tell of taverns emptying, old debts being repaid, and courts with little to do.


**The Hebrides (1949).** On the Isle of Lewis, two elderly sisters, Peggy and Christine Smith, one of them blind, prayed for months for their parish. Their minister and a handful of men prayed with them in a barn. When the preacher Duncan Campbell arrived, the island was ready, and the awakening spread across the islands.


**Asbury (2023).** An ordinary chapel service at a small Kentucky college did not end. Students stayed to pray and confess, and it continued around the clock for about two weeks, drawing visitors from across the country. Whether it becomes more than a moment depends on what follows, but its beginning fits the pattern.


The elements repeat:


- **Desperation.** Someone saw his town’s or nation’s spiritual condition and could not bear it.

- **Persistent, united prayer**, often by ordinary people, often for a long time before anything visible happened.

- **The Word preached plainly**, including sin, judgment, repentance, and the cross. Not a softened gospel. Will Graham put it simply in Frederick: the church did not die for your sins; Jesus Christ did.

- **Holiness beginning with the church.** Confession and restitution among believers came first.

- **Ordinary people as carriers.** Revivals are rarely run by institutions. They spread through laymen, students, farmers, and grandmothers.

- **Public fruit.** Changed lives produced changed communities, and eventually changed laws.


## Is Revival Sovereign or Conditional?


Christians have argued for three centuries over whether revival is purely God’s sovereign gift (Edwards’s “surprising work”) or a result of meeting conditions (Finney’s “right use of means”). I hold that both are true, and that the argument itself is a distraction. Paul planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the increase **(1 Corinthians 3:6)**. No one can schedule a revival, and no campaign can produce one. But God has never sent one to a people who were not seeking it, and He has commanded us to seek. God’s sovereignty is not an excuse for idleness. It is the reason idleness is disobedience.


## Where Evidence and Argument Fit


I have spent most of my life working on a physical theory, Conscious Point Physics, in which God is not an embarrassment to the equations but the ground they stand on. I believe that work matters, and I believe the false claim that “science has disproved God” is one of the strongest obstacles keeping modern people from taking Him seriously. Paul speaks of *“casting down imaginations, and every high thing that exalteth itself against the knowledge of God”* **(2 Corinthians 10:5)**. Removing a false objection is real work, and a serious, testable framework that grounds physics in God gives the honest skeptic permission to reconsider.


But I want to be clear about what argument does and does not do. Scripture says God is already “clearly seen” in what He has made, and that men suppress what they know **(Romans 1:18–20)**. Jesus said that those who will not hear Moses and the prophets will not be persuaded *“though one rose from the dead”* **(Luke 16:31)**. John the Baptist, the voice crying in the wilderness, drew the whole nation to the Jordan, and yet *“John did no miracle: but all things that John spake of this man were true”* **(John 10:41)**. Evidence can plow the ground. It cannot make the rain fall. The barrier for most people is not a lack of information about God, but a refusal to be governed by Him. That is a matter of the heart, and only conviction of sin reaches the heart.


So I place the scientific work where I believe it belongs: as one way to break up fallow ground, especially among people taught that faith is for the uneducated. It is not a substitute for repentance, and no discovery, however striking, will produce revival by itself.


## Why the Church Must Go First


If revival begins with God’s people, then Christians today must ask not “what is wrong with America?” but “what is wrong with us?” Kincaid lists the failures: pulpits silent on the commandments, churches endorsing what God calls sin, candidates claiming Christ while opposing His Word. The Lord warned of exactly this: *“Not every one that saith unto me, Lord, Lord, shall enter into the kingdom of heaven; but he that doeth the will of my Father”* **(Matthew 7:21)**. Many will say they did great works in His name, and He will answer, *“I never knew you.”*


A church in that condition cannot call the nation to repentance, because it has not repented itself. The order cannot be reversed. We will not see righteousness in Washington until we see it in the pews, and we will not see it in the pews until it begins in the person reading this.


## What Then Shall We Do?


Here is what I believe each of us can do, starting now.


**Repent personally and specifically.** Not “forgive me for my sins” in general, but naming them, making restitution where possible, and putting away what displeases God. Every revival on record began with individuals doing this.


**Pray in concert.** Find two or three others and meet regularly and persistently to pray for revival, without waiting for anything visible to happen. Lanphier began with six men. The Smith sisters began with two.


**Recover the Book.** Read the Scriptures, aloud and together, in homes and churches. Like Josiah’s generation, many Christians have lost the Book in the very house of God. They know about it, but they do not know it.


**Insist on the whole counsel of God from the pulpit.** Support pastors who preach the commandments, sin, judgment, repentance, and grace. If your church will not preach them, seek one that will.


**Live the fruit publicly.** Honesty in business, faithfulness in marriage, care for the poor and the unborn, courage in speaking truth with love. A watching world believes what it sees before it believes what it hears.


**Then carry it into public life.** Righteous law follows a righteous people. It can protect the innocent and restrain evil, but it cannot make anyone holy. That is why I treat this campaign as a witness: a call to the nation to return to God, with a platform describing what a people who had returned would want their government to do.


## Conclusion


Can a revival save America? Yes. It has done so before, more than once, when the nation was as divided and as corrupt as it is now. But a revival does not come because we long for it. It comes when God’s people, broken over their own sin and the condition of their land, humble themselves, pray, seek His face, and turn from their wicked ways. That is the condition He has set, and He has promised to answer it.


I do not know whether this generation will meet it. I know that every great awakening began with a few people who decided not to wait for someone else to go first. I invite you to be one of them.
