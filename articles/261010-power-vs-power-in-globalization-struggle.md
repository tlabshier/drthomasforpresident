# 261010 - Power vs Power in Globalization Struggle

# Power Versus Power: The End of Globalization and What a Free People Should Want


*A position paper responding to Andrei Jikh’s video on the World Economic Forum and the new world order*


## The Moral Question First


Every argument about trade, currency, and finance is finally an argument about how people treat one another. Before weighing any policy, this paper asks the question it asks of every public act: would a neighbor on the receiving end of this policy feel loved by it? Would the machinist in Ohio whose plant moved overseas feel loved? Would the retiree whose savings earn a fraction of a percent while others profit from them? Would the family whose index fund votes on matters it never heard of? And, because the neighbor does not stop at the border, would the factory worker in Asia who escaped poverty through trade?


Those questions do not yield slogans. They yield judgments of proportion. What follows is an attempt to make such judgments about the claims in a widely viewed video and to take positions that I hold open to any better argument.


## What the Video Claims


Andrei Jikh, a popular finance commentator, argues that the order that governed the world economy for about eighty years is ending. As he tells it, after the Second World War the major powers settled into roles. China became the world’s factory. Japan kept interest rates near zero and served as the lender that kept the system liquid. Europe became the great consumer, trading military strength for stability and social welfare. The United States enforced the arrangement through the dollar as reserve currency, the petrodollar, and military power. Borrowing a phrase from the commentator Simon Dixon, he calls this a “proof of weapons” network: nations that played by the rules gained access to capital and protection, and nations that broke them faced sanctions or war.


For Americans, he argues, the benefits were lopsided. Investors did very well. Working people saw factories and supply chains leave, the middle class hollowed out, and the nation become dependent on others for the things it no longer made. The system also required something like permanent war to enforce.


Now, he says, rival nations have built their own arrangements and are strong enough that the old system cannot be enforced without a world war nobody wants. He points to the January 2026 meeting of the World Economic Forum in Davos, where U.S. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick told the assembled leaders that globalization had failed the West and the United States and had left American workers behind.


Jikh then describes four groups contending for what comes next:


- **The financial powers**, which he divides into the great asset managers (BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street), the banks and central banks, and the crypto industry. The asset managers want tokenized, fractional assets moving instantly on digital rails they control. The banks want to avoid being cut out and are turning to central bank digital currencies. The crypto firms want to compete with banks, especially by paying interest-like rewards on dollar-backed tokens.

- **The sovereigns**, national leaders such as Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping, value control of energy, food, industry, and security above market returns and resist foreign capital because it would cost them leverage.

- **The technologists**, who care about efficiency and scale, not ideology, will build digital IDs, social credit systems, automation, and surveillance tools for whichever power pays and grants data access.

- **The military-industrial complex**, the enforcement layer, thrives on instability and he regards it as ultimately subordinate to financial powers rather than to elected government.


He closes by stressing that this is not good versus evil but power versus power, and then offers, explicitly as a personal theory he cannot prove, the view that the outcomes have largely been negotiated in advance and that the geopolitical drama we watch is theater.


## What Is Right in the Picture


This is a fair compression of a complicated reality.


It is true that the United States offshored much of its manufacturing capacity, that communities built around it suffered badly, and that the gains flowed disproportionately to owners of capital. The nation became dependent on foreign suppliers for medicine, semiconductors, and critical materials, a dependency the pandemic exposed. The Commerce Secretary said what Jikh reports; Lutnick told the Davos audience that nations should not offshore their medicine, semiconductors, or industrial base.


Proxy voting is concentrated to a remarkable degree. Jikh’s figures for BlackRock were close to the company’s own. As of mid-2026, BlackRock reports $8.79 trillion in index equity assets, of which $3.96 trillion is eligible for its “Voting Choice” program, which lets clients direct their own votes, and about $923 billion is exercising that choice. In other words, roughly nine dollars in ten of those index assets are voted by BlackRock’s own stewardship team, not by the people who own the shares. The firm points out, fairly, that many clients have chosen to entrust their votes to it. But most of the default is inertia, not choice.


Banks and crypto firms are fighting over whether dollar-backed tokens may pay their holders something like interest. The GENIUS Act of 2025 barred stablecoin issuers from paying interest directly. The dispute moved to the pending market-structure bill (the CLARITY Act), where a Senate compromise in spring 2026 banned rewards that function like deposit interest while permitting rewards tied to actual activity. Major bank trade groups rejected the compromise as a loophole; Coinbase endorsed it. Jikh’s description of this as a contest between the bank lobby and the crypto lobby is accurate.


And it is true that nations are jockeying for position. In the same week as Davos, Putin offered to pay the billion-dollar fee for a permanent seat on President Trump’s proposed Board of Peace out of Russian assets frozen in the United States.


## What Is Compressed or Mistaken


Some of the picture is too tidy.


**The “postwar agreement” is several eras stacked into one.** The Bretton Woods system of 1944 tied the dollar to gold; that link ended in 1971. The petrodollar arrangement dates to 1974. Japan’s near-zero interest rates began in the late 1990s. China became the world’s factory only after its 1978 reforms and its entry into the World Trade Organization in 2001. Jikh describes real roles, but they were not assigned at a single postwar table.


**Banks and central banks are not the same player.** Jikh says the banks want central bank digital currencies. In the United States, the reverse is closer to the truth. Commercial banks have generally opposed a retail central bank digital currency, because it would let depositors move money out of banks and into the central bank. The European Central Bank is pursuing a digital euro, but the U.S. executive branch prohibited federal agencies from developing a digital dollar in January 2025, and the House of Representatives later passed legislation against one. The real threat to American privacy may come less from a Federal Reserve coin than from the same programmable features built into privately issued tokens, digital identification, and payment platforms.


**The “fourth turning” is a theory, not a law.** Jikh invokes William Strauss and Neil Howe’s generational model, in which each of four “turnings” lasts roughly twenty to twenty-five years and a full cycle roughly eighty to a hundred. It is an interesting interpretive scheme, but its predictive record is disputed, and it should not be treated as a schedule.


**Globalization did not only fail.** It is true that it failed many American workers. It also lowered consumer prices and helped lift several hundred million people abroad out of extreme poverty. A Christian cannot count only the losses to his own neighbors and ignore the gains to his neighbors overseas, just as he cannot do the reverse. The honest verdict is that globalization, as practiced, served capital well, served the foreign poor considerably, and served the American working family poorly, and no one asked that family’s consent.


**The “pre-negotiated theater” theory cannot be checked.** Jikh is candid that he cannot prove it. A theory that treats all visible events as staged cannot be tested by any visible event, and it has a cost: it teaches citizens that nothing they do matters. That conclusion is not only unproven; it is corrosive. This paper declines to adopt it.


## The Moral Frame


The Scriptures do not give us a monetary policy, but they give us principles by which we can test any policy.


A household has a first duty to its own: *“if any provide not for his own, and specially for those of his own house, he hath denied the faith”* **(1 Timothy 5:8)**. A nation is a larger household, and its government’s first economic duty is to the families within its borders.


That duty does not license contempt for strangers. *“The stranger that dwelleth with you shall be unto you as one born among you, and thou shalt love him as thyself”* **(Leviticus 19:34)**. Trade freely chosen between persons is a form of neighborliness, and a policy that treats every foreign transaction as a theft is as false as one that treats every domestic job as expendable.


Honest measures are a moral requirement: *“Thou shalt not have in thy bag divers weights”* **(Deuteronomy 25:13)**. Money is the nation’s common measure. A currency quietly debased, or designed so an authority can switch its value on and off, is a divers weight.


Debt is a form of bondage: *“the borrower is servant to the lender”* **(Proverbs 22:7)**. A nation that cannot make what it needs and must borrow to buy it has surrendered part of its freedom.


And the biblical picture of a secure people is not a population managed from above but *“every man under his vine and under his fig tree; and none shall make them afraid”* **(Micah 4:4)**. Liberty, on this view, is ownership that no distant power can revoke.


From these principles follow the positions below.


## Positions


**1. Votes should follow ownership.** When a family’s retirement savings are invested in an index fund, the shares are theirs in every sense that matters, and the votes attached to those shares should express their will, not an asset manager’s. I propose that large index funds offer pass-through voting to all investors by default, not as an option. If an investor gives no instruction, the fund may vote on ordinary business matters, such as auditors and routine board elections, but should abstain on contested social and political proposals. A neighbor whose savings are used to push causes he never endorsed has not been loved. He has been conscripted.


**2. No retail central bank digital currency, and protection for cash.** Programmable money can be programmed against the one who holds it: frozen, restricted to approved purchases, or made to expire. That power is too great to entrust to any government or any corporation. I support a statutory prohibition on a retail central bank digital currency and a legal right to pay for ordinary goods and debts in cash. The same principle should govern privately issued digital money: no issuer may be permitted to reach into a holder’s balance to restrict lawful use.


**3. Let savers be paid for their money, honestly.** The saver who earns a fraction of a percent while the institution holding his deposits earns far more is a neighbor too. If a fully reserved, independently audited dollar token can pay its holders a fair return, the law should not forbid it merely to protect bank deposits from competition. The protection belongs to the saver, not to the incumbent: full reserves, public audits, clear disclosure, and swift redemption. Rules written chiefly to shield an industry from rivals fail the neighbor test, whichever industry writes them.


**4. Make at home what a free nation cannot safely buy abroad.** The Commerce Secretary is right: a nation should not depend on others for its medicine, semiconductors, energy, or food. Strategic self-sufficiency in those areas is a proper use of public policy. But tariffs are taxes, and domestic buyers pay them. They should target genuine security needs and nations that break their commitments, not impose them as a general tax on trade. The goal is a nation that can stand on its own feet, not one that has swapped dependence on foreign factories for dependence on protected domestic monopolies.


**5. Restore the constitutional power over war.** A system that requires perpetual war to function has already failed the neighbor test, because its costs fall on soldiers’ families and on the foreign poor. The Constitution gives Congress the power to declare war. That power should be reclaimed: no sustained hostilities without a declaration or specific authorization, sunset dates on all authorizations, and public auditing of defense contracts.


**6. No national digital identity or social scoring.** The technologies Jikh describes, digital identification tied to payments and scoring of citizens’ conduct, would give whoever controls them a leash on the whole population. I oppose any federal mandate that requires digital identity for banking, travel, or employment, and any public or publicly contracted system that scores citizens’ behavior. A person’s data is part of his property and should be under his control.


**7. Refuse fatalism.** Whether the powerful discussed some outcomes in advance, the citizen is not a spectator. He has a vote, a seat in the jury box, a purse, and a household. He can buy from his neighbor, bank locally, direct his own proxy votes, and hold his representatives to their oaths. A free people remains free by acting as if its choices matter, because they do.


## Conclusion


Jikh’s most useful sentence is that what we are watching is not good against evil but power against power. That fairly describes the contenders. It does not fully describe what citizens should want. Each of the four powers he describes, financial, sovereign, technological, and military, seeks to check the others while expanding its reach over ordinary families. The right response is not to pick a champion among them. It is to insist, in every policy, on the standard that none of them naturally serves: that the family at the kitchen table should own its property, vote its own shares, hold honest money, earn a fair return, work at home in the things that matter to the nation’s safety, and never be asked to send its sons to wars that no Congress declared.


These positions are advocacy, not decrees. If someone shows me a better way to love the neighbor in these matters, I will change my mind.

## Notes


**On the BlackRock figures.** Jikh cited $7.4 trillion in index equity, $3.65 trillion eligible for voting choice, and $812 billion voting. BlackRock’s published figures as of June 30, 2026 are $8.79 trillion, $3.96 trillion, and about $923 billion. The proportion Jikh emphasized, roughly one-ninth of index equity voted by clients themselves, is essentially unchanged. Vanguard and State Street have introduced similar programs.


**On the stablecoin dispute.** Senators Thom Tillis and Angela Alsobrooks negotiated a CLARITY Act compromise that cleared the Senate Banking Committee in May 2026. It bans stablecoin rewards that are economically or functionally equivalent to deposit interest while permitting rewards tied to activity such as payments, transfers, and staking, leaving regulators to define the line. Readers should check the bill’s current status.


**On the Davos quotation.** Secretary Lutnick’s remarks were delivered on January 20, 2026.


**On Russia and the Board of Peace.** Putin’s offer was made at a meeting of Russia’s Security Council on January 21, 2026. Of the roughly $300 billion in Russian sovereign assets frozen by Western nations after 2022, only about $5 billion is held in the United States; most is in Europe.
