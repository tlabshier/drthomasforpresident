# 261012 - Faith and Campaigns

# The Wall and the Plumb Line: Faith in the 2026 Campaign


*Dr. Thomas — Position Paper | 12 October 2026*


## The Complaint


In a recent column for *USA Today*, law professor Jonathan Turley observes that the 2026 midterms have become the most openly religious campaign season in living memory. Republicans have long spoken the language of faith; now Democrats are doing the same. Pastors, chaplains, and seminarians are running for Congress on their Christian credentials, and the result, he argues, is that the left has followed the right in tearing down Jefferson’s “wall of separation” between church and state.


His centerpiece is the Texas Senate race. According to his account, Democratic candidate James Talarico, a seminarian, has invoked his faith in support of abortion and of transgender boys competing in girls’ sports, has said that God is nonbinary and created six genders, and has said that God is not a Christian. Turley notes that Talarico has since walked back two of those positions. He also reports that a minister at Talarico’s church was recorded saying that whether Jesus existed doesn’t matter much to her. In Iowa, he describes ordained ministers running as part of a movement that calls itself “resistance preaching.”


Turley’s observation is accurate: faith is everywhere in this campaign. His conclusion — that the trouble lies in faith entering politics at all — is the part I want to examine. As the founder of [www.renaissance-ministeries.com](http://www.renaissance-ministeries.com) and a presidential candidate running on a platform centered on Righteousness, I owe the reader an account of why I think he has located the problem in the wrong place.


## What the Wall Was For


Jefferson’s phrase comes from his 1802 reply to the Danbury Baptist Association of Connecticut. The Baptists wrote to him because, in their state, the legislature treated their freedom of worship as a privilege rather than a right given by God. They were a minority under an established church, and they feared the government. Jefferson assured them that the First Amendment built a wall between church and state—meaning the federal government would make no law establishing a church or prohibiting its free exercise.


The wall, then, was built to keep the state out of the church: to forbid an official denomination, to forbid tax-supported clergy, and to forbid the government from compelling or punishing anyone’s worship. It restrains Congress. It does not restrain a voter’s conscience, and it does not require a candidate to stand before the people without accounting for what he believes is true and good.


The founders who ratified the First Amendment understood this. In his Farewell Address, Washington wrote: “Of all the dispositions and habits which lead to political prosperity, religion and morality are indispensable supports.” Adams, writing in 1798, said: “Our Constitution was made only for a moral and religious People. It is wholly inadequate to the government of any other.” These men did not want a church running the government. They also did not imagine a government run by men who had no moral law above their own will.


## Every Government Serves Something


No candidate is morally neutral. Every man who seeks power intends to use it for some purpose, and that purpose rests on what he believes is ultimately good. If he does not name God as the source of that good, he will name something else — the will of the majority, the needs of the economy, the interests of his party, or simply his own judgment. When a government acknowledges no authority above itself, the state becomes the highest authority by default. That is not the absence of religion; it is a religion with the government at its altar.


For that reason, I think it is better, not worse, when a candidate tells the voters plainly whom he serves. The voter can then judge him by the standard he has claimed. This is the moral justification for faith in public life: a man who seeks power over his neighbors owes them an honest account of the law he intends to follow, so they can hold him to it.


## The Real Danger: Bearing God’s Name Falsely


If faith in the public square is not the problem, what is? I believe it is the one Turley’s column describes without naming: using God’s name to sanction what God has not said.


The third commandment reads, *“Thou shalt not take the name of the LORD thy God in vain”* **(Exodus 20:7)**. The Hebrew verb means to lift up or carry. The command is not chiefly about profanity. It forbids bearing God’s name emptily — attaching His authority to a claim He has not made. A politician who invokes God to win votes for a position God opposes is not separating church and state; he is counterfeiting the church’s currency to buy influence in the state.


That gives voters a test, and I propose it in this order. First: *Is it lawful?* That is, is this position righteous — consistent with what God has revealed? Second, and only then: *Is it loving?* Does it serve the neighbor’s real good? Love that ignores God’s law becomes sentiment that can justify anything. God’s law applied without love becomes cruelty. Christ is the calibration for both.


## Applying the Test


Measured that way, several of the reported claims fail the first question. Scripture says,* “So God created man in his own image, in the image of God created he him; male and female created he them”* **(Genesis 1:27)**. It describes the child in the womb as known and formed by God **(Psalm 139:13–16; Jeremiah 1:5)**, and it says, *“Thou shalt not kill”* **(Exodus 20:13)**. A man is free in America to believe that God is nonbinary, that there are six sexes, or that the Bible supports abortion. He is not free to claim that Scripture teaches these things, because it does not, and anyone can open the book and check.


I should give Talarico credit where it is due: he has retracted two of those statements. The question a voter should ask is what the retraction means. Did he come to believe his earlier claim was false, or did he find it politically costly? Only he knows. But it is fair to ask him, because a conviction that moves with the polls is not a conviction about God.


Likewise, a church whose minister says it does not matter whether Jesus existed has not offered a different version of Christianity. Christianity stands or falls on a historical claim: that Jesus lived, died, and rose again. Paul put it directly: *“If Christ be not raised, your faith is vain”* **(1 Corinthians 15:17)**. A congregation may hold whatever philosophy it likes; it should not present that philosophy to the public as the Christian faith.


## The Same Plumb Line for Both Parties


This test belongs to neither party. Turley is right that Republicans began heavy use of religious language decades ago, and some of that use has been just as empty. A candidate who quotes Scripture on Sunday and lies, demeans, or worships power on Monday has taken the Lord’s name in vain as surely as one who reads the Bible upside down. Amos tells us that God measures His people with a plumb line **(Amos 7:7–8)**. A plumb line doesn’t care which side of the wall it hangs from. If I hold the left to the text, I must hold the right, and myself, to the same text.


## On Judging Candidates


Some of Talarico’s critics have gone further. Turley reports that one prominent pastor has called him not a Christian, and evil. I think this goes beyond what we can know. Jesus told us to judge a teacher by his fruits **(Matthew 7:16)**, and the public teaching of a candidate is fair to evaluate — a false teaching should be called false, plainly. But God alone knows the final state of a man’s soul. We can say that a doctrine is wrong without claiming to see into the heart of the man who holds it. Proportion matters, and so does charity: the aim of correction is that a man turn, not that he be destroyed.


## On the Fear of Sectarian Conflict


Turley notes that many Americans fear religious campaigning because they have seen sectarian violence elsewhere. That fear is reasonable, but the remedy is not to stay silent about God. The remedy is the content of the Christian faith itself, which forbids coercing belief, commands love of enemies, and recognizes that each man answers to God for his own conscience. The American liberty of conscience did not grow out of secularism; it grew out of Baptists and other dissenters who believed that faith compelled by the state is not faith at all. A candidate who actually believes the Gospel is, for that very reason, the last man who should want to use the state to force it on anyone.


## What I Ask of Voters


I suggest three questions for every candidate, of either party, who speaks of faith:


- **Whom do you serve?** Name the authority above your own will.

- **Does your position match what that authority actually says?** Not a slogan about it — the text itself. Read it yourself; don’t take a candidate’s word, or mine.

- **Will you hold the position when it costs you?** A conviction proves itself under pressure.


The wall of separation should stand: no established church, no state control of worship, no punishment of conscience. But that wall was never meant to keep righteousness out of government. What it cannot do, and what no law can do, is keep a candidate honest about God. That falls to the voter, armed with the text and the willingness to read it.


This is my position, and I hold it with reasons. If someone can show me a better reading of the history or of Scripture, I am open to it.
