# 261127 - Platform Comparison - Republican vs Democratic

# Judging the Parties by What They Would Build


### A Plank-by-Plank Reading of the 2024 Platforms on Life, God, and the Family

**Thomas Lee Abshier, ND** | **27 November 2026**


---


In an earlier essay, "The Enemy of My Enemy," I argued that a movement must be judged by what it would build, not by whom it opposes. That principle applies to political parties. A party's platform is its own statement of what it would build if given power. It is the fairest place to judge a party, because it is the party's own words, adopted by its own delegates.

This paper applies that test to the two major parties' 2024 platforms, the most recent ones adopted. (Each party writes a new platform every four years, in presidential election years.) I will look at three questions that I believe matter more than any others for a nation's moral health: the protection of human life, the place of God in public life, and the structure of sex, marriage, and family.

I will state my conclusion at the outset. On all three questions, I find the Democratic platform committed to positions I believe are opposed to God's standards and enforced by the power of government. I also find the Republican platform retreating on two of the three. I will try to show my reasons plank by plank, in the platforms' own terms, so that a reader who disagrees with me can see exactly where and why.

## A Word About Precision

I have chosen my words carefully in this paper.

The Democratic Party has allied with, or at least not rejected, a faction that calls itself the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA). This platform calls for expanded government programs, such as a higher federal minimum wage and an expanded Child Tax Credit, within a market economy; it does not call for abolishing private property. I believe its direction expands the state's control over economic and moral life, and I will argue that.

Nor do I call it a Godless party. According to the Public Religion Research Institute, the Democratic Party remains majority religious and majority Christian. Many of its members are believers, and many vote as they do because of their faith. My argument is with the platform, which is what the party proposes to enforce, not with the faith of the people in it.

## Life

**What the Democratic platform says.** The 2024 Democratic platform pledges to pass national legislation to "make Roe the law of the land again." Like its predecessors, it pledges to eliminate the Hyde Amendment, the long-standing rule that bars federal tax dollars from paying for abortion. It promises to protect access to abortion pills.

**What the Republican platform says.** The 2024 Republican platform states that the Fourteenth Amendment guarantees that no person can be denied life or liberty without due process, and that the states are therefore free to pass laws protecting those rights. It opposes late-term abortion and supports access to birth control and IVF. For the first time in four decades, it does not call for a national law protecting unborn life, and it removed the explicit "right to life" plank. The 2016 platform had affirmed that "the unborn child has a fundamental right to life which cannot be infringed." Pro-life leaders split over the change: some called the new language a betrayal, while others counted the Fourteenth Amendment reference as a gain.

**My judgment.** I hold that a human life begins at conception and is known by God before it is born: *"Before I formed thee in the belly I knew thee; and before thou camest forth out of the womb I sanctified thee"* **(Jeremiah 1:5)**. On that understanding, the Democratic platform proposes to establish the legal killing of unborn children as national law and to make every taxpayer help pay for it. That fails every test I apply. It is not righteous, because it takes innocent life. It is not loving, because it abandons the most defenseless neighbor. And no one would want it done to himself; every person reading this was once the unborn child whose life the law would leave unprotected.

The Republican platform does not propose this, but it has stepped back from defending the unborn at the national level. I count that as a retreat, and I say so, because a principle that excuses my own side is no principle.

## God and Religious Liberty

**What the Democratic platform says.** The 2024 Democratic platform pledges to protect the free exercise of religion and to maintain the separation of church and state. It promises to "continue to honor both religious freedom and other civil rights, not put them at war with one another." Reporting on the draft found that it contained no mention of "God," "Lord," or "Almighty." In 2012, the party removed God from its platform and then restored the reference by a contested voice vote on the convention floor. The 2020 platform referred to "God-given potential."

**What the Republican platform says.** The 2024 Republican platform pledges to champion the "First Amendment Right to Pray and Read the Bible in school."

**My judgment.** The absence of a word is not the heart of the matter. Platforms are policy documents, and a party can honor God without naming Him, or name Him without honoring Him. The heart of the matter is what happens when religious conscience and newly declared civil rights conflict. The Democratic platform's promise not to put religious freedom and "other civil rights" at war has been read by religion reporters and Christian critics alike as a commitment not to let religious conscience override anti-discrimination law. Combined with its support for the Equality Act, discussed below, that means a Christian school, hospital, adoption agency, or business could be compelled by law to act against its faith. I regard that as the government taking up the sword against conscience.

The founders understood rights as gifts of the Creator, not grants of the state. A government that treats conscience as subordinate to its own declarations has placed itself above the source of the rights it claims to protect: *"Righteousness exalteth a nation: but sin is a reproach to any people"* **(Proverbs 14:34)**.

## Sex, Marriage, and the Family

**What the Democratic platform says.** The 2024 Democratic platform pledges to pass the Equality Act, which would add sexual orientation and gender identity to federal anti-discrimination law in housing, public accommodations, education, credit, jury service, and federal programs. It pledges to protect "marriage equality in federal law." It states that Democrats will "vigorously oppose state and federal bans on gender-affirming health care" and calls for insurance coverage of "all medically necessary care for gender transition."

**What the Republican platform says.** The 2024 Republican platform pledges to "keep men out of women's sports, ban Taxpayer funding for sex change surgeries, and stop Taxpayer-funded Schools from promoting gender transition," and to restore protections for women and girls under Title IX. It promises to restore parental rights and to support universal school choice, including education savings accounts for homeschooling. It promises to "promote a culture that values the sanctity of marriage." It also dropped long-standing language defending traditional marriage between one man and one woman and its condemnation of the Supreme Court's *Obergefell* decision.

**My judgment.** I hold, with historic Christian teaching, that sexual union belongs within the marriage of one man and one woman, and that conduct outside it calls for repentance rather than celebration. I hold that a child's body is not a field for experiment, and that parents, not the state, bear primary responsibility for raising their children. On those convictions, the Democratic platform proposes to use the full power of federal law to promote and enforce a view of sex and family that I believe is contrary to God's design, and to compel dissenters to participate.

I am not judging any person's worth before God. Every person bears His image, and every person, including me, needs His mercy. I am judging what government would promote and enforce. A government may protect every citizen from violence and abuse; it may not compel citizens to affirm what their consciences reject, and it should not use public money to promote what it ought to discourage.

The Republican platform resists the Democratic platform on gender and on parental rights. On marriage, it has quietly withdrawn. I count that as a second retreat.

## The Long March and the Long Game

I have watched the culture move a great distance from the moral and legal world of the 1950s in which I was born. That movement has not been accidental. Incrementalism is a deliberate strategy: propose a large change, accept a smaller one, and advance again the next session. The Fabian socialists of the late nineteenth century made gradualism their method, and a phrase associated with the German student left of the late 1960s, "the long march through the institutions," describes the same patience applied to universities, media, courts, and churches.

But incrementalism is a tool, not a creed, and it works for whoever uses it with patience and conviction. The clearest recent proof is the pro-life movement. For nearly fifty years after *Roe v. Wade*, pro-life Americans refused to treat abortion as settled. They passed limited laws, built institutions, changed hearts, and shaped the courts, one step at a time, until the Supreme Court overturned *Roe* in *Dobbs v.** Jackson Women's Health Organization* on 24 June 2022. Their leaders said openly that they believed in the incremental approach.

That is a hopeful lesson. A trend that took decades to build can be reversed by people who are steady, principled, and unwilling to quit. It is also a warning. The Republican retreats of 2024 show that the drift can move a party that claims to resist it. The remedy is not a better slogan. It is leadership by voice, changing hearts first, so that the law can follow.

## Applying the Test to My Own Side

In "The Enemy of My Enemy," I argued that judging a movement by its principles binds my own side as tightly as the other. I have tried to honor that here. In my judgment, the Republican platform is better than the Democratic platform on the three questions I have examined, but it is not faithful on all of them. It has stepped back from defending unborn life at the national level, and it has stepped back from defending marriage. A voter who cares about these questions should say so to the party he usually supports, not only to the one he opposes.

## Why This Concern Remains Serious

Some will say that platforms are aspirational documents that few voters read, that members of each party disagree with parts of their own platform, and that the parties' actual conduct matters more than their words. All of that is partly true. But the concern remains serious for three reasons.

First, platforms show direction. They tell us what a party's most committed members want, and over time a party's officeholders tend to move toward its platform.

Second, these planks are not mere preferences; they are proposals for law. Law is enforced by the sword of government, and what government enforces, it teaches. A generation raised under a law learns to regard it as right.

Third, the questions involved are not secondary. Whether the unborn are protected, whether conscience is free, and whether the family is honored are questions on which, I believe, the health of a nation rests.

I stand on the side of protecting unborn life, freedom of conscience, and the family as God designed it, and on those questions I find the Democratic platform opposed and the Republican platform wavering. The areas of real uncertainty are how faithfully each party's officeholders follow its platform, how much of each platform reflects its voters rather than its activists, and how courts would apply laws like the Equality Act in practice. I hold my position open to a better argument. But I believe a nation is judged by what it chooses to protect, and that our choices on these questions will shape what we become.
