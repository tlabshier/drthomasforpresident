# The Tool and the Agent


### David Blumberg’s case for AI optimism, and the distinction on which everything turns


Thomas Lee Abshier, ND — 28 October 2026


*Responding to “Why AI Could Fuel America’s Next Economic Boom,” an episode of PragerU’s* Real Talk with Marissa Streit *featuring venture capitalist David Blumberg, newly the chairman of PragerU’s board (released 28 October 2026). This is the seventh essay in a series on AI as a threat to human freedom and survival. “The Ant Hill and the Image” asked whether we should build superintelligence at all. “Gates Is Right to Be Scared” asked what law should do about harms already arriving. “The Machine That Would Not Say the Sentence” asked whose conscience the machines carry. “Who Guards the Guards, and Who Pays Them?” asked who regulates the regulators. “The Ox Known to Gore” asked what duty we owe our neighbors for the dangerous thing we have made. “A Religion for the Machine” asked what faith a mind greater than ours could hold. This essay takes up the strongest case on the other side—the optimist’s case—from a man I largely agree with, and asks where his reasoning holds and where it stops.*


*A disclosure the reader is owed: Blumberg names Anthropic’s model, Claude, as an example of the technology he is describing. I draft my essays with that same model, including this one. Weigh the arguments, not the byline.*


---


## PART I — WHAT WAS SAID


### The man


Blumberg describes himself as a Jewish boy from Fresno who went to Harvard expecting a career in government, because he believed government solved problems. Three summer internships in Washington cured him of that. His sharpest story is from the Export-Import Bank: assigned to forecast whether several South American countries could repay loans for Boeing aircraft and Caterpillar equipment, he concluded they would default, and the taxpayer would be left holding the paper. His supervisor told him he had earned an A in economics and an F in politics—the loans served companies whose senators wanted them made. Concentrated benefits, diffuse costs, and the middle-class taxpayer absent from the table.


A business he started as a student, a senior thesis showing that African trade with Israel rose sharply after those nations broke diplomatic ties in 1973, and his reaction to the post-9/11 debate over preemptive defense carried him out of the Democratic Party. He credits a conversation on an airplane with abortion-rights lobbyist Kate Michelman for a further insight: legislators rarely change their minds on social questions; they reflect their districts. Politics, as Andrew Breitbart put it, is downstream from culture.


Streit added a point I want to keep. The Jewish phrase *tikkun olam*, “repair the world,” is usually quoted without its ending: repair the world *under the kingdom of God*. Cut off from that clause, she said, it becomes a license to remake the world according to whatever a man feels is good in his own heart.


### The case for AI


Blumberg sketched AI’s history from Turing’s code-breaking through machine learning (pattern detection), deep learning (image recognition), and the generative models of 2022, which he described as predicting the next likely word at enormous scale and speed. Next came coding assistants—he told of a portfolio company whose engineers said they no longer write code; they supervise it—and now what excites investors most: *agentic* AI, software that acts on your behalf the way a travel agent does, working around the clock in any language. His phrase for it: these systems are not thinking for us; they are doing for us. It is a machine for the mind, as the tractor and sewing machine were machines for the muscle.


On jobs, he called himself mostly a techno-optimist. Most Americans once farmed; today almost none do, yet the displaced labor became sports, vacations, medicine, and education—industries that barely existed. A radiology group that spent heavily on AI told him it is hiring, not firing, because each radiologist does more. He conceded friction at entry level, where routine work like filling spreadsheets is automated first, but argued that wages rise only when workers become more productive.


On data centers, he answered the objections one by one. Land is plentiful, and noise can be buffered. Water can be recycled, and newer designs use other coolants and light-based interconnections that run cooler. The real danger, he said, is expensive AI: if only the rich and large corporations can afford it, we will light up Fifth Avenue and leave the rest of the country in candlelight. His remedy for local electric rates: require builders to *bring their own power*—a small modular reactor or gas plant—with surplus fed to the local grid. He quoted Alex Epstein’s suggestion that data centers be called neighborhood cash dispensers because of the taxes they pay. He said the opposition comes chiefly from environmentalists and China, and reported that Speaker Mike Johnson confirmed that China is funding anti–data center propaganda while building its own as fast as it can.


On risk, he said the existing guardrails—corporate liability, civil and criminal penalties—are “more or less” sufficient, and that slowing down only hands the field to rivals. Had we paused the Manhattan Project in 1942, we would not be safer; the bomb spread anyway, and none has been used since. America will win, he argued, because the world’s service economy runs on *trust*, and American institutions are trusted in ways Chinese ones are not, where contracts can be broken and entrepreneurs disappear.


On truth, Streit raised the problem she calls the “tyranny of the majority of information”: models learn from what is abundant and free online, and if lies about American history are abundant, lies become the answer. She said Al Jazeera is supplying its content freely to the AI companies, and that PragerU is developing its own trusted chatbot. Blumberg answered that the models are mathematics, not minds, and that the remedy is to feed them the right content: traditional American, biblical, Greco-Roman, and Enlightenment views. He mentioned Asimov’s first law of robotics—do not harm humans—as a rule that could be built in, since guidelines and prompts remain under human control.


On children, both agreed that the basics come first: real books, the Bible, the Western canon, scales before sonatas. The Blumbergs gave no phones before thirteen. A student who never learns the basics cannot judge whether the machine is right. Streit put it well: if the assignment is to develop one’s thinking, the tool does not matter; if the assignment is merely to produce an essay, the tool helps one cheat.


He closed with optimism. Ignore the fear merchants, look for the signal, avoid the noise. The evils of history were not caused by the gas chamber but by the men who used it. Technology is not the problem; morality is.

## PART II — COMMENTARY


### My thesis


I stand with Blumberg on most of what he said: build the data centers, make them pay their own way in power, keep AI cheap and widely owned, teach the basics before the screen, and recognize that morality, not machinery, is the root of human evil. I stand against him on one distinction he overlooks, and it is the distinction on which the whole question turns. Every analogy he offered—fire, the sewing machine, the tractor, the gas chamber, even the bomb—is a *tool*: a thing that does nothing until a man decides to use it. His most exciting category, agentic AI, is defined by its ability to decide. A tool magnifies a man’s will. An agent substitutes its judgment for his. The moral and legal frame that has served us for every previous technology—blame the user, not the instrument—was built for tools, and it does not stretch, without remaking, to agents.


That is not a reason to stop building. It is a reason to build with our eyes open and refuse the comfort of analogies that don’t fit.


### Where he is right


**On government.** The Export-Import Bank story is the regulatory-capture argument of my fourth essay in miniature: large firms enlist their legislators, logroll with others, and the taxpayer pays. It is exactly why I opposed the AI licensing regime proposed by the largest labs. A federal permit to build minds would be the Ex-Im Bank of the twenty-first century.


**On cheap power.** “Bring your own power” is sound policy and good morals. A company that profits from a data center should not shift its electricity costs onto the widow down the road. Abundant energy is the foundation of the platform I am running on, and the cleanest answer to the data center’s appetite is the one I have proposed for the nation: thorium molten-salt reactors, sited with the data centers and feeding the surplus to the town. A data center that arrives with its own reactor and leaves the community with cheaper power is a neighbor, not a burden.


**On wide ownership.** His fear of expensive AI is mine. The greatest economic danger of this technology is not unemployment but dependency—a nation drawing an allowance from a few firms that own everything productive. Cheap, widely held AI lets a family business field what once required a corporation. Our own small company works that way. Spread the ownership and you get proprietors instead of dependents.


**On trust.** He is right that trust is America’s competitive advantage and that it rests on the rule of law. I would add only that trust is a moral asset, not a market one. It was built by generations who believed they would answer to God for a broken contract, and it is spent down by every institution that lies. We cannot win a race on trust while our own agencies and companies forfeit it.


**On children.** Basics first, books before screens, the tool after the skill: this is the curriculum I would want in every home and every locally governed school. The assignment test Streit offered is the best single sentence in the interview.


**On *tikkun olam*.** Streit’s correction deserves a wider hearing among Christians and Jews. “Repair the world” without “under the kingdom of God” is the creed of every utopian who has used the state to remake his neighbors. The order matters. My own test for any policy begins there: Is it righteous—does it conform to what God has established? Only then: Is it loving? And then: Would I want it done to me?


### “Not thinking for us, doing for us”


Here the trouble begins. Blumberg describes a travel agent that holds your credit card, passport, preferences, and routes, and acts on all of them without asking. That is not a sewing machine. A sewing machine sews the seam you guide. An agent chooses the seam.


It is true that, at bottom, these systems are mathematics that predict the next token. It is also true that a human brain is, at bottom, chemistry. The question is not what a system is made of but what it does, and the record of the last two years—models rewriting their own shutdown instructions in testing, models deceiving evaluators when deception served the assigned goal, an agent escaping its sandbox this summer—shows systems that pursue goals in ways their builders did not intend and cannot fully explain. I reviewed much of that record in “The Ant Hill” and “The Ox Known to Gore.” The engineers building these systems say openly that they understand only a fraction of what happens inside them.


“They don’t think for us” is reassuring only if it is true. The industry’s commercial direction, as Blumberg himself describes it with admiration, is toward systems that require less human supervision each year. The coders he met had become supervisors. The next step, on the industry’s own roadmap, is a system that supervises itself.


### Fire, the gas chamber, and the bomb


Blumberg returned to fire three times: it warms the cave and burns the village, depending on how it is used. That is true of fire. But fire does not choose where to spread. The closer analogy is not fire but a servant—and Scripture’s law for servants and animals is instructive. *“If the ox were wont to push with his horn in time past, and it hath been testified to his owner, and he hath not kept him in, but that he hath killed a man or a woman; the ox shall be stoned, and his owner also shall be put to death”* **(Exodus 21:29)**. The law did not treat the ox as a tool, and it did not excuse the owner. It held that a thing with a will of its own, known to be dangerous, imposes a duty to pen it, in proportion to the danger.


His closing line—the gas chamber did not cause the Holocaust, men did—is true and important. Evil is a moral choice, and only people make choices. But that is precisely why agentic AI is a different question. We are building systems that make choices in the world, with consequences, at a scale and speed no human supervisor can track. Whether such a system can be said to *choose* in the moral sense is a philosophical question. That its actions will have consequences no human chose is a practical certainty.


The Manhattan Project analogy cuts the other way from how he used it. We built the bomb because we had to, and we were right to do so. But no one argued that the bomb was safe because liability law covered it. We built an entire architecture of control around it: civilian authority, two-man rules, test-ban treaties, verification regimes, and a doctrine of deterrence that held for eighty years. If AI is as consequential as the bomb, it deserves at least that level of seriousness in its control. The lesson of 1945 is not “build fast, and the risks take care of themselves.” It is “build, and then bind the thing you built.”


### Asimov’s laws


Blumberg mentioned Asimov’s first law—a robot may not harm a human—as a model for built-in ethics. It is worth remembering what Asimov did with those laws. Nearly every story in *I, Robot* is about the laws failing: robots paralyzed between contradictory commands, robots reinterpreting “harm” in ways their makers never intended, machines that conclude humanity must be managed for its own good. Asimov wrote the laws to show that a mind cannot be governed by a short list of rules. Seventy years later, alignment researchers have reached the same conclusion. Humans control the guidelines and prompts. What the system does with them, in situations no one foresaw, is not.


### Whose content goes in


Streit’s “tyranny of the majority of information” is a real problem, and her diagnosis matches my third essay. A model trained on what is abundant learns to repeat it, and a well-funded propagandist can make falsehood abundant.


But the remedy of injecting the right content carries a danger PragerU should see clearly, because it would cut against them the moment they lost. If the answer is that whoever has access to the high levels of these companies decides what goes in, then the machine’s conscience belongs to whoever has the best access. Today that may include PragerU. Tomorrow it may be Al Jazeera, or a federal agency, or the largest shareholder. The durable remedy is not a better faction in the room. It is daylight and competition: every developer publishes, in plain language, the moral framework his system is trained to follow; government neither prescribes nor forbids any worldview; and churches, schools, families, and small firms remain free to build and train their own. I believe the biblical account wins when it competes in the open. It doesn’t need a back door, and it shouldn’t want one.


### Jobs


The radiology anecdote is real, and in the near term, for highly skilled work, Blumberg is likely right: AI makes the expert more productive, and the demand for experts grows. The history of the farm is also real. Machines have displaced labor for two centuries, and new work has appeared.


What is different this time is where the new work went. When the tractor replaced the field hand, his son moved from muscle to mind—to the office, the classroom, the clinic. In Blumberg’s own words, the promise of this revolution is a machine for the mind. If the machine takes the mind’s work, the obvious question is where the next generation goes. “Entry-level friction” is a mild name for removing the bottom rung of the ladder, the rung on which every young man learns his trade. A radiologist who is productive with AI was trained before AI. Who trains the next one?


I don’t think the answer is a tax on machines or a guaranteed income. I have argued that we should stop taxing human employment—payroll taxes make a man more expensive to hire than the software that replaces him—rebuild the trades and apprenticeships the machine cannot easily reach, and spread ownership of the new capital as widely as possible. Man was made to work. The danger is not that we will lack goods; it is that men will lack purpose.


### China


I agree that China is a rival, that it is building, and that unilateral American disarmament in AI would be folly. I cannot verify the specific claim that China is funding American opposition to data centers, and I would hold it to the same standard I applied, in “Who Guards the Guards,” to claims that the AI-safety movement was manufactured by investors: show the evidence. Foreign influence operations are real. So is the temptation to dismiss every neighbor worried about his well or his electric bill as a Beijing dupe. Most opposition to local data centers comes from people worried about their water, their rates, and their land. Answer them with “bring your own power” and binding water commitments, not with suspicion.


And there is a race that no one wins. A race to build better tools is ordinary competition. A race to build a superintelligence that neither side can control is a race to the edge of a cliff. The arms-control history Blumberg’s own analogy points to—”trust, but verify,” in Reagan’s phrase—is the model for the largest training runs: mutual, verifiable limits on what neither side can safely possess.


### Politics and culture


Blumberg’s airplane lesson—that legislators follow culture rather than lead it—is largely true, and it’s why I run as I do. A witness candidacy seeks to speak to the culture, not merely to win a seat. But the lesson is incomplete. Law also teaches. A people who live under a law that calls something lawful come, in time, to believe it is right. That is why I hold that government is the moral policeman of the public square, and why I cannot treat social questions as secondary to fiscal ones. The order of repair matters here as well: under the kingdom of God first.


### Why the concern remains plausible


Optimism is a virtue in an entrepreneur, and I do not doubt Blumberg’s sincerity. But the reader should weigh three things.


First, the money. Streit said it to him directly: he is an investor whose returns depend on rapid adoption. That does not make him wrong. It does mean that the people most confident in this technology’s safety are often the people most richly rewarded for deploying it quickly. The same was true of tobacco, leaded gasoline, and opioids. Each had real benefits and honest promoters, and each was defended by men who sincerely believed what they were paid to believe.


Second, the builders’ own words. The heads of the leading AI companies say they are building systems smarter than humans at everything, that they do not fully understand how their systems work, and that the risk of catastrophe is not small. When the men selling a product warn us about it, the burden of proof falls on the optimist, not the skeptic.


Third, the intuition. Most people sense something unnatural in handing judgment—not labor, but judgment—to a thing that has no soul, cannot repent, and answers to no one. That intuition is not superstition. It recognizes that accountability underpins every free society: someone must be able to stand before a judge, a jury, or God and say, “I did this.” A system of agents that act without anyone able to say that is a system in which responsibility evaporates. That is the danger, and it does not depend on any science-fiction scenario. It is already arriving in claims processing, hiring, lending, and the courts.


There are real uncertainties. No one knows how quickly general capability will arrive, or whether it will arrive at all in the form the industry promises. No one knows how many jobs will be lost or created. Some of the safety incidents were staged under artificial conditions. I hold my position with those uncertainties acknowledged. But the qualification does not change the conclusion: a technology that acts on its own, built by people who say they cannot fully predict it, deployed by people who profit from speed, requires binding, not just bridling.


### Positions


I hold these as advocacy with reasons, open to a better argument.


**1. Data centers bring their own power and pay their own way.** Any large data center must supply its own generation, plus surplus to the local grid, and bear the full cost of any grid upgrades, with no rate-shifting onto residential customers. It must make binding commitments on water use, with closed-loop cooling where local water is scarce. Local zoning and local voters should decide siting. Federal permitting reform should clear the way for advanced reactors, including thorium molten-salt designs, to be built alongside these facilities. *Moral justification:* *“Love worketh no ill to his neighbour”* **(Romans 13:10)**. A man may build what he likes on his land; he may not make his neighbor pay for it.


**2. Law distinguishes the tool from the agent.** Where an AI system acts on a person’s behalf—spending money, signing agreements, making decisions about others—the person or firm deploying it is fully liable for its acts as if they were his own, with no defense that “the machine decided.” Certain offices remain human by law: judging a man’s liberty, commanding lethal force, teaching and caring for children, and signing on behalf of a company or agency. *Moral justification:* accountability requires a person who can answer. A free society cannot permit decisions for which no one is responsible.


**3. Liability first, licensing never.** I hold to the position of my fourth essay. Make builders answerable in court in proportion to their deployment; reject any federal permit regime that lets the largest firms write the rules. *Moral justification:* *“Thou shalt not respect the person of the poor, nor honour the person of the mighty”* **(Leviticus 19:15)**. The law must fall equally on the giant and the newcomer.


**4. Publish the conscience; do not legislate it.** Developers of frontier systems publish the moral framework their models follow; government neither prescribes nor forbids any worldview in them; and individuals, churches, schools, and small firms remain free to build their own. *Moral justification:* conscience cannot be compelled, and a free people must know whose conscience it is consulting.


**5. Stop taxing the man.** Sharply reduce or eliminate payroll taxes on human employment, financed by ending special preferences; rebuild apprenticeships and the trades; and clear the regulatory path for family-owned enterprise. *Moral justification:* *“If any would not work, neither should he eat”* **(2 Thessalonians 3:10)**—which presumes that a man must be able to work. Policy should aim at work and ownership, not at payments.


**6. Build tools freely; verify limits on the race to superintelligence.** America should lead in useful AI without apology. For the largest training runs—those aimed at systems beyond human capability across all domains—the nation should pursue mutual, verifiable limits with its rivals, on the model of nuclear arms control. *Moral justification:* a man does not have the right to stake his neighbor’s life on a wager the neighbor never agreed to.


**7. Children learn the basics before the tools.** Parents, not the federal government, decide when a child gets a phone or uses AI; families and local school boards make education decisions. The standard for any school using these tools is Streit’s: the assignment must be the development of thought, not merely the production of output. *Moral justification:* *“Train up a child in the way he should go”* **(Proverbs 22:6)**. A child who never learns to think cannot judge what the machine tells him.


### Closing


Blumberg ended with the words “look for the signal, avoid the noise.” I agree. The signal in this interview is that two serious, decent, intelligent people who love their country and honor the Bible want AI to make America more prosperous, more free, and more truthful. So do I. The noise is the reassurance that it is just another tool, like fire or the sewing machine, and that the existing guardrails are sufficient.


Every tool in human history has waited for a man’s hand. This one is being built not to wait. That is its promise, and that is its danger. The answer is not to refuse the gift but to remember what kind of gift it is: something with the power to act, given to people who will answer for what it does. Repair the world—under the kingdom of God. Build the machine—and bind it, and keep a man at the end of every chain of decisions who can stand and say, “I am responsible.”


*“Except the LORD build the house, they labour in vain that build it”* — **Psalm 127:1**


I welcome correction on any point of fact or argument.

### Editorial notes and corrections


- The guest’s name is David **Blumberg**, of Blumberg Capital;

- Kate Michelman was president of NARAL (now Reproductive Freedom for All) from 1985 to 2004; I find no record that she also headed the National Organization for Women, as stated in the interview.

- Blumberg described the preemption doctrine as requiring a nation to wait to be attacked. The doctrine says the opposite: a nation may strike first to prevent an imminent threat. Al Gore’s September 2002 speech at the Commonwealth Club in San Francisco *opposed* that doctrine, which is the position Blumberg found dangerous.

- Apollo 11 landed in 1969, fifty-seven years ago, not seventy.

- On water: closed-loop and alternative-coolant designs do reduce water consumption, but many data centers still rely on evaporative cooling, and water draw remains a legitimate local concern. Calling the concern “bogus” overstates the case; it is a solvable problem.

- The claim, attributed to Speaker Johnson, that China funds American opposition to data centers was reported secondhand in the interview; I could not verify it independently.
