I wrote it back in 2023, and it basically says, "This is what I've been through." It's a strange tale. I wouldn't recommend anyone follow the path that I've taken, but it changed me for the better, only because, in an altered state, I saw why Jesus is Lord and the Bible is the correct path to life. It changed me, and I believe that transformation is available and should be taken by everyone.

The platform says what I stand for and what I envision for America. Is it a good vision? Would you want the world organized around these principles? **ISAK:** It's somebody you've met in your family or among your friends: the guy with a little drug use and a lot of theories. Maybe that's even accurate, but it's a hard way to live. It's a hard way to keep your brain healthy and functioning. I don't think it's all bad. Psychedelics show you new things, and that's beautiful, but it isn't sustainable. You meet people who are fried. Sometimes I wonder how much more capacity I would have if I had never gotten into drugs or drinking. That's something I have to live with.

**THOMAS:** That's exactly how I feel. Oh my gosh, if only I hadn't done that. But I don't know that I would have come to the same enlightenment and be who I am now without it. I was going down the wrong track, and this let me jump off the track. I went down a new one, got off the drugs after I jumped, and it was a better place. But there was definitely a loss. There's no question about it. I wouldn't recommend the drug path to enlightenment. You might get some insight, but there is real potential for great loss. I feel fortunate that I was able to change my life path because of what I saw in that altered state. Some people have an experience that removes them from contact with reality, and they never get out. The risk is too great. I feel fortunate that I had a positive result from my altered state experiences, but I was lucky, blessed, or protected; it could have been very bad.

**ISAK:** I don't think the thing itself is evil. Everything can be evil, depending on what a person does with it. You're still at the wheel. It's a different lens on your life and your beliefs. Then you come to, and you have all this insight, and you don't want to do the work of fixing yourself. So you think the drug is the process. ### What I saw

I do not tell anyone to use psychedelics. I say that first because the rest of this chapter tells how I used them, and a reader could take the story as an invitation. It is not one. The experience is not a party. It is deeply serious, and it can change a man's picture of the world so thoroughly that the world is never the same for him again. I gained insight. I also lost something I cannot get back, and I have known men who went in and never came out. The insights were real, and every one of them was available by another road: Scripture, prayer, counsel, and the slow work of practicing a better way until it became mine.

In the 1980s I used edible cannabis weekly for about three years, in a vision-quest setting, often with a group of fellow seekers. Over a two-year period I took LSD about five times and psilocybin mushrooms about five times, usually alone. The mushrooms I found myself, crawling in a rain suit through a cow pasture near Tillamook, Oregon, with two other naturopathic students. The two visions that matter here both came on LSD.

My first LSD trip came in 1984, early on a Sunday morning, while I was still married. I was lying in bed, facing the window and the sun, when the window shattered into a million fragments of rainbow crystal and white light. I laughed so hard I could not explain to my wife what was happening.

I think that was neurology: receptors suddenly overstimulated or released from inhibition. I believe the spirit, the point of consciousness that is who I am, observes the brain and assembles its perception of the world from the brain's sensory map. Men who report near-death experiences describe seeing the world as they did in the body, only more clearly. That suggests the spirit can perceive directly, and that in the body it perceives through the senses. A drug that changes how neurons fire changes the map the spirit reads. The world looks different because the instrument has been retuned.

### The flower

Later that same morning, as I stepped into the bath, I saw in my inner vision a living room with a stairway. On the stairs sat a bird-of-paradise flower. I was transfixed. I went to it and gazed into it, and I wanted to stay in its presence forever.

As I gazed, its beauty faded a little. I focused harder, and it returned almost to its full glory, then faded again, further than before. I focused harder still, and it came back a little less. Each time it faded more. It went limp, lost its support, collapsed, rotted, and dripped down the stairs.

The lesson: life does not allow a man to hold one moment forever. He can appreciate the moment, attend to it deeply, and take all the beauty it holds. Then he must let it go and turn to the beauty of the next.

### The rainbow

The second vision came on my second LSD trip, in the middle of the night. My wife had left me by then, and I was alone. I sat on the rug of my office floor and waited. A rainbow spread across my inner vision, like an oil slick, diffuse and beautiful. In it I saw the promise of everything I wanted: complete happiness, every desire satisfied, the answer to every question. It seemed to have a center, and I believed that if I could reach it I would have peace.

I followed it. The colors swirled faster as I went deeper, and the center receded. The speed became exhausting, and I lost it in a blur. Then it reappeared, slow and beckoning, and I followed it again, and again it outran me. The cycle went on for more than six hours. It left me in tears at the futility of chasing the end of the rainbow.

The lesson took me years to put into words. God is at the center of all of it, but the purpose of creation is not to dissolve back into Him. He made the world as many points, many spirits who experience one another and are experienced, so that there could be relationship and love. Happiness is not found by reaching the center of the moment. It is found in appreciating the whole: the small and the large, the parts and their relationships, the flow from one moment to the next, and the bond between self, other, and God.

### March 1987

Neither of these was the vision that set the course of my life. That came three years later, in March 1987, on two nights in a row, after high doses of oral cannabis. The same picture came both nights, and on the second night its meaning came to me as words: a message about the nature of reality at its smallest scale. I believe that vision was directed by God. It was the seed from which my physics work grew, and I have told it more fully in "The Pharisee and the Sheikh." Those two nights ended my seeking.

### What the research says, and what it cannot say

In 2023 a survey of 3,157 people, recruited through social media and email, found that people who had used psychedelics reported more mindfulness, gratitude, awe, and kindness, and less greed, hate, and envy, than users of other substances ([summary](https://www.msn.com/en-us/health/wellness/psychedelic-use-is-linked-to-several-specific-psychological-strengths-study-finds/ar-AA1c7nF3)). A mediation analysis tied those differences to self-transcendent experiences. The study is correlational, its data are self-reported, and its sample was not representative. It cannot say whether the drugs produced the strengths or whether people with those strengths sought the drugs.

The psychiatrist Richard Friedman offered a caution the same year ([summary](https://www.msn.com/en-us/health/other/maybe-psychedelics-aren-t-the-best-idea-for-your-brain/ar-AA17eNN9)). The neuroplastic state these drugs induce is what makes a child impressionable, and it also makes him vulnerable. An adult carrying trauma may have painful memories opened without the skills to process them.

Both points fit my experience. What a psychedelic can open is not a technique but a perspective, and the brain can take on a new perspective at any age. What matters is what a man sees and what he does with it.

### The doors of perception

The most powerful thing a psychedelic shows is the oneness of it all. Before, a man thinks he is a single consciousness living alone, looking out at others who are separate, with God somewhere out there looking at us. After, he has seen that the universe is one consciousness and that he is one of its many points of view. The world is different, and it may never be the same. The change is like the passage from living as a child to seeing life as an adult.

I wonder whether this is forbidden fruit. I cannot say it is the same as eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, but it is like it: a man sees something about reality he did not know before, and he cannot unsee it. *"And the eyes of them both were opened"* **(Genesis 3:7)**. God took great care to disguise the oneness. I think He closed the doors of perception on purpose, so that there could be selves and others, and so that love could be chosen freely. I cannot say for certain that He does not want us to see it, but seeing it plainly may run against His plan.

Yet He made the plants that open those doors. Scripture says the leaves of the tree of life are *"for the healing of the nations"* **(Revelation 22:2)**. It also lists *pharmakeia*, the use of drugs in sorcery, among the works of the flesh, where the King James renders it *"witchcraft"* **(Galatians 5:20)**. I hold both. Life cannot be solved by eating a mushroom, and the answer to every question cannot be as trivial as taking an herb. There must be much more to life than taking a drug and having an experience.

So I do not recommend it. I think of it, at most, as reparative medicine: for some people it may knock a stuck mind loose. It should never be recreational. It is a hinge in a life, and it should be approached soberly, counting the risk and the cost.

I had no guide. I did not know what I was looking for, and no one was there to direct my search. I believe God could have done the same work on my heart and mind without the drugs, through meditation and prayer, if a counselor had been there to point the way. A man can come to know God as the One and the All, and himself as one point of His perception, without forcing the doors open. It takes more work. It is the better way.

The danger lies in what a man does with the oneness once he has seen it. It is true in a sense: the universe is one, and every point of consciousness comes from God. But God made separation on purpose, because love requires an other. A man who learns that all is one and then tries to live dissolved in it has mistaken the canvas for the painting. The unity worth seeking is the unity of the faith: many free men choosing the will and way of God.

### Why this book begins here

Isak said it plainly: a glimpse is not a change. What the drug showed me was everything at once. In every moment there is an object, a person, a feeling, a goal, an economic setting, a drama of relationship, a teaching about how to live, and a music of memory. All of it is present all the time. In the altered state, a man sees it all and cannot rank any of it. "It's all too beautiful."

What a man needs next is what the drug cannot give: priority and practice. He needs to know which part of the moment calls for appreciation and which calls for action. He needs to know which road leads where. Then he needs to do the work, sober, the way a mechanic learns to turn the wrench, until the right way is his own.

That is what a map is for. It shows a man where he is, what else is there, and the way between. This book is my attempt to draw it. It is drawn from my years of counseling patients, from Scripture, and from long conversations with a young friend who walked some of the same roads I did. I did not need the rainbow to learn what is in these pages. You do not either.

## Part I: Seeing

*From "The Mapmaker and the Wanderer," a parable written for Isak from the October conversations. The mapmaker is named Ezra here.*

There was a town built at a crossroads where five roads met. Nobody remembered who had built it, but everyone had a theory. The roads led to five different kingdoms, and from each kingdom came traders, teachers, prophets, and thieves, though it was not always easy to tell one from the other.

In this town lived an old mapmaker named Ezra. He had spent forty years drawing a single map. It was not a map of the roads; anyone could draw those. It was a map of what lay at the end of each one. He had walked them all in his youth, some of them more than once. One road had nearly killed him. Another had shown him something so beautiful he wept for a week, though he could not explain what he had seen.

Now he sat in his shop with the map unfinished, surrounded by drafts and revisions, talking to anyone who would listen.

One day a young man named Eli came into the shop. He was a carpenter's son who had been wandering between the five roads for years, never committing to any of them. He had been down the Eastern road far enough to hear its music, and down the Southern road far enough to taste its wine. He had stood at the mouth of the Northern road and felt the cold, and he had peered down the Western road, where the ground was paved with gold and the air smelled of burning.

"I need a map," Eli said.

"Everyone needs a map," said Ezra. "The question is whether you'll use it."

"I don't know which road is right."

"That's not what you mean," said the old man. "You know which road is right. You're afraid you're wrong about it."

Eli sat down.

## Chapter 1: Everything at Once

*From a conversation between Thomas Lee Abshier, ND, and Isak, 30 October 2026. Edited for clarity by Claude.*

**THOMAS:** The psychedelic experience opens us to the fact that all these layers exist at all times. Take any moment. There's a ball. There's a person in it. There's a feeling associated with it. There's a goal we wanted to reach. There's an economic context it sits in. There's a relationship drama going on at the same time. There's a wisdom teaching about how to live. There's a pattern like music, with its beat and the memories that go with it. That collage is present in every moment of life. We can look at it and ask, "What do I want to do with that pattern? How can I make it more enjoyable, more meaningful, more productive for the future?" Or we can sit back and say, "Wow, man, it's all too beautiful." In the altered state it's very hard to put any priority on it, because it's all too beautiful.

Any of those layers can be isolated. We can look at one as a vignette, a picture at an exhibition, a statue in a museum, and hold it as the moment of beauty. Or we can say, "This is a time when action needs to be taken."

**ISAK:** So all of life is happening at once, and seeing it lets us out of our own framework, our own brain, our own life. If you think life is all about you, or all about your problems, then stepping out shows you that other people exist. We're open to all these different waves, and we can redirect our attention and evaluate which one is important.

**THOMAS:** The data is always coming in, whether we're aware of it or not. Being aware of it, knowing to look for it, is one kind of benefit: you know there is something there to look for. Then, in a particular configuration, one of those points becomes the most salient, the most critical, the most urgent to handle. We pick up the clues, we weigh them, and we decide what to do. I think what we're really doing is changing the weights.

### A moment, opened up

Every moment of life is fuller than we notice. We live in a thin slice of it, usually the task in front of us or the worry behind us, and the rest goes on without our attention.

Take an evening in a backyard. A man is playing catch with his son. There is the ball: its weight, its arc, the slap of it in the glove. There is the boy, eight years old, squinting into the low sun. There is a feeling, pride and tenderness together, with a worry about the boy's schoolwork running underneath. There is a goal: the father wants the boy to learn to keep his eye on the ball, and he wants him to know his father has time for him. There is an economic setting: this hour came out of work he did not finish, and the mortgage is due Friday. There is a drama of relationship: his wife is watching from the kitchen window, and they argued that morning. There is a teaching: *"Train up a child in the way he should go: and when he is old, he will not depart from it"* **(Proverbs 22:6)**. And there is music: a radio on the neighbor's porch is playing a song his own father used to whistle, and with it comes the memory of another backyard forty years ago.

All of that is present at once. None of it is imagined. The father may notice two or three of those layers. The rest are there whether he notices them or not.

### Why we do not see it all

We do not see it all because we cannot. Attention is narrow by design. The senses take in far more than the mind can hold, and the brain sorts it, keeping a little in the foreground and pushing the rest into the background. As I said in the Prologue, I believe the spirit reads the map the brain draws. The brain hands the spirit an edited map.

That editing is a mercy. A man who saw every layer of every moment at full strength could not drive a car or finish a sentence. God built us to see in part, and I think He did it on purpose. *"For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face"* **(1 Corinthians 13:12)**.

The psychedelic state takes the editor away for a few hours. Every layer comes forward at once, lit to the same brightness. That is why it feels like revelation. It is also why it is useless as a way of living, as a later section explains.

### Seeing toward

In October, Isak told me about an afternoon in his backyard. Light was passing through glass light-catchers hanging by the porch and scattering in every direction. He looked at a bush in the corner of the yard and realized he was not seeing the bush. He was seeing sunlight the bush had sent to him. A friend standing beside him would see the same bush by different light, from a different angle, but from the same sun. "I'm not looking at a bush," he said. "I'm looking toward a bush. The only reason I'm seeing it is because something else is showing it to me."

That is true of every layer of every moment. The ball, the boy, the feeling, the mortgage, the song: each is lit by a source that is not itself. Follow any of them back far enough, through light to matter, through matter to the points it is made of, and you arrive at God. That has been the whole of my work for forty years: showing that every line of inquiry, followed to its end, ends at Him. *"For the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made"* **(Romans 1:20)**. The source is easy to miss because it lights everything else. *"For in him we live, and move, and have our being"* **(Acts 17:28)**.

So when I say every moment holds everything at once, I mean more than a crowd of details. I mean that every moment is a window turned toward God, whether or not the man standing in it looks up.

### What you bring

What a man sees in a moment depends a great deal on what he brings to it.

A rock comes out of a canyon wall. In itself it is only a rock. One man picks it up and says, "This will make a good doorstop," and now it is a doorstop. Another man picks up the same rock, sees a fossil in it, and says, "This belongs in a museum." The rock has not changed. The two men brought different frames to it, and the frame gave it its identity. A man who brings nothing does not notice the rock at all. He trips over it and forgets it.

The father in the backyard is no different. If he brings nothing, he sees a ball and a boy and the end of a long day. If he brings Proverbs, he sees a lesson being laid down that will outlast him. If he brings only his anxiety, he sees the hour he is losing at work. The moment holds all of those. What he takes from it is set by what he carries in.

That is why a map matters. A map is not the territory, but it tells a man what is there to look for. Isak called it "knowing to look for it," and he is right that this is a benefit in itself. Most of what a man misses, he misses because no one told him it was there.

### Too beautiful to rank

The altered state shows everything and ranks nothing. The ball and the boy and the song and the mortgage all come forward at the same brightness, and a man sits in the middle of it saying, "It's all too beautiful." He is not wrong. It is beautiful. *"He hath made every thing beautiful in his time"* **(Ecclesiastes 3:11)**.

But notice the last three words: *in his time*. Each layer has its season. The ball needs attention now, and the argument with the wife needs attention tonight. The mortgage needs attention Friday. The song can be enjoyed in passing, and the memory can be savored later. A man who cannot rank them cannot live in them. He can only look.

Isak named the first gift of seeing more: it breaks the illusion that life is all about you. Other people exist, with their own layers and their own moments, and they are as real as you are. That is a great deal to learn. But seeing more is not the same as knowing what matters most. A man can see the whole collage and still not know which part to touch first. That knowledge is wisdom, and wisdom, as I said to Isak, is a matter of changing the weights.

### You are here

The most important mark on any map is the dot that says *You are here*. Every other mark depends on it. A man who does not know where he stands cannot use the map at all.

A moment, seen whole, is that dot. It is where a man stands: in this backyard, with this boy, this debt, this marriage, this song, and this God shining through all of it. The rest of this book is about reading the map from that dot. The next chapter asks why most of us cannot see the dot clearly: we are wearing something over our eyes. Later chapters ask why a glimpse of the whole does not change us, how a mind does change, and how a man decides which part of his moment to handle first.

The drug showed me everything at once. It could not tell me where I was standing, or which way to walk. That is what a map is for.

## Chapter 2: The Hat-Screen

*From a conversation between Thomas Lee Abshier, ND, and Isak, 21 October 2026. Edited for clarity by Claude.*

**THOMAS:** Do you think it is God's will that people experience what you've experienced? I can't imagine a God who says, "I want it for these people, but not those. I don't like them that much." The idea is absurd. We're looking too small; we're looking at the ground. There's a picture I have of a person wearing a hat with a little screen in front of his eyes. Everywhere he looks, all he sees is the little screen. Take the hat off, and he says, "Wow! The world is bigger than I thought!"

That's how people live. People live with a hat with a movie on. That's it. By ten, twelve, fifteen years old, they have the hat on so firmly they don't think they can take it off.

To get people out of that, you have to show them their screen. There are holes in the story. You think mass is real? What's it made of? Atoms. What are atoms made of? Protons and neutrons. What are those made of? Quarks. Where did quarks come from? The physicists say they popped out of nothing. Have you ever seen anything come out of nothing? That's faith: faith in quarks arising from nothing. It sounds like a myth. Why not believe in Apollo and Venus? Your screen is defective. It didn't give you deep answers. You might as well be living in *The Truman Show*.

Are you open to there being something more? Then I tell them about conscious points: everything can be described with conscious points, which came from the mind of God. Where did God come from? We're back to the same question, but a level deeper. It's like 4K against 640 by 480. There's a lot more resolution.

**ISAK:** When I was young, in church, going through the motions, I thought I knew everything. Now I think I know nothing. I'm trying to get to the point of actually knowing something again.

**THOMAS:** If it happened to you, it can happen to anyone. There's not that much difference between people. There's some programming: different screens on different hats. But I took mine off. I saw something different. I didn't have to keep wearing it.

### The hat with a screen

Picture a man wearing a cap with a small screen hanging from the brim, an inch in front of his eyes. The screen plays a movie of the world. Wherever he turns his head, the movie turns with him. He has worn it so long he has forgotten it is there. He thinks he is looking at the world. He is looking at the screen.

That is how most of us live. The screen is a worldview: a set of answers to the questions of what is real, what matters, who I am, what is good, and what happens when I die. It came with a cast and a script. It tells us which people are the heroes and which are the villains, which desires are natural and which are shameful, and which questions are worth asking.

Chapter 1 said that every moment holds far more than we see. Part of the reason is the brain's merciful editing. The other part is the screen. The brain narrows what comes in; the screen decides what it means.

### How the screen is installed

No one chooses his first screen. It is installed in childhood by parents, teachers, friends, churches or the lack of them, schools, television, and now the phone. By ten or twelve it is mostly in place. By fifteen it is fastened tight, and the boy wearing it would defend it with his fists.

This is not a complaint about parents. Every child must be given some picture of the world, or he could not act at all. The question is not whether a child gets a screen, but whether the screen shows what is really there. Jesus put the stakes plainly: *"The light of the body is the eye: if therefore thine eye be single, thy whole body shall be full of light. But if thine eye be evil, thy whole body shall be full of darkness. If therefore the light that is in thee be darkness, how great is that darkness!"* **(Matthew 6:22–23)**.

The last sentence is the hard one. A man with a dark screen does not experience darkness. He experiences his screen, and his screen tells him he sees.

### Testing the screen

A screen can be tested the way a story can be tested: by following its answers down until they stop.

Take the modern secular screen, the one most Americans were handed in school. It says the world is matter and energy, and that is all. So ask it the child's question, "What is that made of?" and keep asking. Matter is made of atoms. Atoms are made of protons, neutrons, and electrons. Protons and neutrons are made of quarks. Where did the quarks come from? Physics answers that they condensed out of the energy of the first instant of the universe. Where did that energy come from, and the laws it obeyed? Here the screen goes blank. Some physicists say the universe arose from a quantum vacuum, but a vacuum with laws and fields in it is not nothing; it is something no one can account for. Others say it simply began, with no cause at all.

Have you ever seen anything come out of nothing? Neither has anyone else. To believe it is an act of faith, and not a small one. I do not say this to mock the physicists; I have spent my life in physics. I say it because the secular screen presents itself as the one with no faith in it, and that is not true. At its foundation it has a blank, and it asks you not to look there. A man who discovers that blank is a little like Truman Burbank finding the painted wall at the edge of his sea.

### Every hat has a screen

It would be easy to say the secular man wears a screen and the Christian does not. That is false, and Isak is the proof. He grew up in church, and he wore a church screen. "I thought I knew everything," he said. It played the right hymns, but it played them as a movie; he went through the motions. He had to take that hat off too, and stand for a while knowing nothing, before he could begin to know something.

The Pharisees wore the most respectable screen of their day. They had the Scriptures memorized, and when the Author of the Scriptures stood in front of them, they could not see Him. Paul, who had worn that screen himself, called it a veil: *"But their minds were blinded: for until this day remaineth the same vail untaken away in the reading of the old testament; which vail is done away in Christ. ... Nevertheless when it shall turn to the Lord, the vail shall be taken away"* **(2 Corinthians 3:14, 16)**.

Notice what Paul says removes the veil. Not more religion, and not a better denomination, but turning to the Lord Himself. The Bible is not meant to be one more screen. It is an imperfect instrument pointing at something perfect: Christ. A man can wear the Bible as a screen, using its words to keep from seeing; the Pharisees did. Or he can look along it, the way you look along a pointing finger, to the One it points to.

So the honest question is not "Which screen is mine?" but "Is my screen showing me what is there?" Every Christian should ask it of himself before he asks it of anyone else.

### More resolution

When I show a man the blank at the bottom of his screen, the fair reply is: "What is at the bottom of yours?"

My answer is that the smallest things in the world are points of consciousness, and that they came from the mind of God. That does not end every question. A child can still ask, "Where did God come from?" and I cannot answer him any better than the physicist can answer about the quantum vacuum. We both stop somewhere.

The difference is where we stop, and what we can see on the way down. The secular screen stops at a blank and calls it chance. Mine stops at a Person, and from there the rest of the picture comes into focus: why there are minds at all, why there is order, why we sense good and evil, why love matters. It is the difference between a television of 640 by 480 pixels and one of 4K. Both are pictures. One shows far more of what is really there.

### Taking it off

The hardest step is the first one: noticing the hat. A man does not take off what he does not know he is wearing. And when someone points it out, his first impulse is to defend the screen, because the screen is how he knows who he is. To take it off feels like losing himself.

There are two ways the hat comes off. One is crisis: the screen fails so badly, in a divorce or a death or a collapse, that a man can no longer pretend it shows the world. The other is teaching: someone he trusts shows him the holes in the story before the crisis comes. The second is far cheaper, and Chapter 5 is about it.

Either way, the hat can come off. I took mine off. It was not because I was wiser than anyone else; there is not much difference between one man and another. There is only programming, different screens on different hats. What happened to me, and to Isak, can happen to anyone, because God wants it to happen to everyone. He is the God *"who will have all men to be saved, and to come unto the knowledge of the truth"* **(1 Timothy 2:4)**.

That raises a fair worry. Is this book just one more screen, handed to the reader in place of his old one? A screen sits in front of your eyes and replaces the world. A map is different. You hold it in your hand, look up from it, and check it against the ground. If the map says there is a river ahead and you find a desert, the map is wrong, and you should say so. I offer this book as a map. Take your hat off, look at the ground, and test it.

**THOMAS:** That's very similar to where I was going. At some point we have to actually make decisions about what to do in life.

**ISAK:** Imagine doing that without taking a drug. You'd see many different possibilities, many ways things could go. ### The mirror and the forgetting

James describes a man who looks into a mirror: *"For if any be a hearer of the word, and not a doer, he is like unto a man beholding his natural face in a glass: For he beholdeth himself, and goeth his way, and straightway forgetteth what manner of man he was"* **(James 1:23–24)**.

That is a glimpse. For a moment the man sees himself as he is: the spot on his collar, the hardness around his mouth, the years. Then he walks away, and by the time he reaches the door the picture is gone. Nothing in him has changed except a memory, and the memory fades.

The Prologue told of my rainbow, the one I chased for six hours and never reached. That night showed me something true. But if I had left it there, it would have been a mirror I walked away from. What I saw had to be turned into how I lived, and that took years.

### The temptation to stay on the mountain

Peter, James, and John went up a mountain with Jesus and saw Him transfigured, His face shining like the sun, with Moses and Elijah talking with Him. It was the greatest glimpse any men have had. Peter's response was to try to keep it: *"Master, it is good for us to be here: and let us make three tabernacles; one for thee, and one for Moses, and one for Elias: not knowing what he said"* **(Luke 9:33)**.

*Not knowing what he said.* Peter wanted to build a house on the mountaintop and live in the glimpse. Jesus did not let him. The next day they came down, and at the bottom of the mountain a crowd was waiting, and a father with a son who was tormented and convulsing **(Luke 9:37–42)**. The glory on the mountain was real. The work was in the valley.

Every glimpse carries the same temptation. A man who has seen something wonderful wants to see it again. If a drug showed it to him, he takes the drug again. Isak has watched this happen to friends: they come back from each trip with more theories and less life, until some of them are, in his word, fried. "The thing that revealed it to me was this drug," they reason, "so I'll use the drug to fix it." They have confused the window with the door. The window showed them a place; only walking will take them there.

### It is not only drugs

It would be easy to read this chapter as a warning about psychedelics and nothing more. It is broader than that. A glimpse can come in many ways: a revival meeting, a retreat, a powerful sermon, a funeral, a near miss on the highway, the birth of a child, a week at a conference where everything finally made sense. Each of these can show a man where he is and where he could be. Each of them can also be chased like a high.

The church has its own version of the man who keeps taking the drug. He goes from conference to conference and revival to revival, collecting experiences. He weeps at every altar call. On Monday he is the same husband, the same employee, the same neighbor he was on Saturday. He is not a hypocrite, exactly. He has simply mistaken the feeling of being moved for the fact of having moved.

### What a glimpse is good for

None of this makes a glimpse worthless. A glimpse does three things a man badly needs.

First, it shows him where he is. Chapter 1 called this the dot that says *You are here*. Many men have never seen the dot. They do not know they are standing in a swamp until something lifts them high enough to see it.

Second, it shows him there is somewhere else to stand. The man in the swamp may believe the whole world is swamp. A glimpse of solid ground, even from far off, tells him otherwise.

Third, it shows him the distance between the two. This is the painful part, and the useful part. Isak said a glimpse shows "what got you on the path you're on, where you are, and where you could be." That is a map in a single flash: origin, position, destination.

What a glimpse cannot do is walk the distance for him.

### Repentance is a decision, then a road

The biblical word for what must happen next is *repentance*. In Greek it is *metanoia*, a change of mind. But Scripture never lets repentance stay in the mind. John the Baptist told the crowds at the Jordan, *"Bring forth therefore fruits meet for repentance"* **(Matthew 3:8)**. When they asked what that meant, he did not tell them to have another experience. He told the man with two coats to give one away, the tax collector to stop overcharging, and the soldier to stop extorting and be content with his wages **(Luke 3:10–14)**. Each answer was concrete, small, and daily.

Isak saw the same pattern in the man at the altar. The man's glimpse is that he is not right with his brother. His repentance is the decision to go. His fruit is the conversation, the apology, the debt repaid. Only then does he return to the altar. Jesus put the order plainly: *"Leave there thy gift before the altar, and go thy way; first be reconciled to thy brother, and then come and offer thy gift"* **(Matthew 5:24)**. God would rather have the brother reconciled than the gift on the altar.

So the real change has two parts. The first is a decision: "This is something in my life I want to change." Isak said it: without a decision, a man is not changed; he is only worn down. The second is a road: the decision carried into ordinary days, until what began as a choice becomes a habit and then a character.

### From glimpse to road

A glimpse is not a change. It is an invitation to one. It says, "Here is where you are. Here is where you could be. Will you go?" Most people who receive the invitation do not answer it. They remember the view and stay where they were.

The next chapter asks the practical question: once a man has decided to go, how does the change actually happen? How does a decision become a habit, and a habit become who he is? The answer is less mysterious than a vision and much more demanding. It is the way a man learns to turn a wrench.

## Chapter 4: How Minds Actually Change

*From a conversation between Thomas Lee Abshier, ND, and Isak, 30 October 2026. Edited for clarity by Claude.*

**THOMAS:** Practicing a thing over and over in the right way seems a reasonable method of growth. That's how you develop a skill. You learn that if you do things in a particular sequence, you get a good outcome, and life works for you. You practice it, the brain develops pathways, and now you have a neural track that knows how to do that thing: a track of coordination, a way of thinking, a way of listening and seeing. I think that really is what growth is.

In the concrete world, the skills are fairly clear. You get a job, you take up a hobby, you learn chords, you learn how to take the bolts off an engine. The real challenge is somewhere else. I think what we're really looking for, when we take psychedelics and have that moment of neuroplasticity, is the patterns of life and thought: how we organize a way of looking at things, a way of relating to people, the meaning of life, how one thought connects to another. That more abstract realm is where we're really trying to grow, where we want to embed new skills.

**ISAK:** You can train your mind, and trust that neuroplasticity isn't just there for a drug. It's there for you to keep changing the patterns of your brain.

### The wrench and the bolt

The first time a boy takes a bolt off an engine, he does not know which way to turn the wrench. He guesses, strains, and skins his knuckles. His father shows him: righty-tighty, lefty-loosey, and seat the wrench all the way on the head so it does not round the corners. The boy tries again. By the twentieth bolt he no longer thinks about it. His hands know.

What changed? Not the bolt, and not the wrench. The boy changed. Something in his brain was laid down by repetition, like a path worn across a field by many feet. The first trip across the field is hard going through tall grass. By the hundredth trip there is a trail, and walking it takes no thought at all.

Neuroscientists have a shorthand for this, drawn from the psychologist Donald Hebb: neurons that fire together wire together. Every time a pattern of thought or action is repeated, the connections that carry it grow stronger. Every time it is left unused, they weaken. Brain scans of London taxi drivers, who spend years memorizing the city's tangle of streets, have found the region of the brain that handles spatial memory enlarged in them compared with other people. The brain is shaped by what it does.

### The skills no one teaches

No one doubts that hands can be trained. What we forget is that everything else can be trained the same way.

How a man answers his wife when she is short with him is a skill. How he listens to a son who is telling a long story badly is a skill. Whether he holds a grudge or lets it go, whether he tells the truth when a lie would be easier, whether he notices the beauty in a moment or rushes past it: each is a pattern, laid down by repetition, and each can be laid down differently.

Scripture treats these as skills to be practiced, not feelings to be waited for. *"A soft answer turneth away wrath: but grievous words stir up anger"* **(Proverbs 15:1)**. A soft answer is not a mood. It is a thing a man does, and does badly at first, and better the hundredth time. *"Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath"* **(Ephesians 4:26)**. Letting go of anger before the day ends is a practice. A man who does it once a day for a year has worn a trail that the man who never does it does not have.

The writer of Hebrews says mature believers are *"those who by reason of use have their senses exercised to discern both good and evil"* **(Hebrews 5:14)**. *By reason of use.* *Exercised.* Even the ability to tell good from evil is trained, the way an athlete trains a muscle.

### Plasticity is not reserved for drugs

The 2023 article I commented on in the Prologue raised a worry: psychedelics put the brain into a highly plastic state, and in that state a man may be opened to harm as easily as to growth. I answered then, and I still think, that the real skill a psychedelic can open is a perspective, not a technique. But the deeper point is Isak's: the brain's capacity to change is not something a drug creates. It is how God built us. The drug only floods it all at once, without direction.

The ordinary way of change uses the same capacity slowly and on purpose. It is slower when we are older; the trails are deeper and the grass grows back more slowly where we try to cut a new one. But the older brain can learn, and men change their habits at seventy. What they need is not a chemical. It is a clear picture of the new pattern and the patience to walk it until it becomes a trail.

### Putting off and putting on

Paul describes the change in the language of clothing: *"That ye put off concerning the former conversation the old man, which is corrupt according to the deceitful lusts; And be renewed in the spirit of your mind; And that ye put on the new man, which after God is created in righteousness and true holiness"* **(Ephesians 4:22–24)**.

There are two motions here, and both are needed. The old trail does not vanish because a man has decided to stop using it. Under stress, when he is tired or afraid or insulted, his feet will find the old path, because it is still the deepest groove in the field. Putting off means noticing that, catching himself on the old path, and stepping off it again and again until the grass grows back.

Putting on means walking the new trail deliberately, before it is easy. A man who has decided to answer softly will answer harshly many times before the soft answer comes first. Each time he catches himself, apologizes, and tries again, the new path deepens a little. *"Be not conformed to this world: but be ye transformed by the renewing of your mind"* **(Romans 12:2)**. Renewing is a continuous verb. It is done daily.

### Not by practice alone

This could be read as self-help: pick a habit, repeat it, become a better man. That is part of it, but it is not the whole.

Paul holds two things together that most of us would keep apart: *"Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling. For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure"* **(Philippians 2:12–13)**. The man works, and God works in the man. The desire to change is itself God's gift; so is the strength to keep at it when the old trail is easier; so is the light that shows which new trail to cut. Practice without grace becomes grim self-improvement. Grace without practice becomes a feeling that never reaches the hands.

### How the change is actually made

Put together, the change runs like this.


 	- **See the old pattern.** This is the glimpse of Chapter 3: a moment of seeing where you are.

 	- **Name the new pattern.** It must be concrete. "Be more loving" is not a pattern. "When she is short with me, answer softly and ask what is wrong" is.

 	- **Practice it small and often, in real life.** Not in imagination, and not only in church, but at the kitchen table, in traffic, and in the office, where the old pattern lives.

 	- **Expect to fail and step back on.** Each failure, caught and corrected, deepens the new trail.

 	- **Ask for help, from God and from other people.** A man walking a new trail does better with someone beside him who knows the way.


None of this is glamorous. It has none of the light of a vision. But it is how every man who has truly changed has changed. The boy with the wrench did not learn by seeing a picture of a loosened bolt. He learned by turning it.

There is one more question, and it is the subject of the next chapter. If change is laid down by practice, where does a man get the right pattern to practice? He can learn it the hard way, by walking the wrong trail until it ends in ruin. Or he can be told.

## Chapter 5: Precept Before Experience

*From a conversation between Thomas Lee Abshier, ND, and Isak, 30 October 2026. Edited for clarity by Claude.*

**THOMAS:** There's the principle of precept versus experience: being taught versus the school of hard knocks. By precept, someone shows us the domains of life. Here is an example of the domain of beauty. Here is work. Here is relationship. We look at them and say, "Right now I'm focused only on work. I'm not paying attention to my family, and relationship is an important part of life." So I can take a fast from work and focus on relationship. Or I can have a dramatic breaking experience: do something dangerous or difficult, or take a drug, and suddenly see that there was relationship and beauty in addition to work. Or a tragedy can break the pattern for me. But I don't have to go through a divorce because I never learned there was something other than work.

Learning by precept sounds like this: "Son, when you get married, remember: don't always just work. You have to spend some time with her." "Okay, that's something to remember." Then we consciously choose: "I'm going to spend some time not working." That's learning by precept, as against divorce, which is learning by experience. Precept is a much cheaper way of learning the patterns of life: the spectrum of life, and its proper distribution.


---


### Two schools

There are two schools in life, and everyone attends one or the other. In the first, someone who has walked the road tells you where it goes. In the second, you walk it yourself and find out.

The second school has excellent teachers. Pain is a vivid instructor, and almost no one forgets its lessons. Its problem is the tuition. A man who learns by experience that his marriage needed more of him than his work did pays for the lesson with the marriage. A man who learns by experience what alcohol does pays with his liver, his license, or his children's trust. A man who learns by experience what lies do to a reputation pays with the reputation. The lessons are real. The price is often everything the lesson was about.

The first school charges almost nothing. It costs a conversation, a book, an hour of listening to someone older. Its only real price is humility: the willingness to believe that someone else has seen what you have not.

### The divorce that did not have to happen

I know a great many men who learned, after their first marriage ended, that a wife needs time and attention as much as she needs a paycheck. Most of them learned it too late. Some of them, I think, could have kept their marriages with one sentence said to them at twenty-five by a man they respected: *Don't always just work. Spend time with her.*

That is all a precept is: a sentence that carries a lesson someone else paid for, handed to you for free. *"My son, hear the instruction of thy father, and forsake not the law of thy mother: For they shall be an ornament of grace unto thy head, and chains about thy neck"* **(Proverbs 1:8–9)**.

The proverb calls instruction an ornament, not a burden. A man wearing the instruction of his father walks into situations already knowing what they hold. *"A prudent man foreseeth the evil, and hideth himself; but the simple pass on, and are punished"* **(Proverbs 22:3)**. The prudent man has not seen the evil before. He has been told about it, and he believed what he was told.

### Why we refuse to be told

If precept is so much cheaper, why does nearly everyone choose the expensive school?

Part of the answer is pride. To accept a precept is to admit that someone else knows something you do not. Young men in particular would often rather be wrong on their own than right on someone else's word.

Part of it is the culture we were handed. The screen most of us wear (Chapter 2) tells us to find our own truth, follow our hearts, and learn from our own mistakes. It treats inherited wisdom as a kind of prison, and treats the man who walks off a cliff on his own judgment as braver than the man who listened to the warning sign. That is not courage. It is waste.

And part of it, honestly, is that many teachers have not earned trust. Isak has said more than once that what troubles him most about organized religion is that it so often becomes a system of control: men in authority handing down rules that serve the men in authority. He is right that this happens. A precept is only as good as its source, and a man who has been handed bad precepts by people who used them to control him has good reason to be wary.

### Testing the teacher

So the answer cannot be "believe whatever you are told." It has to be "learn whom to believe."

I test a teacher in three ways. First, does his teaching agree with Scripture, which has carried the accumulated wisdom of God's dealings with men for four thousand years? Second, does he live what he teaches, and what fruit does his life bear? *"Ye shall know them by their fruits"* **(Matthew 7:16)**. Third, is his teaching confirmed by others? *"In the mouth of two or three witnesses shall every word be established"* **(2 Corinthians 13:1)**. No single man, however wise, should be the only voice a person listens to. Every man should take what he hears to God and to his brothers and ask, "What did you hear?"

A teacher who passes those tests is worth listening to, even when what he says is unwelcome. A teacher who asks to be believed simply because he holds an office has, in Isak's phrase, changed the authority. That is a warning sign, and Part III of this book will return to it.

### What experience is still good for

None of this means experience teaches nothing. The prodigal son learned by experience. He took his inheritance, wasted it, and ended feeding pigs and envying their food. And there, Jesus says, *"he came to himself"* **(Luke 15:17)**. He went home, and his father ran to meet him.

That story tells us two things. Experience can teach a man what precept could not, when he has refused every precept. And God receives the man who comes home by the long road. But it also tells us what the long road cost: the inheritance was gone, and it did not come back. The father restored the son. He did not restore the money.

I learned some of the lessons in this book by experience. The Prologue told how. I am grateful for what God made of those years, and I would not wish them on anyone. That is the whole reason for writing a book like this one: to hand on by precept what I bought by experience, so that someone else does not have to pay what I paid.

### The precepts are already written

The good news is that most of the precepts a man needs have already been written down. Proverbs alone holds more practical wisdom about work, money, speech, anger, marriage, and friendship than most men will use in a lifetime. The histories of Israel record a nation learning by experience, and Paul says why they were preserved: *"Now all these things happened unto them for ensamples: and they are written for our admonition"* **(1 Corinthians 10:11)**. Their experience is meant to become our precept.

That is the end of Part I. A moment holds everything at once (Chapter 1). We see it through a screen we did not choose (Chapter 2). A glimpse of the whole does not change us (Chapter 3); practice does (Chapter 4). And the cheapest way to learn what to practice is to be told by someone trustworthy (Chapter 5).

Part II takes up the question that comes next. A man standing in a full moment, with his hat off and a map in his hand, still has to decide what to do with the next five minutes. How does he choose?

### Triage

In an emergency room, the first skill is not medicine. It is triage: deciding who is treated first. A man with a broken finger may be shouting louder than anyone in the room, and a quiet man in the corner may not be breathing. The nurse who treats the loudest patient first will lose the quiet one. Every physician learns the order: airway, breathing, circulation, and only then the rest.

Life is an emergency room most of the time, though it rarely feels like one. Chapter 1 showed that every moment holds many layers at once. Part I was about seeing them. Part II is about choosing among them, and the first rule of choosing is the triage rule: handle what will kill you before you handle what merely annoys you, and handle what can wait after what cannot.

That is the tiger and the kitten. When the tiger is coming, no one needs to be told to stop petting the kitten. The rule is obvious, which is why I called it kindergarten.

### Kindergarten wisdom

Obvious does not mean easy. A remarkable share of the ruin I have seen in my years of practice came from ignoring the obvious. The man who knew he should see a doctor about the chest pain and went to the game instead. The woman who knew the man she was dating drank too much and married him anyway. The young man who knew he should not sign the loan. None of them needed a philosopher. They needed to obey what they already knew.

The kindergarten lessons are worth stating plainly, because they are the first page of the map. Tell the truth. Keep your promises. Pay your debts. Take care of your body. Provide for your family. Don't take what isn't yours. Say you are sorry. Forgive. Most of Proverbs is kindergarten wisdom, written down because grown men keep forgetting it. *"Wisdom is the principal thing; therefore get wisdom: and with all thy getting get understanding"* **(Proverbs 4:7)**.

### After kindergarten

But Isak and I agreed that kindergarten is not the end of school. Most of life is not a tiger and a kitten. It is several kittens and something half-seen in the grass that might be a tiger or might be a shadow.

Inside any moment, there are many claims on a man: play, hunger, safety, beauty, work, rest, relationship, worship. Each has its own arena, and each is good in its place. Not one of them is better than the others in the abstract. Rest is not better than work, and work is not better than rest. Beauty is not better than duty, and duty is not better than beauty. Each is the right thing at some moment and the wrong thing at another.

This is what makes choosing hard. If one layer were always most important, there would be nothing to decide. The weights change with the moment. Wisdom is not a ranked list. It is the ability to read a particular moment and see which layer is calling for him now.

### The tiger is not always the loud thing

There is a story in Luke that turns the tiger-and-kitten rule on its head. Jesus came to the house of two sisters. Martha was busy with the meal, the hosting, the dozen urgent tasks of a houseful of guests. Mary sat at Jesus's feet and listened. Martha complained, and Jesus answered: *"Martha, Martha, thou art careful and troubled about many things: But one thing is needful: and Mary hath chosen that good part, which shall not be taken away from her"* **(Luke 10:41–42)**.

Martha thought the meal was the tiger. It was a kitten. The tiger, the one thing that would not come again, was the Lord sitting in her living room, and her sister saw it.

This is the subtler lesson after kindergarten. The urgent thing is not always the important thing. The tiger is often quiet. A man's real tiger may be a marriage drifting apart slowly, while the loud thing demanding his attention is an email from work. The slow tiger is the most dangerous kind, because by the time it roars, it is too late to run.

### Live by beauty, die by beauty

Isak's line deserves its own space. A man who lives only for beauty, only for the experience of the moment, will be eaten. That is the danger I described in the Prologue: the man who saw everything at once and could not stop gazing. He mistakes appreciation for the whole of life, and the tiger finds him admiring the sunset.

But Isak saw the opposite error too, and he is right. A man who never stops to see beauty becomes ugly in what he does. He bulldozes the meadow because he never noticed it. He speaks harshly because he never heard how his words sounded. He works himself and everyone around him into the ground and calls it duty. A man must love the world to serve it well.

So the answer is not to choose action over beauty or beauty over action. It is to handle the tiger so that you can go back to the beauty, and to look at the beauty so that you remember what you are protecting from the tiger.

### Learning the weights

How does a man learn to read his moment rightly? The same way he learns anything else, as Part I described: by precept and by practice. Wise people tell him what usually matters most, and he practices choosing until the choosing becomes a habit. And he asks. *"If any of you lack wisdom, let him ask of God, that giveth to all men liberally, and upbraideth not; and it shall be given him"* **(James 1:5)**.

Two questions help me. The first is "What, if I leave it undone today, will cost the most tomorrow?" That is the tiger question. The second is "What will I wish I had noticed, when this moment is gone?" That is the beauty question. Most days, a man who asks both, honestly, will know what to do with the next hour.

But the size of the frame matters too. Seen from far enough away, nothing is urgent; seen from close enough, everything is. The next chapter is about the scale at which a man looks at his life.

## Chapter 7: Scales of Attention

*From a conversation between Thomas Lee Abshier, ND, and Isak, 30 October 2026. Edited for clarity by Claude.*

**THOMAS:** There's a lot of subtlety in all the patterns in a particular moment, and really it comes down to what scale you want to look at. Do you want the big scale? Everything just goes on: the Earth spins on its axis and goes around the sun, the sun goes around the galaxy, the galaxy moves with its group and its cluster, and things are fine. Nothing needs to be done. But oh, I actually have a tiger coming at me. Right now I need to deal with survival, or protecting others, or serving others. It comes down to which perspective of the moment you need to focus on, recognizing that there are different perspectives. There are large layers and small layers. There's a time of beauty and a time of work, a time of reflection and a time to take action.

**ISAK:** You can say, "The world is so tiny, and I'm in it, and we're all insignificant, so my life will just be about appreciating what we have." Or you can say, "This planet is really big. There are a lot of people on it, and some things need taking care of. I have a family to take care of, friends I want to take care of. I want people to be better educated. Let me take an action that brings the world closer to that vision." That's a different vision from the one you have standing on the beach saying, "Wow, that's beautiful." Maybe it's a balance of both.


---


### Every map has a scale

A map of the world and a trail map of a single canyon are both true. Neither is better. But a man who tries to find the trailhead with a globe will get lost, and a man who tries to plan a voyage across an ocean with a trail map will never leave port. Every map has a scale, printed in the corner, and the first thing a traveler should check is whether the scale fits the decision he is making.

A man's attention works the same way. He can look at his life at the scale of the cosmos, of a century, of a year, of a day, or of the next ten seconds. Each scale shows something real. Each hides something real. The art is to choose the scale that fits the moment.

### Zooming out

Stand back far enough and everything grows quiet. The Earth turns. The galaxy wheels. Empires rise and fall in the time it takes light to cross a corner of the sky. At that scale, the argument with a coworker vanishes, and so does the overdue bill.

There is great good in zooming out. It brings peace to a man who has been frantic over small things. It brings humility to a man who thought himself the center of the story. David stood under the night sky and felt it: *"When I consider thy heavens, the work of thy fingers, the moon and the stars, which thou hast ordained; What is man, that thou art mindful of him?"* **(Psalm 8:3–4)**.

But zooming out has its own danger, and Isak named it. Seen from far enough away, a man is insignificant, and it is a short step from "I am small" to "nothing I do matters." That is the voice of despair, and also of laziness dressed as wisdom. The man on the beach who says only, "Wow, it's beautiful," and goes home to do nothing, has mistaken the view from the mountain for permission to stay on it.

### Zooming in

Come close enough and everything is urgent. The tiger is ten feet away. The child is crying. The pot is boiling over. The email needs an answer now. At this scale, there is no beach and no galaxy; there is only the next thing.

Zooming in is how anything gets done. A surgeon does not contemplate the cosmos while he sutures. A father pulling his son out of the river does not think about the centuries. The near scale is where love becomes action: the meal cooked, the bolt tightened, the word spoken to the person in front of you.

Its danger is the opposite of the other. A man who lives only at the near scale becomes anxious and small. Everything is an emergency, so nothing has perspective. He fights every battle as though it were the last one, and he cannot tell a tiger from a kitten, because at a distance of one inch they both fill the whole view.

### God sees at every scale

The remarkable thing about God is that He does not have to choose. Psalm 147 puts the two scales in adjoining verses: *"He healeth the broken in heart, and bindeth up their wounds. He telleth the number of the stars; he calleth them all by their names"* **(Psalm 147:3–4)**. The same God who names every star binds up one man's broken heart.

Jesus said the same thing in the other direction: *"Are not two sparrows sold for a farthing? and one of them shall not fall on the ground without your Father. But the very hairs of your head are all numbered"* **(Matthew 10:29–30)**.

This matters for how we use the two scales. Zooming out should never make a man feel that he does not matter, because the God of the galaxies counts his hairs. And zooming in should never make him feel that the near thing is all there is, because the same God holds the galaxies. Both scales are true at once. We cannot hold both in view at the same moment, as God does, so we move between them.

### A time to every purpose

Scale applies to time as well as space. There is the scale of the hour, the day, the year, the life, and eternity. And at every scale of time, the right thing to be doing changes.

*"To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven: A time to be born, and a time to die; a time to plant, and a time to pluck up that which is planted; ... A time to weep, and a time to laugh; a time to mourn, and a time to dance; ... A time to keep silence, and a time to speak"* **(Ecclesiastes 3:1–2, 4, 7)**.

The Preacher does not say that planting is better than reaping, or that laughing is better than weeping. Each is right in its season. That is what I meant when I told Isak that beauty, work, reflection, and action each have their place, and one is not better than another. The question is never "Which is best?" It is "What season is this?"

### Choosing the scale

A few rules of thumb have served me.


 	- **In a crisis, zoom in.** When the tiger is coming, the galaxy can wait.

 	- **When choosing a direction, zoom out.** Decisions about marriage, vocation, and faith should be made from the mountain, not from the middle of a bad day.

 	- **When anxious, zoom out.** Most of what feels urgent at noon will not matter in a year.

 	- **When paralyzed, zoom in.** A man who cannot face the whole of his life can usually face the next hour, and the next right thing in it.

 	- **Begin and end the day at the far scale.** Prayer in the morning and at night is, among other things, a daily practice of zooming out: seeing the day from God's distance before entering it, and after leaving it.


Isak said maybe the answer is a balance of both, and he is right, but it is a moving balance. A man needs the far scale to know which way to walk and the near scale to take the next step. Chapter 8 turns from where a man looks to what he has to work with: the palette of his particular life.

## Chapter 8: The Palette and the Canvas

*From a conversation between Thomas Lee Abshier, ND, and Isak, 30 October 2026. Edited for clarity by Claude.*

**THOMAS:** The context of your life gives you the idea of what your options are, which tells you what you should probably spend time doing. Those are the colors you have to paint with, to create texture and drama and context. That's the palette of life you can paint with right now, given the circumstances you're in. If you're single, you don't have a spouse to spend time with, and spending all your time working is certainly an option. But there's a cost to that too, because you'll probably get tired of it; it all seems flat and shallow, or monotonous, or stressful. So you spend time with friends, with nature, learning different aspects of life you can blend in.

When new things come into your life, you have a new color to put on the palette. Or maybe the scene changes, and you take out an entirely different canvas, and you make the best picture you can, with the most enjoyment you can, with that canvas and that palette.

The metaphor we're developing is really a search for the proper way of framing life, holding life, doing life at each moment. We have an internal canvas we're painting. At each moment we have options for what we could be thinking, speaking, and doing, and those put color on the canvas, depending on what we focus on. Thought, speech, and action: each is a color, and those end up being what we experience, because we've put them onto the canvas of our consciousness. Consciousness is the canvas of our life. If we're not aware of something, we haven't put it onto our canvas. The sensory experience is the first imprint, and then we modify it by thinking.


---


### The canvas

Every man carries a canvas, and it is his consciousness. Whatever he is aware of is painted on it. Whatever he is not aware of is not there, however real it may be in the world.

The senses lay down the first coat: the light in the room, the voice on the phone, the ache in the knee. Then the mind goes to work on that first coat, the way an impressionist works over a sketch. It emphasizes some things and blurs others, adds memory here and expectation there, and gives the whole a mood. Two men in the same room have the same first coat and two very different paintings, because each worked over it differently.

This is what Chapter 1 meant by "what you bring." The moment hands every man the same raw material. The painting he makes of it is his own.

### The palette

A painter can only paint with the colors on his palette. No one would fault a man working in charcoal for leaving out the blue.

The palette of a life is set largely by its circumstances. A young single man has one set of colors: time, freedom, energy, and few people depending on him. A married man with small children has another: less time and freedom, and colors the single man does not have at all, such as a wife's companionship, a child's trust, and the weight of a household. An old man has still another: less energy, and the colors of memory, patience, and a long view. Illness takes some colors away. Wealth adds some and dims others. Each life has its own palette, and no two are the same.

Jesus's parable of the talents begins with this fact: *"And unto one he gave five talents, to another two, and to another one; to every man according to his several ability"* **(Matthew 25:15)**. The master did not judge the servants by how much they were given. He judged them by what they did with it. The servant with two talents who doubled them received the same praise as the servant with five.

So the first discipline of the palette is to stop comparing it. A man who spends his life envying another man's colors will never paint with his own.

### Three brushes: thought, speech, and action

If the canvas is consciousness and the palette is the set of colors a life offers, the brushes are three: thought, speech, and action.

Thought paints only on a man's own canvas, but it paints constantly, and it sets the tone for everything else. Speech paints on two canvases at once, his own and his hearer's. A word spoken in a marriage is a stroke on two canvases, and it may stay there for years. *"Death and life are in the power of the tongue"* **(Proverbs 18:21)**. Action paints on the world itself: the meal cooked, the house built, the check written, the wrong done or undone.

Scripture addresses all three. It tells us what to think about **(Philippians 4:8)**, how to speak **(Ephesians 4:29)**, and how to act **(Micah 6:8)**. A man who guards only one brush and lets the other two run wild will not make a good painting.

### The single palette and the married palette

When I told Isak that a single man can spend all his time working, I was describing a real freedom. Paul saw it too: *"He that is unmarried careth for the things that belong to the Lord, how he may please the Lord: But he that is married careth for the things that are of the world, how he may please his wife"* **(1 Corinthians 7:32–33)**. The single man has colors the married man has given up.

But every palette tempts a man to paint with one color only. The single man who paints only with work ends with a canvas that is flat and gray: productive, perhaps, but monotonous, and often stressful. He needs the other colors his life offers: friends, nature, learning, worship, service. The married man faces the same temptation from the other side. Work can crowd out the wife and children he has been given, which is the divorce Chapter 5 warned about. Whatever the palette, a canvas painted in one color is a poor picture.

### New colors and new canvases

Palettes change. A man marries, and a color he never had appears. A child is born, and another. Then a parent dies, or health fails, or a job ends, and colors disappear. Sometimes the change is so large that it is not a new color at all but a new canvas: a move across the country, a widowing, a conversion, retirement. The old painting is finished whether the painter was ready or not, and a blank surface waits.

The temptation at such moments is to keep trying to paint the old picture with colors that are no longer there. The widower who keeps setting two plates. The retired man who keeps going to the office. The better way is the one Paul learned: *"I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content. I know both how to be abased, and I know how to abound"* **(Philippians 4:11–12)**. Contentment is not resignation. It is the skill of painting the best picture possible with the palette actually in hand.

### Guarding the canvas

Because whatever a man attends to goes onto his canvas, attention is the most important choice he makes. A man who spends three hours a night on outrage and pornography has painted those colors over everything else, whatever he believes. A man who spends the same hours with his family, with Scripture, or with good work has painted a different picture, though he lives in the same house and the same town.

*"Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it are the issues of life"* **(Proverbs 4:23)**. And Paul tells us which colors to choose: *"whatsoever things are true, whatsoever things are honest, whatsoever things are just, whatsoever things are pure, whatsoever things are lovely, whatsoever things are of good report; if there be any virtue, and if there be any praise, think on these things"* **(Philippians 4:8)**.

The good news is that a canvas can be painted over. A man who has spent years painting in dark colors can begin, today, to lay lighter ones on top. That is part of what repentance means. The old picture does not have to be the final one.

The canvas is inward. But a man does not live only inside his own head. He looks out at the world, and he walks out into it. The next chapter is about the windows he looks through and the doors he walks through.

## Chapter 9: Windows and Doors

*From a conversation between Thomas Lee Abshier, ND, and Isak, 30 October 2026. Edited for clarity by Claude.*

**THOMAS:** Say I'm in a room, and the door frame reminds me of the doors of life and the windows of the soul. I can think about each of them. What's outside that window? I can go through different doors, and something different is happening behind every door I open and outside every window I look through. Each window has a different frame. What do I want to create outside that window?

Then there's another person with me. What am I going to do with that person? We talk about what we saw out the windows and doors, how that life could be, and how we could walk from where we are to a place where we can create a new reality. So we spend our time sharing window visions and door actions. Having had a planning session, we say, "Okay, let's go out and do it." We take action, and it helps somebody else, because the world we acted in made life easier for that person. He's thankful, and that makes us feel good. And we've served God, because He experienced that better life happening for the person we served.

Life is a series of pictures at an exhibition, the Emerson, Lake and Palmer way of looking at it. We appreciate each one. We take action and make it better; we relate and enjoy relating; we go out and create; we rest in between, and we go out and do it some more.

What's the point of all the talk, all the reading, all the self-help literature, all the economic analysis? Each of them is aimed in a different direction for action. Reading is only preparation. It's locker-room time, where the coach is telling you what to do. We're locker-rooming it right now. The actual living of life is when you have the screwdriver in your hand and the bolt is turning, or when there's another person in front of you, words are spoken, and his mind is moving to a new place because of your words. Your screwdriver, in that case, is words, turning the bolts of another person's neurons.


---


### Windows

Every room has windows, and every moment does too. A window is a place where a man looks out of where he is toward what could be: a better marriage, a healthier body, a business that serves people well, a neighborhood where children are safe, a church that lives what it preaches.

Looking out a window is not daydreaming. It is vision, and vision is a God-given capacity. *"Where there is no vision, the people perish"* **(Proverbs 29:18)**. A man who never looks out a window lives inside his room as if it were the whole world. Everything he does is maintenance; nothing he does is building.

Different windows frame different views. From the kitchen window, a man may see his family; from the office window, his work; from the window of prayer, the kingdom of God. Part of wisdom is knowing which window to stand at, and the scale from Chapter 7 applies here too: some windows look across the street, and some look across a lifetime.

### Doors

A window shows. A door lets a man walk through. Vision without action is only a view; action without vision is only motion. The two belong together.

Doors open and close. Paul wrote, *"For a great door and effectual is opened unto me, and there are many adversaries"* **(1 Corinthians 16:9)**. He saw an opening, knew it would not stay open forever, and went through it despite the opposition. Many men stand at an open door for years, admiring what is on the other side, until it closes. Others rush through every door they see without first looking out the window to learn where it leads. The first man wastes his vision; the second wastes his life.

The sequence matters: look through the window, then walk through the door. See what could be, then go and do the thing that makes it so.

### The locker room and the field

All the reading a man does, all the sermons he hears, all the counsel he receives, all the planning: that is locker-room time. The coach draws the plays on the board, and the team learns them. It is necessary. A team that skips the locker room loses.

But no game is won in the locker room. The game is on the field, where the screwdriver is in a man's hand and the bolt is turning, or where another person is in front of him and his words are moving that person's mind. The locker room exists for the sake of the field.

Chapter 3 described men who chase glimpses. There are also men who live in the locker room: forever reading one more book, attending one more seminar, refining one more plan, and never taking the field. *"Be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves"* **(James 1:22)**. Reading this book is locker-room time. It is worth something only if it changes what a man does on Monday.

### Words are tools

One of the most powerful things a man does on the field is speak. When he speaks to another person, his words go in through that person's ears and change the pattern of his thoughts. In the most literal sense I know, words turn the bolts of another man's neurons. A sentence can loosen a fear that has been rusted tight for years, or strip the threads of a friendship.

That is why Scripture treats speech as action, not as mere talk: *"Let no corrupt communication proceed out of your mouth, but that which is good to the use of edifying, that it may minister grace unto the hearers"* **(Ephesians 4:29)**. *Edifying* means building. A good word is a tool used well.

### Looking out the window together

Vision is better shared. Two men at a window see more than one, because each notices what the other misses. That is what Isak and I do in our conversations, and what every good marriage, partnership, and fellowship does: stand at the window together, talk about what could be, and plan how to get there. *"Without counsel purposes are disappointed: but in the multitude of counsellors they are established"* **(Proverbs 15:22)**. *"Two are better than one; because they have a good reward for their labour"* **(Ecclesiastes 4:9)**.

The planning session ends, though, and the two men go out the door. A plan that is never carried out was only a conversation.

### The circle of joy

When the action is done well, something wonderful happens. The person helped is glad. His life is easier, or safer, or more beautiful because of what was done. His gladness comes back to the one who helped him, and he is glad too. And I believe God experiences both.

This is central to how I understand the world. God is not watching our lives from a distance. He inhabits them; we are, in a real sense, His experience of His creation. When I make another man's life better, God experiences that better life from inside the man I served, and He experiences my joy in serving from inside me. Jesus said as much: *"Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me"* **(Matthew 25:40)**.

This is why service is not a grim duty. It completes a circle: the person served, the servant, and God, each glad.

### Pictures at an exhibition

In 1874 the Russian composer Modest Mussorgsky wrote a suite of piano pieces called *Pictures at an Exhibition*, inspired by a memorial show of a friend's paintings. Each piece depicts one painting. Between them is a recurring theme he called the *Promenade*: the visitor walking from one picture to the next. Emerson, Lake and Palmer brought it to a rock audience in 1971.

I think that is a fair picture of life. A man walks into a room. He looks at what is there and appreciates it. He looks out the window and sees what could be. He goes through a door and does something about it. He relates to the people he finds, and enjoys them. He rests. Then the promenade begins again, and he walks into the next room.

Scripture gives a great deal of guidance on how to do each of these well: how to relate to people, to things, to work, to substances, to rest. A man who follows it, appreciating, acting, relating, creating, and resting each in its season, will find that life is good. Not easy, but good.

There is one more thing about doors, though, and it is the subject of the next chapter. Every door a man walks through closes others behind him.

## Chapter 10: Cause, Effect, and Consequence

*From conversations between Thomas Lee Abshier, ND, and Isak, 30 and 23 October 2026. Edited for clarity by Claude.*

**ISAK:** We look back fondly on older experiences, and we don't realize in the moment that those are the moments that leave a legacy on us and on other people. We're doing everything in the moment, but there are after-effects that keep existing. The ball keeps rolling. If we walk around blind to what's possible, we can create chaos or misfortune for the people around us without even thinking about it, just because we say, "That's not my battle." But it's still people.

If we choose one route, another one goes away. So it's gaining awareness of what's possible, and then asking not just what's possible but what's right. It's like looking ahead on a chessboard. I could drive drunk from the bar tonight and get home fine and see my kids. Or I could drive drunk and kill somebody, and my kids never see me again, because I'm in jail or I'm dead. Both start with getting in the car. Then you can ask what's actually putting you in that situation. If the goal was to see your kids more, you shouldn't be in the bar drinking.

So it's not a blanket "this is good and this is bad." It's "What will this action do? If I changed my life, what would the other effects be?" You get good at identifying the possibilities and then playing them out. With more information and more colors come more combinations. To quote Spider-Man, with great power comes great responsibility.

**ISAK** (23 October): History shows you the way the world goes. It doesn't repeat exactly, but it does a cover version. The more you learn about the patterns of behavior, the more you can predict.


---


### The ball keeps rolling

Every act goes on after it is done. A word spoken to a child at breakfast is still working in him at forty. A lie told to close a sale shapes how the customer trusts the next salesman. A kindness to a stranger may be the reason he does not give up that night. Isak put it simply: we act in the moment, but the ball keeps rolling.

Most of these effects are invisible to the man who started them. He will never know about the child at forty, the customer, or the stranger. That is why a man cannot judge his acts only by what he sees come of them. He has to judge them by what they are likely to set in motion, including what he will never see.

Scripture says the effects run across generations. God visits *"the iniquity of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation of them that hate me; And shewing mercy unto thousands of them that love me, and keep my commandments"* **(Exodus 20:5–6)**. Notice the proportion. The harm runs three or four generations; the mercy runs to thousands. Good, too, keeps rolling, and it rolls farther.

### Thinking moves ahead

A good chess player does not look only at the move in front of him. He asks, "If I do this, what will he do, and then what will I do?" Isak's example of the bar shows why this matters in life.

The drunk man standing by his car at midnight faces a choice with two very different endings. But the choice that really mattered was made hours earlier, when he sat down at the bar instead of going home. By the time he reaches the car, the dangerous move has already been played. The time to see the danger was three moves back.

This is true of almost every disaster. The affair begins with a lunch. The bankruptcy begins with a purchase that seemed small. The addiction begins with a choice that seemed harmless. *"There is a way which seemeth right unto a man, but the end thereof are the ways of death"* **(Proverbs 14:12)**. The way seems right because the end is out of sight. Thinking ahead brings the end into view while there is still time to choose a different way.

Isak's point about history belongs here too. A man who studies how things have gone before, in his own family, in his community, in the history of nations, learns the patterns. He does not need to touch the stove to know it is hot, and he does not need to repeat his father's mistakes to know where they lead.

### Sowing and reaping

*"Be not deceived; God is not mocked: for whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap"* **(Galatians 6:7)**.

This is not a threat. It is a description of how the world is built. Consequences are not arbitrary punishments added afterward by an angry God. They are built into the nature of things, the way a crop is built into a seed. A man who sows distrust reaps suspicion. A man who sows faithfulness reaps a trust he can lean on when he is old. *"Can a man take fire in his bosom, and his clothes not be burned?"* **(Proverbs 6:27)**.

This is one reason I trust the Bible's moral law. It is not a list of arbitrary rules. It is a map of where the roads go, drawn by the One who laid them out.

### Every door closes others

Isak said something easy to miss: if we choose one route, another one goes away. Chapter 9 ended on the same point. Every door a man walks through closes others behind him.

This is not a defect in life. It is what makes choices real. A man who marries one woman gives up all the others; that is what makes the marriage a gift and not a trial. A man who gives his twenties to learning a trade gives up whatever else he might have done with them. A man who serves God cannot also serve himself as his first master. *"Choose you this day whom ye will serve"* **(Joshua 24:15)**. Choosing is always also refusing.

The man who never chooses, keeping every door open, does not keep his options. He loses them, one by one, as the doors close on their own.

### Counting the cost

Jesus told His listeners to think before following Him: *"For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost, whether he have sufficient to finish it?"* **(Luke 14:28)**. Counting the cost is not timidity. It is how a builder finishes what he starts.

A few questions help a man count the cost before a significant choice:


 	- If I do this, what is likely to happen next, and after that?

 	- Who else will this touch, now and later?

 	- What doors does this close?

 	- What is the worst likely ending, and could I live with it?

 	- Have I seen this pattern before, in my life or in someone else's?


None of these require genius. They require only the habit of looking three moves ahead.

### More colors, more responsibility

Isak quoted Spider-Man, but Jesus said it first: *"For unto whomsoever much is given, of him shall be much required"* **(Luke 12:48)**. The more a man sees, the more he is responsible for. A man who has never been told that his anger wounds his children is less guilty than a man who has been told and keeps shouting. *"Therefore to him that knoweth to do good, and doeth it not, to him it is sin"* **(James 4:17)**.

This is a sobering thought for anyone reading a book like this one. Every chapter adds colors to the palette and possibilities to the board. It also adds responsibility.

### The harm we do by passing by

Isak named one more kind of consequence: the harm done by doing nothing. "That's not my battle," a man says, and walks on. In the parable of the Good Samaritan, the priest and the Levite did nothing wrong in the ordinary sense. They did not rob the man in the ditch. They simply *"passed by on the other side"* **(Luke 10:31–32)**. Jesus held them up as examples of failure. Not acting is a choice, and it has consequences too.

### Mercy and the chain

If consequences are built into the world, is a man who has sown badly simply doomed to reap? Scripture gives an honest answer. Forgiveness is real, and it is complete in the matter that matters most: a man's standing with God. When David repented of his adultery and murder, Nathan told him, *"The LORD also hath put away thy sin; thou shalt not die"* **(2 Samuel 12:13)**. But in the next breath he told David that the child would die. Forgiveness did not undo every natural consequence.

And yet God does something greater than undoing. He weaves even the consequences of our worst choices into His purposes: *"And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God"* **(Romans 8:28)**. David's next son by Bathsheba was Solomon.

That is the end of Part II. A man must choose (Chapter 6), at the right scale (Chapter 7), with the palette he has (Chapter 8), looking out and then walking through (Chapter 9), counting what each choice sets in motion (Chapter 10). But one question has been waiting underneath all of this. When we say an outcome is good or bad, by what standard do we say it? Part III takes up that question.

## Chapter 11: The Standard

### The question underneath

Part II used the words *good* and *bad* on nearly every page. Handle the tiger; that is good. Drive drunk; that is bad. Serve your neighbor; that is good. But underneath every one of those judgments sat a question this book has not yet answered: good by what standard?

Our culture has an answer ready. Good is what you feel is good. You have your truth and I have mine. Everyone should be free to define good for himself, so long as he does not hurt anyone else.

That answer collapses as soon as it meets a real case. Who decides what counts as hurt? Everyone, it turns out, does believe in an absolute standard somewhere. As I told Isak in *Transforming America*, everybody wants good roads. Nobody wants a road with chasms in it, or boulders across it. The only people who want bad roads are highway robbers. Ask a relativist whether it would be fine for someone to steal his car, and he becomes an absolutist on the spot.

So the question is not whether there is a standard. It is whose.

### Good is what God delights in

My answer is that good is what God delights in. God made the world in order to experience it, and He experiences it from inside every one of us. When we act in a way He loves, He experiences that as good. When we act in a way He hates, He experiences that too, and grieves.

We can know the difference because He made us in His image, and our sense of good resonates with His. Paul says even people without the Scriptures *"shew the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience also bearing witness"* **(Romans 2:15)**. That is the compass Isak described: a sense, trained on something beyond himself, that tells him "That was bad" before he has worked out why.

But a compass can be thrown off. Put a magnet near it, and it points the wrong way while looking just as confident. Our culture has put a great many magnets near the conscience. It has even turned the words around: "bad" means good, "sick" means excellent, "wicked" means admirable. Isaiah saw it twenty-seven centuries ago: *"Woe unto them that call evil good, and good evil; that put darkness for light, and light for darkness"* **(Isaiah 5:20)**. A conscience needs a fixed reference to check against, and that is what God's revealed word is for.

### Two kingdoms, two standards

A man can switch standards. He can say, as I put it to Isak, that a chainsaw massacre was a fine night's entertainment. That is not a different opinion within the same standard. It is a different standard altogether: a different lord, a different kingdom, a different idea of what makes an experience good.

This is what Jesus meant when He said, *"No man can serve two masters"* **(Matthew 6:24)**. Every man serves some master. The real choice is which one. And each kingdom produces its own kind of life: one tends toward heaven, the other toward hell, and both begin here.

### Love is not enough by itself

For a long time I thought the whole standard could be summed up in one question: would my neighbor feel loved by this act? I still think that question points the right way. But in October I realized it misses the center.

Love without a standard becomes whatever feels good to the one receiving it. A father who gives his son everything he asks for may make the boy feel loved, and ruin him. A friend who never tells you a hard truth may make you feel loved, and let you walk off a cliff. Scripture is blunt about this: *"Thou shalt not hate thy brother in thine heart: thou shalt in any wise rebuke thy neighbour, and not suffer sin upon him"* **(Leviticus 19:17)**. To humor a man in his sin is not love. It is a kind of hatred wearing love's clothes.

That is why the R in the ERA framework I run on is *Righteousness*. Love must be directed by a standard of right and wrong, and that standard comes from God's character, not from human consensus.

### The ordered test

So I now ask three questions, in order.


 	- **Is it righteous?** Does it agree with what God has established as right? This comes first, because God comes before the neighbor. If the act is not righteous, it is not loving, however it feels.

 	- **If it is righteous, is it loving?** Many things are permitted that are not kind. An act can be lawful and still be cruel, and Christ's love does not stop at what is lawful.

 	- **Would I want it done to me?** *"Therefore all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them: for this is the law and the prophets"* **(Matthew 7:12)**. But the "me" here is not my momentary appetite. It is the self committed to righteousness, my soul seen from eternity. A drunk man may want another drink poured for him. His sober, eternal self does not.


The order matters, because each question catches what the others miss. Humoring a neighbor in his sin fails the first question, though it may pass the third in a shallow way. Exposing a man's private shame to the world fails the second: it may be truthful, even righteous in a narrow sense, and still be cruel. A public execution turned into entertainment fails the second and third, even where justice was done. Only an act that passes all three is good in the full sense.

### Calibrated by Christ

The three questions need a calibration, the way a scale needs a known weight to check against. That weight is Christ. *"A new commandment I give unto you, That ye love one another; as I have loved you"* **(John 13:34)**. When I am unsure what love requires, I ask how He loved: truthfully, without flattery; firmly, without cruelty; at His own cost, not at others'. People thought "What would Jesus do?" was a trite slogan. It is the truth, and in many situations it is the only question a man needs.

### Gray areas within the way

Isak once told me he saw only gray. My answer was that gray is what you get when you mix black and white. It does not prove black and white do not exist.

Still, honest believers do disagree about how the standard applies in particular cases: how to spend a Sunday, how much to drink, how to vote, how to raise children in a hard culture. For these, Scripture gives a method rather than a ruling. Go deeper into the text. Look at the fruit each choice produces. Reason together before acting: *"Come now, and let us reason together, saith the LORD"* **(Isaiah 1:18)**. And where the matter is truly disputable, extend freedom: *"Let every man be fully persuaded in his own mind"* **(Romans 14:5)**. I hold my positions on such things firmly, and I hold them open to a better argument.

### The standard judges me first

One more thing must be said before Part III goes on to judge religions and ideologies. The standard applies to the one holding it before it applies to anyone else. *"Thou hypocrite, first cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye"* **(Matthew 7:5)**.

Every test in the chapters ahead will be turned on Christians and on the history of the church as well as outward. A standard that only ever condemns other people is not a standard. It is a weapon. The next chapter takes up the tests themselves: how a man can tell whether the voice behind a religion is from God.

### Why tests are needed

If every spiritual voice spoke truth, no one would need to test anything. But Scripture assumes the opposite: *"Beloved, believe not every spirit, but try the spirits whether they are of God: because many false prophets are gone out into the world"* **(1 John 4:1)**. And it warns that the most dangerous voices will not look dangerous: *"for Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light"* **(2 Corinthians 11:14)**.

That is why the counterfeit Isak and I discussed is so hard to detect. A counterfeit bill made of newspaper fools no one. The one that fools the bank is printed on the right paper, with the right ink, and differs from the real thing in a single detail. A false religion made entirely of lies would collapse. The one that lasts is mostly true, with one thing changed.

### They cannot all be true

It is fashionable to say that all religions are paths up the same mountain. That is a generous thought, and it is not possible. The religions do not merely describe the mountain differently; they contradict one another about the mountain. The New Testament says Jesus is the Son of God, that He died on the cross, and that He rose. The Quran says God has no son and that Jesus was not crucified **(Quran 4:157, 4:171)**. Both cannot be true. Sincerity does not settle it; men have been sincerely wrong about everything.

So a man who wants to know where the roads lead has no choice but to test them. Here are the tests I use. Four of them anyone can apply to any religion, including mine. One belongs to Christians in particular, and I will say so when I get to it.

### First test: prediction

The Torah gives the oldest test: *"When a prophet speaketh in the name of the LORD, if the thing follow not, nor come to pass, that is the thing which the LORD hath not spoken, but the prophet hath spoken it presumptuously"* **(Deuteronomy 18:22)**. A voice that claims to speak for God and predicts what does not happen has disqualified itself. This is falsification, three thousand years before the philosophers named it.

### Second test: does the revelation rebuke the one who receives it?

The Bible gives a second test that is never stated as a rule but appears on nearly every page. Genuine revelation from God rebukes the man who receives it.

Nathan walks into David's court and says, *"Thou art the man"* **(2 Samuel 12:7)**. David, who could have had him killed, writes Psalm 51 instead. The Gospels, written by Peter's own church, record every one of Peter's denials. Paul writes that *"when Peter was come to Antioch, I withstood him to the face, because he was to be blamed"* **(Galatians 2:11)**. Moses is barred from the promised land for his disobedience, and the book that records it was attributed to him. The prophets of Israel delivered messages they did not want to deliver, against kings who could kill them.

Now consider a revelation that consistently serves the man who receives it: giving him what he wants, settling his quarrels in his favor, granting him privileges denied to others. That pattern does not prove fraud. But it is the opposite of the biblical pattern, and it should make a careful man ask questions. In *Islam as Religion and Political Ideology*, I traced how Danny Burmawi, raised a devout Muslim, came to this test by reading Islam's own sources, and how a remark of Aisha preserved in those sources noticed the same pattern. Chapter 14 returns to it.

### Third test: one voice or many

Religions differ in where their authority comes from, and the difference is more like a spectrum than a line. At one end are religions whose authoritative content comes from a single human medium: one prophet, one guru, one revelator, whose report the community organizes around. At the other end is the Bible: sixty-six books, dozens of authors, many genres, across roughly four thousand years, all bearing witness to one God whose work culminates in Christ.

The single-voice religion can only be validated from within. Its credibility rests entirely on the credibility of one man, and the follower's spiritual life is in a real sense handed over to the spirit speaking through that man. The many-voiced canon can be checked from outside, against fulfilled prophecy, against history its writers reported as eyewitnesses, against the conscience written in every heart, and against the testimony of the saints across twenty centuries.

This is what Isak saw when he said the authority had been changed. A religion that moves its authority from the finished witness to a living office can rewrite itself every generation, and the follower has no fixed ground to stand on to object.

This test must be applied inward too, and on a second axis. Separate from where a religion's sources come from is what its institutions do after the founder dies. An institution can be more controlling than the founder ever was. Catholicism's multi-voiced canon was overlaid for centuries by a magisterium that was highly controlling, and the Inquisition is part of the record. Isak's worry about organized religion as a system of control is, on this axis, a fair worry, and it applies to churches as well as to anyone else.

### Fourth test: liberty

When I evaluate any tradition, including the Christian denominations, I ask two questions. Does it bring a man into a reconciled relationship with God? And does it limit his liberty no more than that reconciliation requires?

God made the world so that free creatures could choose to love Him. A religion that loads its followers with rules beyond what God requires, or that makes their standing before God depend on endless religious labor, works against the purpose of creation. *"Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free, and be not entangled again with the yoke of bondage"* **(Galatians 5:1)**. *"Where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is liberty"* **(2 Corinthians 3:17)**. A spirit that steadily narrows a man's freedom and binds him to another man's direction is not behaving like the Spirit of the Lord.

### Fifth test: who is sacrificed?

Isak supplied the fifth test, and it may be the plainest. Does the voice ask you to sacrifice yourself for others, or to sacrifice others for yourself?

At the center of Christianity is God sacrificing Himself for the people who killed Him. *"Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends"* **(John 15:13)**. Christ *"made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant"* **(Philippians 2:7)**. A system whose distinctive teachings require others to be taxed, subdued, or punished for their belief, so that the system may spread and endure, is running in the opposite direction.

This is the test of fruit Jesus gave: *"Every good tree bringeth forth good fruit; but a corrupt tree bringeth forth evil fruit"* **(Matthew 7:17)**. A tree is judged by what grows when it is allowed to grow according to its own nature.

### The tests turned inward

I promised in Chapter 11 that every test would be turned on the church. Here is the plain result: Christians have failed these tests many times. Men acting in Christ's name have sacrificed others for themselves. They tortured in the Inquisition, divided the world for conquest under papal decree, and enslaved and justified it from the pulpit. A Muslim sheikh who visited our fellowship in May pressed exactly this point, and he was right about the history.

But notice what the tests measure. They measure a religion by its own standard: what it teaches its followers to be. When a Christian tortures a heretic, he is not following his standard. He is violating it, and Christ's own words condemn him. The question for any religion is whether its atrocities are departures from its founder's teaching and example, or applications of them. That distinction is not a dodge. It is the difference between a disease and a design. Chapter 18 takes up what it means for the church to be judged first.

### A Christian test, and common ground

There is one more test I use, and it is fair to say which kind it is. John wrote, *"Every spirit that confesseth that Jesus Christ is come in the flesh is of God: And every spirit that confesseth not that Jesus Christ is come in the flesh is not of God"* **(1 John 4:2–3)**. Paul wrote that even an angel preaching another gospel should be refused **(Galatians 1:8)**.

These are tests for a man who already accepts the apostles' witness. A Muslim does not, and he can run the same kind of test in reverse, judging the Bible by the Quran. So I do not offer this test to a Muslim as proof. I offer it as an honest statement of how a Christian judges, and I rely on the other four, which do not assume my conclusion, for the common ground where a Christian and a Muslim can reason together.

### What would change my mind

Isak has asked me a fair question: what evidence could change my mind? A belief that no evidence could touch is not a conviction; it is a fortress.

Paul answered for every Christian: *"And if Christ be not risen, then is our preaching vain, and your faith is also vain"* **(1 Corinthians 15:14)**. The Christian faith rests on a public event that could, in principle, be disproved. Find the body, and Christianity falls. Show that the prophecies did not come to pass, that the eyewitnesses were not eyewitnesses, or that Christ's teaching, faithfully followed, produces cruelty and bondage rather than love and liberty, and Christianity fails its own tests. I have looked at those questions for forty years, and the evidence has run the other way. But the door is not welded shut. A man should hold any conviction that way: firmly, on the evidence, and open to a better argument.

The tests sort the roads. The next chapter describes, in the simplest terms I know, the two directions they sort them into.

## Chapter 13: Two Rivers

*From "The Mapmaker and the Wanderer."*

A woman named Maren, who ran a school for children, came into the shop looking for something she couldn't name.

"My students are confused," she said. "Half of them say there is no right road. The other half say their road is the only one. Both groups are angry."

"That's because neither group has a map," said Ezra. He pulled out a second drawing, smaller and simpler. It showed two rivers flowing in opposite directions through the same valley. ### Two directions

Under all the variety of human lives, there are finally only two directions a life can run: toward God or away from Him. Moses put it to Israel at the end of his life: *"I call heaven and earth to record this day against you, that I have set before you life and death, blessing and cursing: therefore choose life"* **(Deuteronomy 30:19)**. Jesus put it as two roads: *"Wide is the gate, and broad is the way, that leadeth to destruction ... strait is the gate, and narrow is the way, which leadeth unto life"* **(Matthew 7:13–14)**.

The first river flows toward self-giving love, truthfulness, faithfulness in marriage, forgiveness, honest work, voluntary generosity, and humility. The second flows toward self-serving, deception, sexual license, grudges, exploitation, and pride. Every man has eddies of both in him. But each life, and each culture, has a main current, and it runs one way or the other.

### Why there is turbulence

In physics, a fluid moving smoothly in one direction is said to flow in a *laminar* way: in layers that slide past each other without mixing violently. Put two currents through the same channel in opposite directions, and the flow turns *turbulent*: eddies, whirlpools, chaotic mixing, lost energy. The turbulence is not caused by bad water. It is caused by opposed directions in a shared space.

That is the best explanation I know for the turmoil of our time. A marriage in which one spouse is trying to keep his vows and the other has decided not to is turbulent, however polite they are. A school where half the teachers are teaching children that marriage, sex, and the body have a God-given shape, and the other half that they do not, is turbulent. A nation whose laws run one direction and whose culture runs the other is turbulent. Much of the anger in our public life is simply the friction of two rivers in one valley.

### The one thing

Why do men choose the river running away from God? Often it is not because they have weighed the whole Bible and found it wanting. It is because the Bible names one thing they love as sin. A man's whole identity may be built around that one thing: his desires, his friends, his politics, his buttons. When the Bible says it is wrong, he does not adjust the one thing. He throws out the whole book, and then organizes his life against it.

The rich young ruler is the pattern. He had kept the commandments from his youth, and Jesus loved him. Then Jesus said, *"One thing thou lackest"* **(Mark 10:21)**, and named it: his wealth. *"And he was sad at that saying, and went away grieved: for he had great possessions"* **(Mark 10:22)**. One thing, and he walked away from everything else.

The honest way to test the one thing is to step back and look at the whole pattern. Does the Bible's way of life, taken as a whole, produce good marriages, sound children, honest dealings, and peaceful communities? If it does, then the one point where I disagree with it is probably an outlier, and I am probably the one who is wrong about it. The thing I loved because it felt good may turn out to have carried a great deal of harm with it that I did not want to see.

### Peace is not stillness

Maren's fear was that one direction means tyranny. But Ezra's answer is the right one: the rivers are not forced, they choose. And the peace of the right river is not stillness.

A river flowing toward the sea still has rapids, rocks, shallows, and falls. Life lived God's way is not free of hardship. *"In the world ye shall have tribulation: but be of good cheer; I have overcome the world"* **(John 16:33)**. What changes is not the presence of obstacles but the direction of the flow, and so the meaning of the obstacles. Rapids on the right river build strength. Rapids on the wrong river only speed the wreck.

That is what I meant when I told Isak we are not trying to get rid of chaos. Chaos is built into creation; there are endless ways things can go. The goal is to change the parameters, so that the chaos oscillates around a new center instead of the old one. God Himself described the peace He intended in exactly these terms: *"O that thou hadst hearkened to my commandments! then had thy peace been as a river, and thy righteousness as the waves of the sea"* **(Isaiah 48:18)**. Peace like a river: moving, alive, sometimes rough, and going somewhere.

### Showing where the sea is

How does a river change direction? Not by being forced. A government cannot command a man's heart to flow toward God, and a church that tries to do so becomes the tyranny Maren feared. A river changes direction when it sees where the sea is.

That means the work is showing, not forcing: living the right direction visibly, so that others can see where it goes; telling the truth about where each river ends; and reasoning patiently with those who are flowing the other way. It also means remembering what Isak has pressed on me more than once. The people in the other river are not the enemy. Many of them were born into its current and have never been shown another. They are children of God, and they are owed the map.

### Which river am I in?

The hardest use of this chapter is the inward one. It is easy to see the other river. It is harder to notice the places where my own life has been running the wrong direction while I was sure I was in the right river: the grudge kept past sundown, the dishonest shortcut, the pride that calls itself conviction. Every Christian has eddies of the other current in him. The question is not whether I am pure water. It is which way my main current runs, and whether I am letting God turn the eddies.

### Where I stand

I stand on this judgment: the revelation Muhammad received did not come from the God of Israel and of Jesus Christ, and the political program that classical Islam built on it cannot be reconciled with a free constitutional republic. ### The room and the sofa

Danny Burmawi was raised a devout Muslim in Jordan and set out to master Islam in order to defend it. The more he studied its own books, the more he concluded it was not true, and he came to Christ at great cost. He has a picture I find clarifying: *Islam is not a religion; Islam has a religion.* Think of a house. In one room there is a sofa: prayer, fasting, charity, devotion to God. That room is the religion that Islam has. But the house has other rooms, and in them is a complete program for law, the state, war, and the place of non-Muslims in society.

The First Amendment protects the room with the sofa: the relation between a man's soul and God, and the practices that express it. It does not protect a program to replace the Constitution simply because that program calls itself a religion. That distinction matters for everything that follows. My quarrel is not with the Muslim at prayer. It is with the claim that the voice behind the whole house is God's, and with the political rooms built on that claim.

### Two voices in one book

The Quran was recited over roughly twenty-three years. In the first period, at Mecca, Muhammad was a persecuted preacher with few followers, and the verses from that period are largely devotional and tolerant: *"There shall be no compulsion in religion"* **(Quran 2:256)**. In the second period, at Medina, he was the ruler of a state with an army, and the tone changes. The best known verse of the later period reads: *"Fight those who do not believe in Allah or in the Last Day ... from among those who were given the Scripture, until they give the jizyah willingly while they are humbled"* **(Quran 9:29)**.

When two verses conflict, which governs? The Quran itself provides for later revelation replacing earlier: *"We do not abrogate a verse or cause it to be forgotten except that We bring forth one better than it or similar to it"* **(Quran 2:106)**. Classical Muslim scholars built a whole discipline on this principle, called *naskh*, and the classical Sunni majority held that the militant verses of the Medinan period, especially in Sura 9, supersede many earlier, more accommodating ones.

Here is the first area of uncertainty. Muslim scholars are divided on whether verses like 9:5 and 9:29 were bound to Muhammad's particular wars or stand as permanent commands. Reformist scholars argue that only defensive war is permitted, and a sheikh who visited our fellowship in May read the verses that way. I welcome that reading wherever it is held. My claim is narrower and, I think, hard to dispute: enough of the classical tradition read these verses as standing commands that they shaped Islamic law for centuries, and they remain available to anyone who wants to read them that way now.

### Applying the tests

Chapter 12 set out five tests. Here is how four of them bear on Islam.

**Does the revelation rebuke the one who receives it?** Burmawi says that as he read the Quran, the biography of Muhammad, and the hadith, he found that whenever Muhammad needed something, a revelation arrived to provide it: settling a quarrel in his household, granting him marital privileges denied to other men, deciding disputes in his favor. The Bible's pattern runs the other way; its prophets are rebuked by the God they speak for. One remark preserved in Islam's most trusted hadith collection noticed the pattern. When a verse came giving Muhammad latitude among his wives **(Quran 33:51)**, his wife Aisha said, *"I feel that your Lord hastens in fulfilling your wishes and desires"* **(Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of Tafsir, on Quran 33:51)**.

Fairness requires two notes. Muslims preserved this remark themselves, which speaks to the honesty of the transmitters. And Muslim scholars read it as an affectionate remark, not a charge of fraud; Aisha remained devoted to Muhammad for the rest of her life. I do not rest anything on one sentence. The evidence is the pattern, and Aisha's remark is evidence that the pattern was visible from inside the house.

**One voice or many?** The Quran came through one man. Its authority rests entirely on his report of what the angel told him, and there is no independent witness against which to check it. Later, the jurists built on that single voice a body of law far more detailed and binding than anything in the Quran itself. That is the shape Chapter 12 warned about: a single medium, and an institution that grew stronger than the founder.

**Liberty.** The Quran prescribes no worldly penalty for leaving Islam. But a hadith in Bukhari reports Muhammad saying, *"Whoever changes his religion, kill him"* **(Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of Jihad)**, and the classical schools of law adopted the death penalty for apostasy. A number of Muslim-majority states still punish apostasy or blasphemy today, and many Muslim scholars and leaders now reject that reading. A religion that once a man is in, he may not leave, fails the liberty test, wherever it is practiced that way.

**Who is sacrificed?** At the center of Christianity, God dies for His enemies. At the center of the classical Islamic order is a structure in which unbelievers are fought, and Christians and Jews under Muslim rule pay a tax and accept a subordinate status. Burmawi adds an observation from inside: in a system of ten thousand commandments with hellfire threatened on every page, the one certain assurance of paradise the text offers is to die fighting in the way of Allah **(Quran 9:111)**. He calls it *sola jihad*, in deliberate contrast with *sola fide*. Christianity says God paid the debt, at His own cost, and the life that follows is gratitude. The difference is the difference between a Father and a master who threatens.

### The history, honestly

If the tests are fair, they must be fair to the history too. The dhimmi system, under which Christians and Jews lived in Muslim lands, was real, and it was hierarchical and often humiliating. It was also, in some times and places, more tolerant than medieval Christendom was of Jews. ### A claim of restoration

The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints does not present itself as one more Christian denomination. It presents itself as the only true church on earth, restored after a Great Apostasy in which, it teaches, no true church existed at all. In 1830 Joseph Smith, then twenty-four, published the Book of Mormon and organized the church with six members. The church now reports more than seventeen million.

That claim deserves to be taken seriously, because it is a serious claim. If the church of the apostles truly lost something necessary for salvation, and God restored it through Joseph Smith, then every Christian should become a Latter-day Saint. If nothing necessary was lost, then the restoration is not a restoration. It is a second authority set beside the first.

### The record

The historical record of Mormonism's origins raises hard questions, and many of them are now acknowledged by the church itself.

In March 1826, four years before the Book of Mormon appeared, Joseph Smith stood before a justice of the peace in Bainbridge, New York, charged as a disorderly person in connection with treasure-digging. A farmer named Josiah Stowell had hired him to find buried silver by looking into a seer stone placed in a hat. No official transcript survives; the main account is a recollection published in 1877, and historians debate its details. But nearly everyone agrees the hearing took place. The same stone-in-a-hat method, by the accounts of those present, was how the Book of Mormon was later dictated; the church has published photographs of the stone.

The papyri from which Smith said he translated the Book of Abraham have since been identified by Egyptologists as ordinary funerary texts. The church acknowledges that Smith gave several differing accounts of his First Vision. And for more than a century the church barred men of African descent from its priesthood, until a revelation in 1978 ended the ban; in 2013 it formally disavowed the theories once taught to justify it.

The pattern is worth noticing. Each of these facts would have toppled a smaller claim. The institution absorbed them and kept growing. A story that builds a world around itself, with real costs for leaving and real rewards for staying, does not collapse the way a hoax does. It adjusts.

### Fraud, or something else?

Scott Lively has argued that Mormonism is the most successful fraud in American history: the Cardiff Giant or the Piltdown Man, but with an institution behind it. I understand the argument, and the record gives it weight.

My own view is slightly different. I do not think Joseph Smith made it all up. When I worked through Mormonism's history and theology with our fellowship, what struck me was not how easy it was to refute but how hard. If it were an obvious fraud, its weak points would be easy to find. Its strength is that it is so nearly true: the God of the Bible, the fall, Christ, the atonement, the call to righteous living, all present and taught with devotion. That is the signature of the counterfeit Chapter 12 described. You do not fill a counterfeit with lies. You fill it with truth and change one thing: who is speaking.

I therefore conclude that the angel who appeared to Joseph Smith was not sent by God. That is a theological judgment, made by the test Paul gave: *"But though we, or an angel from heaven, preach any other gospel unto you than that which we have preached unto you, let him be accursed"* **(Galatians 1:8)**. A Latter-day Saint will reject it, and I respect his right to.

### What was missing?

The fairest way to test a restoration is to ask what, specifically, it restored.

Was the gospel of grace through faith missing? No. The New Testament preserves it plainly: *"For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God"* **(Ephesians 2:8)**. Was Christ's atoning death and resurrection missing? No; Paul calls it the gospel of first importance **(1 Corinthians 15:3–4)**. Was the indwelling Holy Spirit missing? No; Jesus promised Him and Acts records His coming **(John 14:16–17; Acts 2:1–4)**. Baptism? The Lord's Supper? Prayer? Both Testaments give them in full.

What the restoration supplied instead were additions: the doctrine of exaltation, in which faithful men may become gods; three degrees of glory in the afterlife **(Doctrine and Covenants 76)**; the temple endowment, whose ceremonies closely resemble the Masonic rites Smith had joined shortly before he introduced them; eternal marriage sealed in the temple; and, for a time, plural marriage. None of these restores something the apostles lost. Each adds something the apostles did not teach. Jude wrote of *"the faith which was once delivered unto the saints"* **(Jude 3)**. Once.

### The changed authority

Isak saw the structural heart of it in a single phrase: Joseph Smith changed the authority. In historic Christianity, authority rests in Christ and the completed witness of the apostles. In Mormonism, it rests in a living prophet, and in every prophet who succeeds him.

That difference is not abstract. It means the road can be changed from the top. In 1890 a revelation ended the practice of plural marriage, which earlier revelation had commanded. In 1978 a revelation ended the priesthood ban. Many Christians welcomed both changes, and so do I. But the method that produced them is the problem. A church whose doctrine can be revised by its president's revelation has no fixed ground on which a member can stand and say, "This is wrong." It is the single-voice structure of Chapter 12: an authority that can be validated only from within.

### The test of liberty

A brother in our own fellowship follows Denver Snuffer, a former Latter-day Saint who reports that he, too, has met Christ and received the same restoration Joseph Smith received. The Snufferite movement has almost no institution and exercises little control over its members, which is to its credit. But it still rests the believer's spiritual life on the testimony of single men, now two of them, and on a body of commandments beyond what the Bible requires.

That is the liberty test at work. The question is not whether the Mormon or the Snufferite is devout; many are more disciplined than most Christians I know. It is whether the additional revelation gives him a fuller life with God, or a narrower one, bound by a layer of rules the apostles did not lay on him. *"Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free"* **(Galatians 5:1)**.

### Good people on another road

Isak said it, and I agree: the Mormons we know are good people. They are often more faithful in prayer, more generous with time and money, and more devoted to family than their Protestant neighbors. A Latter-day Saint whose first allegiance is to Christ is my brother in Christ, whatever secondary commitments I cannot share. He is to be loved as the Lord loves him.

Love does not require pretending to agree. The claim to be the one restored church, holding an authority the rest of Christianity lost, is a claim with consequences, and saying nothing about it would be neither honest nor loving. But persuasion, where it comes, will come through long friendship and the work of the Spirit, not through one sharp conversation. Our task is to bear witness and leave the harvest to the Lord of the harvest.

### Turned inward

Isak's deeper worry is that every religion that grows big does what Jesus rebuked: makes a rulebook, builds an institution, and draws a line between the insiders and everyone else. He is right that this happens, and it is not only a Mormon problem. Christian churches have had corrupt clergy, abusive structures, doctrinal drift, and alliances with worldly power. The Reformation was, in part, a response to exactly those failures.

So the Christian's witness to a Latter-day Saint is not the witness of a perfect tradition to a flawed one. It is the witness of imperfect believers to an unrepealed gospel. The gospel is what matters. The institutions that carry it are its servants, and they stand under it.

### "Who's to say?"

The third road offered to Americans today is not a religion in the usual sense. It has no prophet and no scripture. Its creed is a question: *Who's to say what's right?* Everyone has his own truth. Every way of life is valid. The only sin is to judge.

In *Transforming America* I called this the worldview without a center. It sounds humble. It dresses itself in care for others, in fairness, in letting people be. It seems so reasonable that it can be harder to see than the other roads. A man who would never follow a prophet who claimed golden plates will follow this road without noticing he has chosen it, because it does not feel like a choice. It feels like the absence of one.

### The case at its strongest

This road deserves to be stated at its best, because it has real goods in it, and its followers are often moved by real moral concern.

Its best instinct is compassion for people who have been mistreated. It looks at those the powerful have pushed aside, the poor, minorities, people whose desires the majority found strange, and says they deserve dignity. That instinct is biblical. The prophets thundered against Israel for crushing the poor **(Amos 2:6–7)**, and Jesus ate with tax collectors and sinners. Christians have sometimes been cruel to people they considered sinners, and that cruelty has driven many into this road. Its second instinct is liberty: a man should be free to live as he chooses so long as he harms no one. That too has a deep root in the American founding.

Isak feels the pull of these instincts, and I do not think less of him for it. A Christian who cannot see what is good in this road will not be able to speak to the people on it.

### Why it cannot hold

The trouble is not with its compassion or its love of liberty. It is that it has no center to hold them in place.

"So long as he harms no one" sounds like a standard, but it hides the real question: what counts as harm? Is it harm to end the life of a child in the womb? Harm to give a confused thirteen-year-old drugs that will stop his puberty? Harm to raise a child without a father? The "no harm" principle cannot answer, because it has no account of what a human being is or what he is for. So the question is settled by whoever has the power to settle it. A worldview that begins with "Who's to say?" ends with "We say," and it enforces its answer as firmly as any church ever did.

Scripture describes such a time: *"In those days there was no king in Israel: every man did that which was right in his own eyes"* **(Judges 21:25)**. The book of Judges is the record of what that produced. It was not freedom. It was chaos, followed by a hunger for a strong man to end the chaos.

### The platform and the faith

I have been plain that I think the Democratic Party's platform has become the political expression of this road. Its 2024 platform pledged national legislation to restore abortion rights, to repeal the long-standing ban on federal funding of abortion, to pass the Equality Act, and to "vigorously oppose state and federal bans on gender-affirming health care" ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/on-lgbt-issues-both-parties-move-left), [Angelus News](https://angelusnews.com/?p=140965)). On life, sex, and marriage, it has taken the opposite side from the Bible.

That is why I told Isak that "I'm a Christian" and "I'm a Democrat" do not fit easily in one sentence. But I want to say exactly what I mean, as I did when I first wrote it down. A registered Democrat is not necessarily a man who has embraced every plank. My claim is about standing for the platform, not about the hidden state of any particular voter's soul. Many Democrats are faithful Christians who vote as they do out of concern for the poor, or for workers, or from family habit. My argument is with what the platform stands for, and I put it to them as a question to weigh, not a verdict on their salvation.

The other party does not get a pass. The 2024 Republican platform softened its long-standing language on abortion ([Christian Post](https://www.christianpost.com/voices/the-2024-democratic-platform-is-more-extreme-than-the-draft.html)), and the Republican tradition has its own versions of the worldview without a center, especially in its libertarian wing, which I think may be the most dangerous form of it because it seems so right. Neither party is the kingdom of God. That is why the campaign's motto is *No King but King Jesus*.

### One center, many nations

The road without a center calls its creed multiculturalism, and the word hides two different things.

One is the variety of human cultures: their languages, food, music, customs, and ways of keeping house. That variety is good, and God loves it. John's vision of heaven is gloriously multicultural: *"a great multitude, which no man could number, of all nations, and kindreds, and people, and tongues, stood before the throne, and before the Lamb"* **(Revelation 7:9)**. Notice where they are standing. Every nation and tongue, and one throne.

The other thing the word hides is the claim that every way of life is equally valid, that no belief or practice may be called wrong, and that to call it wrong is itself the only sin. That is not the variety of cultures. It is the loss of a center. When I told Isak that America has become multicultural in a way that embraces what God calls evil and calls it personal rights, this is the second meaning I was naming. A nation can welcome every culture and still refuse to honor every departure from God as a right. Heaven does.

### What is missing

In *Transforming America* I said that most of what this road wants is good, and I still think so. Most of what people want to do is right. Most of what they feel is good. About the only thing that needs to be added is this: there is a God, and some things really are right, and a few of the things we enjoy are not.

That one addition changes everything, because it puts a center back in the circle. Isak's father, Charlie, once told me of a moment when he was studying child development, sat down on a bench, and had the sense that all the wisdom of the universe opened up to him in a single word: *family*. Behind it was a beauty and wisdom he could only call eternal. That is the kind of center the road without one is missing. It is not a list of prohibitions. It is a picture of what human life is for, and once a man has seen it, the prohibitions make sense as the fences around it.

### The people on this road

The people on this road are not the enemy. Many of them came to it fleeing a hard and loveless religion. Many were raised in it and have never been shown another. They are children of God, and Isak is right to keep reminding me of it. The way to reach them is the way of Chapter 13: show them where the sea is. Live the center visibly. Tell the truth about where the road without one leads. And answer their best instincts, compassion and liberty, with the One who is the source of both.

### There is a war

Many people dislike any talk of war in matters of faith, and I understand why. The history of religious wars is ugly. But Scripture does speak of a war, and it is careful to say what kind. *"For we wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against the rulers of the darkness of this world, against spiritual wickedness in high places"* **(Ephesians 6:12)**.

### Defense is never aggression

Here is the principle I have come to. When good resists evil, the war is always defensive. When evil moves against good, it is always the aggressor. A man who stops a murderer is not committing violence in the same sense the murderer is. A nation that resists invasion is not the moral twin of the invader.

This is the line between the two chessboards, society and state, which Chapter 14 introduced and Chapter 21 takes up. The state carries the sword, and it carries it for defense: to restrain those who violate the life, liberty, and property of others **(Romans 13:4)**. It does not carry the sword against ideas. No government should punish a man for believing what is false.

But against ideas, the church and every Christian are called to go on the offense, with weapons of a different kind. *"For though we walk in the flesh, we do not war after the flesh: (For the weapons of our warfare are not carnal, but mighty through God to the pulling down of strong holds;) Casting down imaginations, and every high thing that exalteth itself against the knowledge of God, and bringing into captivity every thought to the obedience of Christ"* **(2 Corinthians 10:3–5)**. The strongholds are thoughts. The weapons are truth, argument, testimony, prayer, and example. The captives are thoughts, brought home to Christ.

That is the only offensive war a Christian may wage against evil: a war of words, openly declared, fought for hearts.

### Johnny and the bully

The story of Johnny is about the confusion that makes people unable to see any of this. The teacher sees one boy hit another and stops there. Violence happened; violence is bad; both boys are guilty. She has no way to tell the difference between the bully's fist and Johnny's, because she has no standard beyond "no fighting."

Scripture does have a standard: *"Deliver the poor and needy: rid them out of the hand of the wicked"* **(Psalm 82:4)**. Johnny protected Sally from a bully. That was right, and he deserves a medal, not a suspension.

Notice, though, what Johnny did and did not do. He stopped a boy who was hurting someone. He did not punch the boy who believed something wrong. The physical defense of the weak against violence is one thing. The contest of ideas is another, and it is fought only with words. A Christian who confuses the two, and reaches for force against a false belief, has stopped being Johnny and become the bully.

### Not neutral

The teacher's mistake is the mistake of a great many decent people. They see the conflict between the two rivers of Chapter 13 and conclude that the problem is the conflict itself. "Why can't everyone just get along?" It sounds like peace, but it is a peace with no content. Jeremiah condemned the prophets of his day for exactly this: *"They have healed also the hurt of the daughter of my people slightly, saying, Peace, peace; when there is no peace"* **(Jeremiah 6:14)**.

Real peace is not a truce between right and wrong. It is everyone flowing the same direction. That cannot be imposed, but it can be offered, and offering it requires saying plainly that one direction is right and the other is wrong. Neutrality on that question is not a third side. It is not knowing which side you are on.

### Love your enemy

Jesus gave the hardest command in this war: *"Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you, and persecute you"* **(Matthew 5:44)**.

Isak is right that this is easier in conversation than in the trenches. But the war of words is the place where it can actually be done. You cannot love a man while you are trying to destroy him. You can love him while you are trying to persuade him, because persuading him means wanting what is best for him: that he would see the truth and be free.

Isak is also right about the innocents. In every country ruled by a false creed, most of the people did not choose the rulers or write the laws. Many would welcome another way if they saw it. Our sympathy for them is good, and it should never curdle into contempt for whole peoples. But sympathy must not become an excuse to stop telling the truth. Chapter 11 said it: to humor a man in his error is not love **(Leviticus 19:17)**. The kindest thing a Christian can do for someone caught in the middle is to show him, gently and clearly, which way is home.

### How words win

The goal of the war of words is not to win an argument. It is the moment I described to Isak: "You're right, brother. I was wrong." That is *metanoia*, a changed mind, and only God can finally give it. Paul describes the posture that makes room for it: *"And the servant of the Lord must not strive; but be gentle unto all men, apt to teach, patient, In meekness instructing those that oppose themselves; if God peradventure will give them repentance to the acknowledging of the truth"* **(2 Timothy 2:24–25)**.

That is a remarkable list for a war: gentle, patient, meek. It is the opposite of the shouting that passes for public argument today. The Christian is to be *"ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you with meekness and fear"* **(1 Peter 3:15)**, and to speak *"the truth in love"* **(Ephesians 4:15)**. Truth without love is a club. Love without truth is a sedative. The war of words needs both.

### The war inside

The first front in this war is not out in the culture. It is inside every Christian. Isak read me a passage from Ephesians in the same conversation, about putting off the old man and putting on the new **(Ephesians 4:22–24)**, the passage Chapter 4 explored. Paul says the same elsewhere: *"For the flesh lusteth against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh"* **(Galatians 5:17)**.

Isak added an observation about drama that I have thought about since. Drama is fun when you are watching it: the fight video, the gossip, the feud between other people. Then you share the video, and someone's friends come after you, and suddenly it is your drama, and you are at the bottom of the pile. The road away from God is more exciting; I will not deny it. It has more highs and lows. But the excitement is a roller coaster without a seat belt. The road toward God has a steadier baseline, what Isak called the undisturbed baseline of sobriety and godliness, and it is the only place a man can stand still long enough to speak truth to anyone else.

A Christian who wants to fight the war of words must first win it in himself. And that brings Part III to its last chapter, which turns every test in this book back on the people who hold it.

### Isak's question

Throughout these conversations Isak has pressed one question harder than any other: can I turn my tools inward? I have used the five tests, the counterfeit analysis, and the study of men who crave power to examine Islam, Mormonism, and the road without a center. Applied to the history of the church, what do they show?

That is a fair question, and this book would be dishonest without an answer to it. Chapter 11 promised that the standard would judge its holders first. This chapter keeps the promise.

### Where judgment begins

Peter wrote it plainly: *"For the time is come that judgment must begin at the house of God: and if it first begin at us, what shall the end be of them that obey not the gospel of God?"* **(1 Peter 4:17)**.

The prophet Amos built a sermon around this principle, and it is a trap worth walking into. He begins by pronouncing God's judgment on Israel's neighbors, one after another: Damascus, Gaza, Tyre, Edom, Ammon, Moab **(Amos 1:3–2:3)**. His listeners must have nodded at each one. Then he turns to Judah, and then, for the longest indictment of all, to Israel itself: *"because they sold the righteous for silver, and the poor for a pair of shoes"* **(Amos 2:6)**. The nations were judged. God's own people were judged most.

The Hebrew prophets spent far more of their breath on the sins of Israel than on the sins of the nations around it. A Christian who reads them honestly cannot spend all his breath on the sins of other religions.

### The power-seekers in the pulpit

In our 23 October conversation I described a type of man who rises to the top of corrupt systems: one who craves power, cannot feel what others feel, and loves himself above everyone. Psychologists call the combination the dark triad: Machiavellianism, psychopathy, and narcissism. I applied it to the leaders of Islamist movements. Isak was right to ask whether it applies to Christian leaders too.

It does. The New Testament itself names one: *"I wrote unto the church: but Diotrephes, who loveth to have the preeminence among them, receiveth us not"* **(3 John 9)**. He refused the apostle, slandered him, and threw faithful believers out of the church. The type was in the church before the last apostle died.

It has never left. The record of the last generation alone includes the sexual abuse of children by Catholic clergy, covered up by bishops across several countries; a 2022 independent report on the leaders of the Southern Baptist Convention, which found that abuse survivors had been ignored and stonewalled for years; and preachers who grew rich promising wealth to the poor. The Inquisition, the wars of religion, and the defense of slavery from American pulpits belong to the same record. Saul, who held the anointing and tried to kill the man God had chosen to replace him, is the biblical pattern for all of it.

So the answer to Isak's question is yes. The tools work inward, and what they find is ugly. A man who wants power will use whatever vehicle is at hand, and a church is as useful a vehicle as any.

### A disease, not the design

Is there any difference, then, between the church and the systems this book has criticized? I believe there is, but the difference must be stated carefully, because it can sound like an excuse.

I have said that Christianity done wrong is not Christianity; it is a counterfeit wearing the cross. Isak's reviewers pointed out that this can sound like the old trick of defining away every bad example: "no true Christian would do that." The way to avoid the trick is to tie the judgment to something fixed and public: the recorded teaching and example of the founder.

Jesus's teaching on this point is not ambiguous. When James and John wanted to call down fire on a Samaritan village that refused to receive Him, *"he turned, and rebuked them, and said, Ye know not what manner of spirit ye are of. For the Son of man is not come to destroy men's lives, but to save them"* **(Luke 9:55–56)**. When Peter drew a sword to defend Him, He said, *"Put up again thy sword into his place"* **(Matthew 26:52)**. He told His followers to love their enemies, turn the other cheek, and serve rather than rule **(Mark 10:42–45)**. And He went to His own death rather than raise a hand.

So when a Christian tortures a heretic or enslaves a man, he is not applying his founder's example. He is violating it, and anyone can open the Gospels and see the violation. That is what I mean by a disease rather than a design. The question for every religion, including mine, is the same: when its followers do evil, are they departing from what their founder taught and did, or carrying it out? The test is public, and it cuts in every direction.

That does not excuse the church. A disease can still kill. It only tells us where the cure is: not in a better institution, but in a return to the Founder.

### Why some leave

The sharpest report on the church's condition comes from those who leave it. In October our fellowship discussed an article about Christians in the West converting to Islam. Many commenters answered with a verse: *"They went out from us, but they were not of us"* **(1 John 2:19)**. Good riddance.

The numbers call for proportion. The best American data, from the Pew Research Center's 2017 survey of U.S. Muslims, found that about a quarter of Muslim adults in America are converts, and that about half of those were Protestant before converting. But the same survey found that about a quarter of those raised Muslim no longer identify as Muslim, so Islam in America loses about as many people as it gains. This is not a flood. It is a small stream, and it is instructive because Islam is the one destination that asks *more* of a man than the church he left.

What does the convert find? A rhythm of prayer five times a day. A standard stated out loud, on drink, dress, sex, and food. A clear and honored role for men. A community that notices when he is absent. A faith that claims the whole of his life without apology. Every one of those belonged to the church first. Daniel prayed three times a day **(Daniel 6:10)**. The early Christian manual called the *Didache* told believers to pray the Lord's Prayer three times daily. Fasting, Sabbath, catechism, church discipline, and fathers leading family worship were all ours. We let them lapse.

The man who leaves for the mosque is not fleeing holiness. He is looking for it. He has left a soft counterfeit and found a hard one. The church's error was not that it preached grace. It preached a grace that taught nothing. Scripture describes a different grace: *"For the grace of God that bringeth salvation hath appeared to all men, Teaching us that, denying ungodliness and worldly lusts, we should live soberly, righteously, and godly, in this present world"* **(Titus 2:11–12)**. A grace that teaches. A free gift that changes the one who receives it.

### The shepherds, not the sheep

The "good riddance" answer takes one truth and uses it to cancel another. The man who left is responsible for leaving. But the community he left is responsible for what he found there before he left. When God indicted Israel's leaders through Ezekiel, He did not blame the scattered sheep: *"The diseased have ye not strengthened, neither have ye healed that which was sick ... neither have ye brought again that which was driven away, neither have ye sought that which was lost"* **(Ezekiel 34:4)**. And Jesus's shepherd does not say good riddance. He leaves the ninety-nine and goes looking for the one **(Luke 15:4)**.

### Remember from whence thou art fallen

The seven churches of Revelation stood in what is now Turkey, a Muslim-majority nation. To the first of them Jesus said, *"Remember therefore from whence thou art fallen, and repent, and do the first works; or else I will come unto thee quickly, and will remove thy candlestick out of his place"* **(Revelation 2:5)**. The candlesticks were removed. Islam was the instrument, but the cause was churches that had stopped doing the first works.

In 634, Sophronius, the patriarch of Jerusalem, preached on Christmas Day to a congregation that could not travel the five miles to Bethlehem, because Arab raiders held the road. He named the invaders for what they were. And he told his people that the enemy's success was owed to their own sins, and called them to repent. He held both truths together: the threat outside was real, and the hardest scrutiny belonged inside. Those who keep only the first end in rage. Those who keep only the second end in surrender.

In Hagia Sophia, the great church of Constantinople that became a mosque, the Christian mosaics were not chiseled away. They are curtained during prayers. The West's Christian inheritance, its law, hospitals, universities, and the teaching that every man bears God's image, is still on the ceiling. We stopped looking up.

### If my people

The promise for this condition is old, and it is addressed to the church, not to the nation's enemies: *"If my people, which are called by my name, shall humble themselves, and pray, and seek my face, and turn from their wicked ways; then will I hear from heaven, and will forgive their sin, and will heal their land"* **(2 Chronicles 7:14)**.

The practical meaning is not mysterious. Fathers who disciple their children. Marriages that keep covenant. Sexual holiness that is taught and expected. A rhythm of prayer. Believers who can explain what they believe and why it is true, to a thoughtful young man weighing Islam across a table. Churches that notice when someone is missing and go looking for him. Leaders who are watched, and removed when they abuse their trust.

That is the end of Part III. A standard exists (Chapter 11). Religions can be tested (Chapter 12). The roads run two directions (Chapter 13). Three roads offered today fail the tests (Chapters 14–16). The contest between them is fought with words (Chapter 17). And the first place every test applies is the house of God (Chapter 18). Part IV turns to what a man does with all of this: how he lives from the center, starting with his own uncertain faith.

## Part IV: Living from the Center

*From "The Mapmaker and the Wanderer."*

Months passed. Ezra kept working on his map. Eli kept visiting. Maren sent her students. Other townspeople came, some curious, some hostile, some just lonely.

One afternoon Ezra set down his pen and said something he had been thinking about for a long time. "I've been counseling people for forty years. Individuals, couples, families. "That's a testimony, Eli. If it can happen to you, it can happen to anyone. The road doesn't change. The map doesn't change. What changes is that one person at a time decides to walk it."

## Chapter 19: Faith Outpaces Knowledge

### More faith than knowledge

Isak's sentence is one of the most honest a believer can say: *I have more faith than I do knowledge.* I said in reply that it is true of everyone, including me, and I meant it. I have studied for forty years, and my knowledge is still small next to what I trust.

That is not a confession of weakness. It is the normal condition of a creature. *"Now faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen"* **(Hebrews 11:1)**. If we could see everything, we would not need faith; we would simply know. A finite mind in an infinite world will always have to trust beyond what it can prove. The only question is what it trusts.

### Hope and faith

Hope and faith are often used as if they meant the same thing. They do not. Hope says, "I wish it were true." Faith says, "I am going to act as though it is, pay what it costs, and keep holding it until it becomes real."

Abraham is the pattern: *"By faith Abraham, when he was called to go out into a place which he should after receive for an inheritance, obeyed; and he went out, not knowing whither he went"* **(Hebrews 11:8)**. He did not know the destination. He knew the One who called him, and he walked. That is why James says faith without works is dead **(James 2:17)**. Faith is not a feeling about the future. It is a way of walking toward it.

This connects with Part I. A glimpse is not a change, and neither is a hope. Faith is the decision that turns a glimpse into a road.

### Faith needs some knowledge

If faith always outruns knowledge, why bother with knowledge at all? Why write a book of arguments?

Because faith needs something to rest on. A man cannot have faith in nothing. He needs enough knowledge to see that the object of his faith is worthy of it. Most people who reject Christ are not rejecting the real Christ. They are rejecting obstacles: a caricature they were taught, a hypocrite who hurt them, a scientific claim they think disproves Him, a question they think has no answer. Knowledge does not create faith, but it can clear those obstacles away. Then faith can do its own work.

That is the purpose of most of this book. It is not to argue anyone into the kingdom; no one gets in that way. It is to remove the boulders from the road, so a man can see where it goes and decide whether to walk it.

### The confusion is part of the design

Why is the truth so hard to find? Why did God allow a world with five roads, a thousand voices, and so much noise?

I told Isak that confusion is built into the construction of a universe that had to be. A real creation must have real choices, and real choices require real alternatives. If the truth were as obvious as the sunrise, choosing it would be no choice at all, and love that cannot choose is not love. So the truth is a treasure that must be sought: *"Again, the kingdom of heaven is like unto a merchant man, seeking goodly pearls: Who, when he had found one pearl of great price, went and sold all that he had, and bought it"* **(Matthew 13:45–46)**.

Solomon saw the same design and saw dignity in it: *"It is the glory of God to conceal a thing: but the honour of kings is to search out a matter"* **(Proverbs 25:2)**. The search is not a punishment. It is the work that makes a man a king. Chapter 22 returns to that.

### Unfairness and faith

Isak told me something that surprised me. He said he had come to peace with the unfairness of the world. Life is not fair by our judgments, and he no longer takes that as evidence against God. He remembered the disciples asking Jesus whose sin had made a man born blind, and Jesus's answer: *"Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents: but that the works of God should be made manifest in him"* **(John 9:3)**. He has seen God more plainly in people who went through hardship and still praised Him than in people who never suffered.

"It doesn't have to be fair for there to be a Creator," he said. "A perfectly fair world sounds boring, and I'd probably have no character." I agree. A world in which good and bad things can happen is the only kind of world in which character can be formed. Unfairness is not proof that God is absent. It is part of the terrain on which faith is tested and grown.

### The people who never heard

There is one form of the unfairness question that Isak has pressed hardest, and I owe him an answer here: what about the billions who have lived and died without ever hearing of Jesus? I worked through it at length in *Transforming America*, Chapter 15. Here is where I have come to rest.

Two truths must be held together. The first is that there is only one way. *"I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me"* **(John 14:6)**. I hear that first as a statement of how reality is built: there is one door, not many. The second is that the One who is the door has been speaking to every human heart all along. He is *"the true Light, which lighteth every man that cometh into the world"* **(John 1:9)**. Whatever is true and good in any tradition came from Him. A seeker in any land who turns toward the light he has is turning toward Christ, whether or not he can yet name Him.

Cornelius shows what God does with such a seeker. He was a Roman officer who had never heard the gospel, but he feared God, gave generously, and prayed. God did not tell him he was fine as he was. He sent an angel to tell him to send for Peter, and gave Peter a vision to prepare him to go **(Acts 10)**. The seeker who responds to the light he has is not left where he is.

What about those who never heard in time? Here I stop, on purpose. I am certain of one thing: *"Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?"* **(Genesis 18:25)**. God will not condemn a man for failing a test he was never given. There are threads in Scripture that lean toward a wider hope than the tradition has usually allowed, and I hold them as hope. I will not preach them as a promise, because a man who is told he will certainly arrive has been given a reason not to walk. We are not appointed to rule on the hard cases. We are appointed to go.

And America has its own unreached. They are the millions who have heard the name of Jesus all their lives, as a curse word, a political brand, or their grandmother's religion, and have never once heard the gospel. They are in some ways harder to reach than a man who has never heard the name, because they think they already know what it means.

### Doubt as a disciple's companion

Isak has compared himself to Peter: a real believer, afraid that when the moment comes he will deny the One he loves. I think the comparison is apt, and it is not a bad one to be stuck with. Peter doubted and sank, denied and wept, and was restored on a beach over breakfast. He became a rock not because he was certain but because he kept coming back.

There is a prayer in the Gospels that every honest believer has prayed. A father brought his son to Jesus and said, *"Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief"* **(Mark 9:24)**. Jesus healed the boy. He did not require the father to settle his doubts first. He required him to come.

Isak put his own aim well: to keep questioning without letting the questions undermine what he already knows to be true. That is the right posture for anyone holding a map he is still learning to read. Faith outpaces knowledge, and should. But a faith that keeps walking will find its knowledge growing to meet it.

The next chapter is about what happened when one person did keep walking.

### Eighteen months

For about a year and a half, Isak and I have been in conversation. The conversations have covered physics, economics, politics, Islam, Mormonism, sexuality, the Bible, the meaning of life, and whatever else came up. This book is built from some of them. *Transforming America* was built from others.

Isak is a young man with a sharp mind and an honest heart. When we began, he was skeptical of nearly everything: both political parties, capitalism, organized religion, and certainty itself. He had grown up in church and gone through the motions. He described himself as seeing only gray.

I am telling this story with his permission, using his first name. I tell it because it is the reason I believe the rest of this book is not only true but useful.

### What changed

The changes are best given in his own words. His conversations with other people have grown deeper, and when they have questions, he has answers. He feels he is bringing value to the people around him, that he is "a better person for them to be around," and no longer "one of the people they shouldn't be around." He is far more at peace. Sobriety, which was once hard, comes much more easily. And the change has spread: friends of his are drinking less because he is.

Underneath those visible changes is something he said that moved me more than any of them: "I want other people to experience what I've experienced." A man who wants to give away what he has received has received something real.

### What did not change

Honesty requires saying what has not changed too. Isak still has more questions than answers. He still finds the world full of noise, and calls it a constant battle for his mind. His faith is real, and so is his uncertainty. He told me that life gets more confusing as he understands how many parts of it he never knew about.

I do not count any of that against the change. It is what growth looks like in a thinking man. A transformation that ended all questions would be suspicious. A transformation that makes a man brave enough to keep asking them, while living better in the meantime, is the kind I trust.

### A testimony, not a proof

One man's story does not prove a method works for everyone. Isak is one data point, and anyone trained in science knows what one data point can and cannot show. It cannot show that the change will happen to everyone, or how often, or under what conditions.

What it can show is that the change is possible. Before the first data point, the question is whether the thing can happen at all. After it, the question is only how to make it happen more often. That is why the moment Isak said "I think I got it" mattered so much to me. It moved the question from *whether* to *how*.

Scripture calls this kind of evidence a testimony. The man born blind, questioned by the Pharisees, could not answer their theology. He said only, *"One thing I know, that, whereas I was blind, now I see"* **(John 9:25)**. A testimony is not an argument; it is a report. And Scripture puts great weight on it: *"And they overcame him by the blood of the Lamb, and by the word of their testimony"* **(Revelation 12:11)**.

### Content or relationship?

Isak has asked a fair question about his own change: was it the ideas, or was it the friendship? Would any patient mentor, of any faith, have produced the same result?

My reading is that what changed him was not mainly my example as a friend. It was the reframing of life we worked through together: looking at philosophy, politics, economics, and theology, and tying all of it back to the Bible, until the pieces of his world began to fit into a single picture with Christ at the center. The ideas did the work.

But I do not think content and relationship can be separated as cleanly as the question suggests. Truth travels best through relationship. Jesus did not hand His disciples a book. He called twelve men *"that they should be with him, and that he might send them forth to preach"* **(Mark 3:14)**. Paul wrote to the Thessalonians that he had given them *"not the gospel of God only, but also our own souls, because ye were dear unto us"* **(1 Thessalonians 2:8)**. The relationship is the vehicle; the truth is the cargo. A vehicle with no cargo produces a follower of the teacher. Cargo with no vehicle rarely arrives at all.

That is also why the goal is not that Isak hears me when he speaks. It is that he hears Christ, and that the people he talks with hear Christ through him. A teacher who makes copies of himself has failed. A teacher who helps a man find his own way to the Lord has done his job.

### Ripples

The most telling detail in Isak's account is not about him. It is about his friends drinking less. Nobody lectured them. They simply watched someone they knew change, asked him about it, and adjusted their own lives.

That is how change has always spread. *"Iron sharpeneth iron; so a man sharpeneth the countenance of his friend"* **(Proverbs 27:17)**. A changed life is a window through which other people can see a door they had not noticed. Chapter 9 said that a man looks out the window and then walks through the door. Sometimes the window he looks through is another man.

### Twelve, and their twelve

If one person can change, the question becomes how to help the next one, and the next. The answer is not mysterious. Jesus showed it. He poured Himself into twelve, who poured themselves into others. Paul described four generations of it in one sentence: *"And the things that thou hast heard of me among many witnesses, the same commit thou to faithful men, who shall be able to teach others also"* **(2 Timothy 2:2)**: Paul, Timothy, faithful men, and others also.

The practical work is to take what has worked and make it clearer, more concentrated, and quicker to grasp: a book, a school, a fellowship, videos, small groups. Try something; if it does not work, try something else. Learn which approaches reach which kinds of people. Teach others to teach. The circle spreads.

It begins with one. In this book's case, it began with a young man who said "I think I got it," and wanted other people to have what he had found. The next chapter asks what happens when that change moves outward, from one man to his household, his neighborhood, and his nation.

## Chapter 21: Concentric Circles of Order

*From a conversation between Thomas Lee Abshier, ND, and Isak, 30 October 2026. Edited for clarity by Claude.*

**THOMAS:** I'm running for president, whether it's a pipe dream or not, and the question is how to win. Winning is the nation saying, "Wow, that is the way to live life." That's what I did in counseling for forty years: telling people how to live. Just live this way, and life will work well if you do it. I've been thinking about how to do the same thing for America for about as long. I'm more certain how to make life work for an individual than how to make it work for a group.

The foundation of the whole thing is the individual's life working. If a person's life is working, he will naturally try to make the group's life work the way his does. So you have to treat the individual, the couple, and the family first, and have that unit work. When it works, the whole will work, because the whole is built on what works for the individual, the couple, and the family.

That's why you have to have health. If you don't have health, you don't have anything. You have to live right and have a healthy body. Then you have to relate to yourself well: think well, know what's right. Then you have to be able to relate to another person who has his own point of view, and make that work. You need a family that teaches the wisdom of the old to the young, through the generations. Then you take it out to the next step: the group, the clan, the tribe, the hundred and fifty people who make up a clan. That group needs a government that works at its level, dealing with property and trade and work and serving each other. Groups of clans make a community, then a state, then a nation. They're different layers, and at each layer different issues come up as the most prominent ones to handle.


---


### From the inside out

Picture a stone dropped in still water. The rings spread outward from the point where it fell: first a small ring, then a larger one, then larger still, each one carrying the motion of the one inside it. Order in human life spreads the same way. It starts at the center, in a single person, and moves outward through the household to the neighborhood and the nation.

Most political programs work from the outside in. They try to fix the nation by passing laws, hoping the families and individuals inside it will be reformed by the pressure. That rarely works, because a law cannot make a man honest or a marriage faithful. The biblical order works from the inside out. Paul's test for a church leader was his home: *"For if a man know not how to rule his own house, how shall he take care of the church of God?"* **(1 Timothy 3:5)**. And Jesus stated the principle in general: *"He that is faithful in that which is least is faithful also in much"* **(Luke 16:10)**.

Here are the circles, from the center out.

### The body

The first circle is the body. A man who is sick, exhausted, or in pain has little to give anyone else. Health is not everything, but without it nearly everything else is harder. *"What? know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost which is in you, which ye have of God, and ye are not your own?"* **(1 Corinthians 6:19)**. Caring for the body is stewardship of something God owns. This is the work I have done as a physician for most of my life.

### The mind

The second circle is the mind: how a man relates to himself. Does he think clearly? Does he know what is right? Does he have the habits of thought that Part I described, or is he ruled by his moods and his screen? *"For as he thinketh in his heart, so is he"* **(Proverbs 23:7)**. Most of my forty years of counseling have been spent here, helping people see their own patterns and practice better ones.

### The couple

The third circle is the couple: two people, each with his or her own point of view, learning to make one life together. It is the first place a man must learn that his perspective is not the only one, and that love requires him to enter another's. *"Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife: and they shall be one flesh"* **(Genesis 2:24)**. A nation of broken marriages cannot be a stable nation, whatever its laws say.

### The family

The fourth circle is the family across generations, where the wisdom of the old is handed to the young. This is where precept, the cheap school of Chapter 5, does most of its work. *"And these words, which I command thee this day, shall be in thine heart: And thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thine house, and when thou walkest by the way, and when thou liest down, and when thou risest up"* **(Deuteronomy 6:6–7)**.

### The clan

The fifth circle is the clan: roughly a hundred and fifty people. The anthropologist Robin Dunbar found that number again and again, in villages, military companies, and congregations. It is about the number of people a man can actually know: their names, faces, families, and how they live. Below that size, a community can largely govern itself through relationship and reputation. Property disputes, trade, work, and care for the needy can be handled face to face, by people who know one another.

### Community, state, and nation

Above the clan, a man can no longer know everyone, and he needs representation: someone he does know, whose character he has seen, to stand for him among people he does not know. That is the structure Moses was given when the work of judging the nation was crushing him. His father-in-law Jethro told him to choose *"able men, such as fear God, men of truth, hating covetousness; and place such over them, to be rulers of thousands, and rulers of hundreds, rulers of fifties, and rulers of tens"* **(Exodus 18:21)**. Nested levels, each handling what arises at its own level, each led by men known for their character.

The principle is that each circle should handle what belongs to it, and the larger circle should step in only when a smaller one cannot. The family should not hand its children's moral education to the state. The town should not hand its roads to Washington. The ERA framework puts accountability in the same order: personal conscience first, then family, church, community, state, and, as a last resort, the federal government. The closer accountability stays to the individual, the healthier the society.

### Why the line has failed before

This book has relied on a distinction between two boards: society, where persuasion works, and the state, which carries the sword only against violence and fraud. Isak asked a hard question about it: why has that line never held? Constantine made Christianity the religion of the empire. Calvin's Geneva punished heresy. The Puritans of Massachusetts banished dissenters. Christians have drawn this line on paper many times and then crossed it when they gained power.

The question deserves a straight answer, and I do not think there is a structure that guarantees the line will hold. James Madison saw why: *If men were angels, no government would be necessary.* Men are not angels, and Christians in power are still men. But I think the circles explain much of the failure, and point toward what helps.

The line has broken when power gathered in the outer circles and reached into the inner ones: when an empire or a state church took over the work of conscience, family, and congregation. It has held better where power stayed close to the people. The American founding was the most serious attempt in history to keep it there. It forbade a national church and any religious test for federal office, and over the following decades the states disestablished their own churches, Massachusetts last of all in 1833. That experiment has held the line, imperfectly, for more than two centuries.

Several things help it hold. The line must be written into law, as the First Amendment writes it. Power must stay as local as possible, so that no one center can command every conscience. Leaders at each level must be men of the character Jethro described. Ordinary citizens must keep the power to refuse unjust laws; that is one reason I regard the jury's right to judge the law as well as the facts as a cornerstone of a free people. And the faith itself must be kept honest about its founder. A Christian who reaches for the sword against a man's belief is not applying Christ's teaching but violating it **(Luke 9:55–56)**. No safeguard is perfect. But a people formed from the inside out, in their bodies, minds, marriages, families, and clans, is the hardest people for any tyrant to rule, including a Christian one.

### A map of the work

The circles also explain how my own work is organized, and they offer the reader a map of it. Each of my websites addresses one or more circles. *naturedox.com* is about the body: health, physiology, and the practice of medicine. *MyCounselor* is about the mind, the couple, and the family. *Renaissance Ministries* is about the soul and its relationship with God, the center of every circle. *Dr. Thomas for President* is about the outer circles: the clan, the community, the state, and the nation. And the *Hyperphysics* site is about the nature of reality itself, the ground all the circles stand on.

Isak is building a map that ties all of these together, with each site as its own circle and a center marked *All*, where every article is gathered around the topics they share. That map is meant to do for the work what this book is trying to do for a life: show where each piece sits in relation to the whole.

The circles move outward, but they all begin with one person. The last chapter asks what that person is called to be.

## Chapter 22: Every Man a Philosopher King

*From "The Mapmaker and the Wanderer."*

The next morning, Eli came in early. "I had an idea," he said. "What if we're thinking about this wrong? You keep saying you want to fix the town. What if the town doesn't need to be fixed? What if it needs philosopher kings?"

"Plato's idea."

"Not one philosopher king. All of them. Every person in this town: every carpenter, every teacher, every mother, every drunk at the tavern. What if the map isn't for you to hold? What if it's for everyone to carry? What if every person is the philosopher king of his own life, his own household, his own street?"

Ezra set down his pen and stared at Eli. Then he laughed, a real laugh, not tired and not ironic, something old and young at the same time.

"That's it," he said. "That's the whole campaign. I'm not trying to be king. I'm trying to enroll everyone in being his own king, under the one King who actually earned the title."

"No king but King Jesus," said Eli.

### Plato's idea

About twenty-four centuries ago, Plato wrote in the *Republic* that cities would have no rest from their troubles until philosophers became kings, or kings became philosophers. He meant that the people who govern ought to be the people who love wisdom and have trained themselves to see what is true and good. It is a fine idea, and Plato meant it for very few. His philosopher kings were a small class of guardians, selected and trained from childhood, who would rule everyone else.

At the end of my conversation with Isak, I realized that what I have been doing for forty years, in counseling, in writing, and now in running for president, is a version of Plato's idea. And Isak, joking, gave the twist that makes it biblical: "I'm also president." Not one philosopher king. All of them.

### Opened to everyone

Every person already governs something. He governs his body: what he eats, whether he rests, what he puts into it. He governs his mind: what he attends to, what he believes, how he answers his moods. Most people govern a household, a marriage, and children. Many govern a crew, a business, a classroom, or a congregation. Every adult citizen of a republic shares in governing a town, a state, and a nation. Chapter 21 traced the circles. Each of them has a throne, and someone is sitting on it, ruling well or badly.

Scripture puts the first throne above all the others: *"He that is slow to anger is better than the mighty; and he that ruleth his spirit than he that taketh a city"* **(Proverbs 16:32)**. The hardest kingdom to rule is a man's own spirit. A man who can rule it is greater than a conqueror.

That is why this book has been about the individual before it was about the nation. A nation of men who cannot govern themselves will be governed by someone else, usually badly. A nation of men who can govern themselves needs very little government, and can choose its leaders well.

### Under the one King

There is a danger in calling every man a king. It sounds like the road without a center from Chapter 16: every man sovereign over himself, answerable to no one. That is not what I mean.

When Israel demanded a king like the nations around it, God told Samuel, *"they have not rejected thee, but they have rejected me, that I should not reign over them"* **(1 Samuel 8:7)**. The problem was not kingship. It was replacing God as King. And at the trial of Jesus, the chief priests made the same choice in its starkest form: *"We have no king but Caesar"* **(John 19:15)**. The campaign's motto is the reverse of their answer: *No King but King Jesus.*

By kingship, I mean a kingship under a King. Scripture says Christ *"hath made us kings and priests unto God and his Father"* **(Revelation 1:6)**. We reign, but as stewards, in His name and by His law. And His idea of ruling is not ours. *"Whosoever will be great among you, shall be your minister: And whosoever of you will be the chiefest, shall be servant of all. For even the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many"* **(Mark 10:43–45)**. A philosopher king in Christ's kingdom rules by serving and leading by example.

### The love of wisdom

*Philosopher* means lover of wisdom. Plato's philosopher loved an abstract form of the Good. The Christian philosopher loves a Person: Christ, whom Paul calls *"the power of God, and the wisdom of God"* **(1 Corinthians 1:24)**.

This kind of philosopher does not need a university. He needs the habits this book has described: to see the whole moment, to take off the hat, to practice what is right until it becomes his own, to learn by precept rather than ruin, to choose well at the right scale, to judge by God's standard, to test every voice, and to apply every test to himself first. Chapter 19 quoted Solomon: *"It is the glory of God to conceal a thing: but the honour of kings is to search out a matter"* **(Proverbs 25:2)**. The search for wisdom is itself the king's work.

And he needs humility. I told Isak I was a legend in my own mind, and he said, "Me too." We laughed, because it is true of everyone. A philosopher king who forgets that he is a small king under a great one becomes a tyrant, in his house or in his nation. A philosopher king who remembers it asks for wisdom every day, *"of God, that giveth to all men liberally, and upbraideth not"* **(James 1:5)**.

### A campaign as revival

This is what my presidential campaign is about. A political campaign says, "Vote for me, and I will fix it." I am not saying that. No president can fix a nation whose people cannot govern themselves, and no law can give a man a new heart.

What I am doing is closer to a revival. A revival says, "Here is the truth. If you live it, your life will work. If enough of us live it, the nation will work." The foundation is not policy. It is the individual's life working: a healthy body, a sound mind, a faithful marriage, children raised in the ways of the Lord, honest work, voluntary generosity, and a clan of neighbors who know and serve one another. From that foundation, the larger circles take care of themselves far better than any program could manage.

I am not running to become king. I am running to enroll as many people as I can in being philosopher kings themselves, under the one King who earned the title. Isaiah saw where that kingdom ends: *"For unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given: and the government shall be upon his shoulder ... Of the increase of his government and peace there shall be no end"* **(Isaiah 9:6–7)**.

### The invitation

So this book ends where it has been heading all along: with you.

You are standing at the dot that says *You are here*. You have a body to steward, a mind to govern, perhaps a marriage to keep and children to raise, neighbors to serve, and a share in the government of a nation. You have a palette of colors no one else has. You have a map, imperfect, to check against the ground. And you have an invitation from the King, to take up the throne He has given you, rule it by serving, and teach someone else to do the same.

That is what it means to be a philosopher king. It is not a title. It is a way of walking. And the walk begins with the next step.

## Epilogue: The Map Is a Conversation

*From "The Mapmaker and the Wanderer."*

That night, Ezra stayed up late. He had been treating his map like a project, a thing to finish and display. Now he saw it differently. The map was not a product. It was a conversation. Every person who walked in and asked a question added a line to it. Every doubt, every objection, every quiet confession of faith: all of it was cartography.

He wrote a title across the top, in letters large enough to read from the door:

**THE MAP OF MORALITY AND MEANING** *You are here. This is where you could be. Choose your road.*

They went back to the map, he and Eli. They still had a great deal of work to do. The roads still diverged. The rivers still ran in opposite directions. The town was still turbulent, still confused, still full of people wearing hats with little screens that showed them only what they already believed.

But the map was getting clearer. And now there were two people drawing it. Then there would be twelve, and then their twelve, and then, maybe, a river flowing all in one direction: smooth enough to carry everyone, rough enough in places to build character, and aimed, finally, at the sea.


---


This book began as a conversation, and it is still one. Every chapter grew out of a morning when a young man brought me his questions, and I tried to answer them, and out of the moments when his questions were better than my answers. Isak never let me rest on a sentence that sounded right but was not. Where this book is honest about the church's failures, about the limits of my sources, and about what I do not know, much of the credit is his.

So I do not offer it as finished. A map is never finished. Surveyors go back to the ground, find that a river has moved or a road has washed out, and redraw the line. If you find a place where this map does not match the ground, I want to hear about it. Tell me where it is wrong. That is how maps get better, and how the people who carry them do too.

The Prologue told of two visions: a flower that faded the harder I held it, and a rainbow whose center I could never reach. I understand them better now than I did then. The flower taught me that no moment can be kept. The rainbow taught me that the center is not a place I arrive at by chasing it. But I have learned one more thing since, which those visions could not teach me. The center I could not reach came to me. *"For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life"* **(John 3:16)**. The center of the map is a Person, and He walked out to the edge of it to find us.

You are here. He is here too. Choose your road.
