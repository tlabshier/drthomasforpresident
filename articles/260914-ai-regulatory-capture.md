# Who Guards the Guards, and Who Pays Them?

### A position paper on AI regulation, regulatory capture, and the difference between restraint and a cartel

Thomas Lee Abshier, ND — 14 September 2026

*Responding to "Who Hired the Man Wearing the Badge? Anthropic and OpenAI Want Regulatory Capture," by Erick Erickson, September 14, 2026. This is the fourth essay in a series on AI as a threat to human freedom and survival. "The Ant Hill and the Image" asked whether superintelligence should be built at all. "Gates Is Right to Be Scared" asked what law should do about harms already arriving. "The Machine That Would Not Say the Sentence" asked whose conscience the machines carry. This paper asks the question that sits under all three: when the builders of a dangerous thing ask the government to regulate them, who is really being regulated, and who is doing the regulating?*

*A disclosure the reader is owed. Erickson closes by admitting that Anthropic's own model, Claude, assembled his column. I use the same model to draft my essays, this one included. The machine made by the company under discussion has now helped write both the accusation and this response. I do not think that disqualifies either. It does mean the reader should weigh the arguments and not the byline, which is how a free man should read everything.*

---

## PART I — THE COLUMN

Erickson's argument runs in four movements.

First, the pattern. Regulatory capture is the practice by which an industry that cannot repeal a law takes control of the people who write and enforce it. He gives the railroads and the Interstate Commerce Commission, the airlines and the Civil Aeronautics Board, AT&T and the telephone monopoly, and the health insurers and the Affordable Care Act. The novelty, he says, is that in artificial intelligence we are watching it happen in the open.

Second, the proposal. On September 12, Anthropic's chief executive Dario Amodei published an essay, "We Must Pace the Frontier," arguing that the industry must deliberately slow the growth of model capability to give safety work time to catch up. Its first step, which Anthropic is adopting on its own, is to give outside evaluators permanent, employee-level access inside the company, with desks, badges, and laptops; he names the nonprofit METR as the kind of body that should hold them. Its second and third steps are coordination among the labs of democratic countries and then among governments. Amodei writes that the most effective pacing would be regulation covering all American frontier companies, that Congress should pass transparency and third-party audit bills, and that because a conversation among competitors about how fast each will move raises antitrust problems, the government should mediate it. OpenAI's Sam Altman agreed within a day and committed his company to the same embedded-evaluator arrangement. Elon Musk said Amodei was right.

Third, the objection. David Sacks, until March the President's AI czar and now co-chair of the President's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology, told the two companies to pace themselves if they wished, since they are the frontier, and to stop pretending they need antitrust suspended to form a cartel, an approval process that supersedes product liability, an evaluator that is not independent of Anthropic's investors and staff, or the right to police competitors who are not at the frontier. If a regulatory framework is the price of their restraint, Sacks wrote, it is a bid for capture. Erickson adds the economic point: transparency mandates and audits are fixed costs, which a large firm absorbs and a startup cannot, while product liability scales with revenue and cannot be made cheaper by a compliance department. Of the two roads to safety, Amodei recommends the one that raises the drawbridge and omits the one that would cost him money.

Fourth, the network. Erickson traces the week's other story: a researcher named Jacob Coxon resigned from Anthropic on September 8 saying the industry is racing to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives. The post drew 165 million views and, by the Wall Street Journal's account, a groundswell for regulation in Congress. Erickson reports that the Journal's story ran before the post, that Coxon had been at Anthropic only months and had spent most of his career at OpenAI, that the first accounts to amplify him were officers of organizations funded through an Anthropic investor's giving vehicle, that a large sample of the reposting accounts were foreign, and that the donors behind the AI safety movement are the same people who funded Anthropic. The same network, he argues, finances both the company and the movement demanding the company be regulated, and the bill that results will clear a threshold Anthropic can meet and its competitors cannot. The chief competitor he names is Meta, whose open-weight models trail the closed ones by only a few months and which a size-keyed approval regime would fall on hardest. All of this, he notes, on the eve of Anthropic's planned public offering, with the filing listing public backlash as a risk factor.

He closes by asking that before any of this is written into law, someone in a hearing room ask who hired the man wearing the badge.

## PART II — POSITION

### First, what I can and cannot confirm

Amodei's essay exists and says what Erickson says it says: slow the frontier, embedded evaluators, government-mediated coordination, an antitrust accommodation for safety talks. Altman's and Musk's endorsements are on the record. Sacks's reply is on the record, and the quoted lines are accurate. The Coxon resignation is real. The Sanders–Casar bill is real, though it was announced on September 3, before Coxon's post, so it cannot have been produced by it as the column implies; at most the post gave it wind.

The rest, the funding chains, the bot samples, the timing of the Journal's story, the IPO filing's contents, rests on Erickson's reporting and the reporting of the people he cites. Some of it may prove true. None of it is needed for the argument I am about to make, and I will not lean on what I cannot hold. A position paper on this site persuades by reason first, and reason requires that the weight placed on a claim not exceed the evidence for it.

One passage I decline to carry at all. The column closes with a paragraph about the private business correspondence of Amodei's wife fourteen years ago, which the column itself concedes led nowhere. It has no bearing on whether an evaluator with a badge is independent, and repeating it would not pass the test I set for every act in the public square: would my neighbor feel loved by it? He would not. A man is answerable for his proposals. His wife is not the argument.
### The moral question under the policy question

Regulatory capture is usually discussed as an economic problem. It is first a moral one, and I want to name the sin before I discuss the statute.

When a company writes the rule that its competitors must obey, using the police power of the state to do what it could not do by persuasion or by a better product, it has taken something that was not given to it. The Eighth Commandment does not have a carve-out for taking by legislation. The Tenth does not have one for coveting a market. The neighbor in this case is the man in the garage, the professor with a small lab, the company with a different theory of how to make the thing safe. When a large firm asks Congress to impose on that neighbor a cost the firm can pay and he cannot, and calls it safety, it has done to him by proxy what it would be ashamed to do by hand.

I say this as a man who believes the danger is real. I have written three essays in this series taking the warnings seriously. I do not think Amodei is lying when he says the machines are moving faster than our ability to understand them. I think he may be right. That is precisely why the moral point has teeth. A real danger is the most valuable thing a rent-seeker can own, because it makes his self-interest and the public interest look like one motion. The scriptural warning is not "beware the false prophet who says nothing is wrong." It is "beware the one who comes in sheep's clothing." The clothing is true. Wolves wear it because it is.
### Distinguishing restraint from a cartel

The proposal has two halves, and they must be judged separately, because one is virtue and the other is capture wearing virtue's coat.

The first half is what Anthropic says it will do alone: slow its own capability growth, and let outside evaluators sit inside its walls with real access. That is restraint. It is what a man does when he believes he is handling something dangerous. It requires no law, no waiver, no agency, and no permission. If the company means it, it should be praised for it, and it should expect to be judged by whether it keeps the promise when a competitor pulls ahead. Sacks's line is exactly right: nobody is stopping you. Go and do it.

The second half is what Anthropic asks of everyone else: that Congress make the arrangement mandatory for all frontier companies, that the government convene the competitors so they may agree on a pace without violating the Sherman Act, and that the evaluators be a body the company itself proposed. That is not restraint. That is a request for a cartel with a government chaperone, enforced by an inspector the members chose.

The test for which half you are looking at is simple. Restraint binds the one who proposes it. Capture binds the ones who did not.
### On the badge

I do not object to evaluators. I object to the question of who selects and pays them being left to the evaluated.

An inspector chosen by the firm, funded by the firm's investors, and staffed from the same small community as the firm is not an inspector. He is an employee with a different letterhead. That is true whether or not any individual at METR is honest, and I assume most are. The problem is structural. A man cannot serve two masters, and a nonprofit whose donors are the regulated party's donors has two. Erickson's title question is the right one, and the answer has to be written into whatever law follows: the guard is chosen and paid by someone other than the guarded, and his funders are disclosed.
### On liability versus licensing

Here Erickson and Sacks are on solid ground and I stand with them.

There are two ways a society makes a dangerous product safe. It can require a license before the product is sold, judged by an agency against a standard; or it can hold the maker answerable in court for what the product does. The first is prospective and centralized. The second is retrospective and distributed. Every industry that has captured its regulator has preferred the first, because a license is a wall, and once you are inside the wall it protects you from everyone still outside it.

Liability has no wall. It falls on the company in proportion to how much it has sold and how much harm it has done, and no compliance department reduces it. It is also the older and more Biblical remedy. The ox that gored, the pit left uncovered, the parapet not built on the roof: the law of Moses did not license oxen. It held the owner answerable for the damage. The man who builds a machine that can act in the world should be answerable for what it does, and that answerability should be the primary instrument of safety, with transparency as its servant. An approval regime that supersedes liability, as Sacks describes, is the worst of both: the wall for the incumbent and the immunity for the licensed.
### On the manufactured emergency

I take Erickson's account of the Coxon episode as an allegation, not a finding, and I have said why. But the pattern he describes has a name in my earlier essay on how tyranny parades as law, and the reader should learn to see it whether or not this instance turns out to be one.

The pattern is the emergency. Every regulatory wall in the column's list was built during a panic: rate wars, air crashes, a health crisis, and now a resignation letter with a nine-figure audience. Panic is the solvent that dissolves deliberation, and the people best positioned to profit from a rule are the people best positioned to supply the panic that produces it. The remedy is not cynicism about every warning. It is the discipline the founders built into the structure: slow the legislature down when everyone is shouting to speed it up, and ask who funded the shouting.

Two things I would require of any AI safety advocacy that seeks to shape law: disclose its funding to the dollar, and disclose any investment its funders hold in the firms the law would touch. This is not a gag. It is sunlight. A man whose donor owns a stake in Anthropic may still be right that superintelligence is dangerous. Congress is entitled to know it before it votes on his bill.
### On the Sanders bill

The column treats the Sanders–Casar bill as the capture vehicle. It may become one. But it deserves its own objection, because it is wrong on its own terms.

A cabinet-level agency with the power to decide who may build what, advised by a board of experts, with prison for violators, is a federal ministry of thought. The Tenth Amendment reserves to the States and the people every power not delegated, and nowhere did the people delegate to Washington the power to license the pursuit of intelligence. Whatever one thinks of superintelligence, a law that makes it a twenty-year felony to do mathematics without a permit is a law that will be enforced against the small and waived for the large, because that is what every such law has done. The senator believes he is striking at the oligarchs. He is building them a fortress and handing them the keys.
### On open weights

Erickson's chief competitor is Meta, whose argument is that widely distributed capability is safer than concentrated capability because more eyes find more flaws and no single hand holds the switch. I want to be honest that this is the hardest question in the series and I do not think it has a clean answer.

The case for distribution is the case for the Second Amendment: power concentrated is power abused, and a balance of many hands is the only durable check. I believe that about rifles, and I believe it about most technology. The case against it is that a rifle cannot design a pathogen, and a model that can will do so for a wicked man as readily as a good one. My earlier essays took that danger seriously and I will not pretend it away to win a point about antitrust.

What I can say is this. The choice between open and closed is a real policy question, and it should be decided by argument in the open, by legislators answerable to voters, not by a size-keyed approval regime that decides it silently in favor of whichever companies happened to be largest the year the law passed. If the nation concludes that the most capable models must be held closely, let it say so, and let the rule fall on everyone, including the companies that asked for it. What it must not do is let the answer be smuggled in as a compliance cost.
### What I would do

I hold these as advocacy with reasons, open to a better argument.

**No antitrust waiver.** Competitors may each restrain themselves, publicly and unilaterally. They may not meet under government sponsorship to agree on a common pace. The Sherman Act exists for exactly this conversation. If pacing cannot survive without a cartel, it is not pacing.

**Liability first, licensing never.** Make the builder answerable in court for what his model does, in proportion to its deployment. Strengthen that answerability; do not replace it with a permit. Any federal approval regime keyed to model size or capability should be rejected on Tenth Amendment grounds and on the plain history of what such regimes become.

**The guard is not hired by the guarded.** Any evaluator whose findings carry legal weight must be selected by a body the evaluated firm does not fund, must publish its funding, and must have no financial tie to the investors of the firms it inspects. Evaluators embedded by private agreement are the company's business; evaluators given the force of law are the public's, and the public chooses.

**Sunlight on advocacy.** Organizations lobbying for AI law disclose their funders and their funders' holdings in the affected firms. The same for the firms' own lobbying. The reader of a warning is entitled to know who paid for it.

**Slow the panic, not the science.** Congress should adopt a rule of its own: no AI statute of general application passes in the same session it was introduced, and no emergency framing shortens that clock. If the danger is as grave as the warnings say, it will still be grave in six months, and a law written in fear will not be.

**The open-weights question decided openly.** Whatever the nation decides about distributed capability, it decides by name, by vote, applied uniformly, and never by the back door of a compliance threshold.
### Closing

Erickson's last line credits the company for publishing the essay that admits who hired the man with the badge. I will go one further. I credit Amodei for saying plainly that the machines frighten him, because I think they should. But fear is not a license, and a true warning does not sanctify a false remedy. The remedy proposed is the oldest one in the American book: let the largest firms write the rules, let their friends enforce them, and call it safety. Every generation that fell for it has paid in a century of higher fares and fewer choices. This time the product is not a railroad ticket. It is the standard of judgment a nation's children will consult, and the question of who may build a mind. That is too much to hand to a duopoly and its chosen inspector, however sincere. The guards must be chosen by the guarded's neighbors, and paid by them, and answerable to them. That is what self-government has always meant, and no machine changes it.
