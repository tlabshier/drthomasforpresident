# 261105 - Texas Turning Blue

# When You Can’t Breathe, You Turn Blue:


# A Classmate’s Verdict on Texas


*Dr. Thomas — The New ERA Vision — November 2026*


A classmate of mine from Industrial High School, Class of 1969, still lives in Victoria, Texas. Two days after the election, she wrote a short post that says more about Tuesday’s results than most of the commentary I have read. Her main points:

>
The election wasn’t about blue or red, Democrat or Republican. We have been in a cultural war for quite some time. This Texas election shows the full decline of our culture and the great loss of traditional Christian values. Many are blaming Californians. We need to put the blame on Texans.


She goes on to name the churches she holds responsible: the Presbyterian Church (USA), the United Church of Christ, the Episcopal Church, the Evangelical Lutheran Church in America, and, from her own experience as a member, the United Methodist Church. She adds the Roman Catholic Church as well. She says these churches have taught people to put Scripture and two thousand years of moral standards aside, and that “this is how you end up with people worshiping men like Talarico.” She notes that the Democratic Party has made the right to end unborn lives central to its platform. Then she gives the line that stays with me: *When you can’t breathe, you turn blue.* She ends by asking us to pray for the governor-elect, Gina Hinojosa, “and so will we.”


I stand with her. The election in Texas was a moral event before it was a partisan one. And the people most responsible are not newcomers from California. They are the Texans, and above all the Texas churches, who stopped teaching that God’s law is God’s and not ours to revise. Below I set out why I think she is right, where the record needs care, and what follows for those of us who share her grief.


## The Question Under the Question


Her post is not really about one Senate seat or one governor’s mansion. Under it lies a harder question: *How did a state where most adults still call themselves Christian elect leaders who openly reject what Christians have always believed about life in the womb and about who God is?*


That is the question I want to answer. Answering it means looking first at the pulpit and only then at the ballot box.


## What Happened on Tuesday


The facts first. On November 3, state Representative James Talarico defeated Attorney General Ken Paxton for the U.S. Senate, and state Representative Gina Hinojosa defeated Governor Greg Abbott, who was seeking a fourth term. In the unofficial returns, Talarico won about 55 percent to Paxton’s 43, and Hinojosa about 53 percent to Abbott’s 46. Abbott conceded on election night, and so did Paxton. Hinojosa will be the first Democratic governor of Texas since Ann Richards left office in 1995. Talarico will be the first Democrat Texas has sent to the Senate since Lloyd Bentsen. Democrats also won both chambers of Congress nationally.


These were not narrow margins. Texas did not drift. It turned.


## Blame Texans, Not Californians


My classmate is right to turn away from the comfortable story that Californians did this. The data does not support it. Researchers who have studied migration into Texas have found that Californians who move here lean Republican: in one analysis, 44 percent of them were Republicans and 29 percent were Democrats. Many came precisely because they wanted to leave California’s politics behind.


So the newcomers are not the explanation. The explanation is closer to home, in the churches Texans attend and the teaching they receive there. Scripture puts the order plainly: *“For the time is come that judgment must begin at the house of God”* **(1 Peter 4:17)**. Blaming an outsider is easier than examining ourselves, but our Lord left us no such option: *“And why beholdest thou the mote that is in thy brother’s eye, but considerest not the beam that is in thine own eye?”* **(Matthew 7:3)**.


## The Churches That Taught Texans to Let Go


My classmate was a United Methodist, and so was I. She says the UMC split was never only about sexuality, and that it began with bishops who denied the Resurrection, who addressed God as Mother, and who put man’s reasoning above Scripture. Her account fits what I saw, and part of it can be checked against the record.


In 2002 Bishop C. Joseph Sprague of Chicago, a sitting United Methodist bishop, publicly rejected the virgin birth and the bodily resurrection of Jesus, both in a speech and in his book *Affirmations of a Dissenter*. Twenty-eight clergy and laypeople filed a formal complaint charging him with spreading doctrines contrary to the church’s own Articles of Religion. The complaint was dismissed. The denomination never disciplined him. That is primary evidence, in a bishop’s own words and the church’s own process, for the very thing she describes: a church that would no longer defend its own creed against its own bishops.


When the break finally came, it was large. Between 2019 and the end of 2023, about 7,650 congregations left the United Methodist Church, roughly a quarter of its American churches. Texas was hit harder than most of the country. The Texas Annual Conference, based in Houston, lost nearly half of its churches, and the Northwest Texas Conference lost 81 percent. The reason given officially was the dispute over sexuality, but a church does not lose a quarter of its congregations over one issue. People leave when they conclude the institution no longer believes what they believe.


Her other two charges, that God was being called Mother and that reason was placed above Scripture, rest here on her firsthand testimony as a member. I take that testimony seriously, because she was in the pews and the Sprague case shows the drift was real at the very top.


The other churches she names come from an article she read that ranks the most liberal denominations in America. I will not try to grade every one of them in this essay. One, however, matters directly to Tuesday’s result, because it formed the man Texas just sent to the Senate. James Talarico is a Presbyterian (PCUSA) seminarian. His home congregation has been reported to recommend non-gendered language in place of “Father” for God and to describe itself as a “reproductive freedom congregation.” That report comes from his political opponents’ side, but it is consistent with what he has said himself.


## The Catholic Question


Her charge against the Roman Catholic Church needs more care, and I want to be fair to her and to the Church.


Part of it is plainly correct. Pope Leo XIV, the first American pope, has repeatedly and pointedly criticized the administration’s immigration enforcement. He has called the treatment of migrants “inhuman” and “extremely disrespectful,” and he publicly backed the U.S. bishops’ statement opposing “indiscriminate mass deportations.”


The second part, that Catholic social services enabled millions to come illegally, is where proportion matters. The record shows that Catholic Charities sheltered and fed migrants whom U.S. Customs and Border Protection had already processed and released into the country. Those migrants had been let in by the federal government; the charities did not bring them across. An independent audit of the Rio Grande Valley agency found it complied with its federal grants, though a later federal review found it had served hundreds of people past their period of eligibility, and its funding was suspended pending investigation. Both Catholic Charities’ denials and the congressional accusations against it come from interested parties, and neither should be taken at face value.


My own judgment is this. The charities did not open the border. But they gave the policy of mass release a humane face and a working logistics system, and the Church’s leadership now spends its moral authority opposing the enforcement of law. To that extent her concern holds. Catholic teaching itself affirms a nation’s right to regulate its borders alongside the duty to treat migrants with dignity. A Church that preaches only the second half of that teaching has stopped teaching the whole of it.


There is a sharper fact that supports her deeper point. In the Texas Senate race, exit polls found that Talarico won 58 percent of Catholic voters. The Catholic Church teaches that abortion is a grave sin, and its last pope compared it to hiring a hitman. Yet a clear majority of Catholic voters chose a candidate who campaigned on restoring *Roe v. Wade* in federal law. Whatever the bishops intended, the teaching did not take hold in those voters.


## The Candidate the Churches Prepared


My classmate writes that people are “worshiping men like Talarico.” I would put it a little differently, but she is pointing at something real. Talarico’s appeal was explicitly religious. He campaigned as a man of faith, and he preached a gospel reshaped to fit the culture.


Here is what he has said, in his own words. He said, “God is nonbinary.” On a national podcast he told millions of listeners that “the idea that there is a set Christian orthodoxy on the issue of abortion is just not rooted in Scripture,” and he argued that because the angel’s announcement came before Mary gave her assent, the Incarnation affirms a right to choose abortion. When a voter challenged him at a CNN town hall, he called those remarks “provocative.” He did not retract them.


Scripture is not silent on the child in the womb: *“For thou hast possessed my reins: thou hast covered me in my mother’s womb”* **(Psalm 139:13)**. And Mary’s answer to the angel was submission, not choice: *“Behold the handmaid of the Lord; be it unto me according to thy word”* **(Luke 1:38)**. Taking the moment when a young woman bowed to God’s will and turning it into an argument for ending a child’s life reverses the meaning of the story.


Paul described this kind of teaching and the kind of hearer it attracts: *“For the time will come when they will not endure sound doctrine; but after their own lusts shall they heap to themselves teachers, having itching ears; And they shall turn away their ears from the truth, and shall be turned unto fables”* **(2 Timothy 4:3–4)**. A congregation trained for decades to treat Scripture as negotiable will hear such a teacher as a prophet. That is what my classmate means, and I think she is right.


## The Party and the Plank


She says the Democratic Party abandoned God and traditional moral standards years ago, and she gives the clearest example: the right to end unborn lives has become a central plank of its platform. On that point the record speaks for itself, and Tuesday’s winner ran on it.


She also calls the party socialist. I will not use that word for the whole party. Many Democrats hold no such views, and in my own writing I have said that overstatement is lying by proportion. But the word is not needed to make her point. A party that treats the killing of the unborn as a right to be defended, and not as a wrong to be ended, has already left the moral ground on which this nation was built. *“Righteousness exalteth a nation: but sin is a reproach to any people”* **(Proverbs 14:34)**.


I apply the same test to every policy I propose. First, is it righteous? Does God’s law permit it? Second, if so, is it loving? Third, would I want it done to me? Abortion fails at the first question, so the second and third never come into play. No child in the womb, if he could speak, would ask to be the one whose life is weighed against another’s convenience.


## On Immigration: Where Her Point Needs Care


She attributes part of the result to a “tremendous influx of ‘immigrants’ who want the USA to become like the country they abandoned.” Here I think the evidence points somewhere she may not expect, and I want to be honest about it.


The swing that decided Texas came largely from Hispanic voters, and most of them are citizens, many of them born in Texas. Two years ago, President Trump won about 55 percent of the Hispanic vote in Texas, a Republican record. This year Hispanic voters moved sharply back. About 2.7 million Hispanic Texans voted, up from 2 million in 2022, and in their own accounts their reasons were mainly economic, with anger at aggressive immigration raids close behind. Many of them are religious, socially conservative people who voted Republican in 2024.


So the problem is not mainly newcomers arriving with foreign ideas. The problem is that conservative Christian voters, Hispanic and Anglo alike, no longer treat a candidate’s stance on abortion and on the nature of God as disqualifying. That is the cultural decline she is describing, and it reaches well beyond immigrants. Her deeper concern about assimilation is legitimate. A nation that does not teach its newcomers and its own children what it believes will not keep those beliefs. But the voters who changed Tuesday’s outcome were overwhelmingly Texans already.


## The Other Explanations, and Why They Don’t Dissolve Her Point


Fairness requires naming the explanations most analysts give. National exit polls and reporting point to the economy and cost of living, voter discontent with the administration and the war with Iran, and a nationwide Democratic wave that swept both houses of Congress. In Texas specifically, many voters said they were voting against Ken Paxton, who was impeached by the Texas House in 2023 (and acquitted by the Senate) and who carried long-standing allegations of corruption and infidelity. The President himself blamed Abbott’s loss on Abbott, calling him a “RINO.”


Each of these is real. None of them dissolves her point, and one strengthens it. Texas Republicans nominated a man with Paxton’s record over a long-serving incumbent senator. If the election was not about red or blue, that standard applies to us as well. A party of righteousness that chooses its champion without regard to righteousness has lost the moral standing it needs to win. That, too, is a symptom of the decline she describes.


As for the economy and the war, those are the occasions of a vote, not its foundations. Hard times test what a people believe. Believers secure in God’s law do not trade it for a promise of lower prices. On Tuesday, enough Texans did.


## Why the Concern Remains Plausible


Some will say that one election in a bad year for the President’s party proves nothing about the soul of Texas. I think her concern holds, for reasons that go beyond one election.


**The trend is measurable.** In 2014, 77 percent of Texas adults identified as Christian. By 2023–24 that share had fallen to 67 percent, a ten-point drop in a decade. The United Methodist Church, once a pillar of Texas religious life, has split, and nationally it now accounts for fewer than 3 percent of adults. Elections follow culture, and culture follows the pulpit.


**The pattern is consistent.** Churches that loosen their hold on Scripture lose members, and the people they keep vote with the secular culture on the questions that matter most. A majority of Catholic voters choosing a candidate who wants abortion protected in federal law is not a fluke. It is what happens after decades of teaching that left the conscience unformed.


**The incentives point one way.** A denomination that depends on federal grants for its social services has a financial reason to oppose enforcement of the law. A politician who can cast his party’s platform in the language of faith has a strong electoral reason to do so. A church that wants to keep its younger members has a reason to soften what Scripture says. None of these motives proves bad faith in any one person. Together they push in the same direction.


**And there is the intuitive sense that something is wrong.** When a seminarian on national radio turns the Annunciation into an argument for abortion, ordinary believers sense a deep unnaturalness, the same sense my classmate put into one sentence about not being able to breathe. That sense of wrongness is not proof by itself, but it should not be dismissed.


There is something more, and it lies at the center of everything I teach. God does not watch our lives from a distance. He lives them with us. He feels every choice we make from inside the one making it. That is why His law is not an arbitrary rulebook but the description of what does not wound Him or us. A church that tells its people God’s commands can be revised to suit the age is not only mistaken about a text. It invites its people to hurt the One who lives inside their choices, and to hurt the children whose lives He also lives. When a whole culture loses that understanding, it is like a body losing its breath. She chose exactly the right image.


## What Righteousness Asks Now


She ends with a prayer for Gina Hinojosa, and with a gentle irony: “and so will we.” I join her in both. Scripture commands it without regard to party: *“I exhort therefore, that, first of all, supplications, prayers, intercessions, and giving of thanks, be made for all men; For kings, and for all that are in authority; that we may lead a quiet and peaceable life in all godliness and honesty”* **(1 Timothy 2:1–2)**. We should pray for the new governor’s wisdom, for her protection, and for her heart. We should pray the same for Senator-elect Talarico, that the Scriptures he has studied would correct him.


But prayer for our leaders is not enough. The remedy begins where she says the problem began, in the house of God. *“If my people, which are called by my name, shall humble themselves, and pray, and seek my face, and turn from their wicked ways; then will I hear from heaven, and will forgive their sin, and will heal their land”* **(2 Chronicles 7:14)**. The verse says *my people*. It does not say Californians, immigrants, or Democrats.


Practically, this means three things. Christians should belong only to congregations that teach Scripture as the Word of God, and leave when a church will not discipline a bishop who denies the Resurrection. Pastors should teach their people what Scripture says about life in the womb and about who God is, plainly and often, so that no candidate can quote the Bible against them without being recognized. And voters should apply the righteousness test before they vote, to their own party’s candidates first.


My classmate saw clearly what happened in her state. I hold this position open to a better argument, as I hold every position I take. But I have not yet heard one that answers her.
