# The Republic Was Handed Back

*A Constitution Day essay — September 17, 2026*

Two hundred thirty-nine years ago today, the delegates in Philadelphia signed the Constitution. Everyone remembers what Benjamin Franklin said the next morning when Elizabeth Powel asked him what they had made: "A republic, if you can keep it."

Most people hear that line as a compliment to the document and a warning about politicians. I think it was neither. It was a statement about us, and it deserves to be read the way Franklin meant it.
## What Franklin actually said

On the final day of the convention, Franklin had a speech read aloud by James Wilson. It was not a celebration. He said he agreed to the Constitution "with all its faults, if they are such," and he offered a prediction that few people quote. The new government, he said, "is likely to be well administered for a course of years, and can only end in Despotism, as other forms have done before it, when the people shall become so corrupted as to need despotic Government, being incapable of any other."

Read that carefully. Franklin did not say the Constitution would fail. He said the people would change, and that a changed people would need, and would accept, a master. Five months earlier he had put the same thought more plainly: "Only a virtuous people are capable of freedom. As nations become corrupt and vicious, they have more need of masters."

The republic, in Franklin's mind, was never going to be kept by parchment. It was going to be kept, or lost, in the character of ordinary men.
## Who was supposed to enforce it

There is a question underneath all of this that we rarely ask: when the government exceeds the Constitution, whose job is it to stop it?

The modern answer is that the courts will handle it, or the next election will. The founders did not believe either. James Madison, arguing *for* ratification, conceded that "a mere demarcation on parchment of the constitutional limits of the several departments, is not a sufficient guard against those encroachments which lead to a tyrannical concentration of all the powers of government in the same hands." Thomas Paine went further on elections: "It is not because a part of the government is elective, that makes it less a despotism, if the persons so elected possess afterwards, as a parliament, unlimited powers." Choosing a new man to hold unchecked power does not check the power. It only changes the hands.

John Dickinson, who drafted the Articles of Confederation, asked the question directly. A good constitution, he wrote, "promotes, but not always produces a good administration." So what happens when a bad administration comes? His answer: the matter returns "before the supreme sovereignty of the people." And then, in capital letters, so no one could miss it: "IT IS THEIR DUTY TO WATCH, AND THEIR RIGHT TO TAKE CARE, THAT THE CONSTITUTION BE PRESERVED."

John Jay, the first Chief Justice, explained why this had to be so. The men in government, he wrote, receive the people's business "not for themselves and as their own, but as agents and overseers for the people to whom they are constantly responsible." A principal does not ask his agent's permission to review the agent's conduct. He does not wait for the agent to rule on whether the agent has exceeded his instructions. The authority to judge the servant belongs to the master, and the master was always the people.

This was not a fringe view. James Otis in 1767: "It is our duty in the best of times to watch over our constitution with a jealous eye." Samuel Adams in 1771: the liberties of the country "are worth defending at all hazards: And it is our duty to defend them against all attacks." The word that repeats is *duty*. Not option. Not hobby. Duty.
## How the duty was surrendered

Nobody stole the Constitution. There was no coup. What happened is quieter and, I think, more damning: the people handed the job back.

We let the government teach our children what the Constitution means. We let the government's own courts decide, with finality, whether the government had overstepped. We let the government define the limits of its own power and then congratulated ourselves that we still get to vote for the men who run it. Each of these was a small act of delegation, and each felt reasonable at the time. Together they amount to asking the accused to serve as his own judge, jury, and schoolmaster.

Jefferson saw where it leads. "It is the manners and spirit of the people which preserve a republic in vigor," he wrote. "A degeneracy in these is a canker which soon eats to the heart of its laws and constitution." The rot is not in Washington. Washington is the symptom. The rot is a citizenry that no longer sees itself as responsible for the thing it owns.

Machiavelli, of all people, said the same: "a corrupt people that lives under the government of a prince can never become free, even though the prince and his whole line should be extinguished." You can replace every officeholder in the country and change nothing if the people themselves have stopped watching.
## Why this is a moral question, not merely a legal one

I have said elsewhere that every position on this site must rest on a moral foundation, so let me state it here. The test I use is simple: would a neighbor feel loved by this act in the public square?

Consider what it means to hand the Constitution's enforcement to the government. It means that when the state wrongs my neighbor, I have arranged things so that his only recourse is to petition the state. I have, in effect, told him: *I will not watch on your behalf. I have delegated that.* That is not love of neighbor. It is abdication dressed up as trust.

Now consider the alternative. A people who take Dickinson's duty seriously are a people who watch for one another. When a jury refuses to convict a man under a law that is unjust, that jury is loving its neighbor in the most concrete way available to it, by standing between him and the state. I regard jury nullification as a cornerstone of a free people for exactly this reason: it is the point where the people's sovereignty stops being a phrase and becomes twelve men in a room with the power to say no. The same holds for a county sheriff who declines to enforce an unconstitutional order, a state legislature that refuses to implement a federal program it has no obligation to implement, and a school board that teaches its children the Constitution from the text rather than from the government's summary of it.

None of these acts require permission. All of them require virtue, which is the thing Franklin said we would run short of.
## What a citizen can do

I am not going to pretend that any of this is easy or that it will be finished in an election cycle. But the path forward is not mysterious, and it does not begin in Washington.

First, learn the document. Read it, and read what the men who wrote it said about it. Do not accept a summary from the institution the document was written to restrain.

Second, teach it. If you have children, this is the first duty. If you do not, there is a neighbor's child, a church, a fellowship group. The state will not do this for you, and it should not be trusted to.

Third, exercise the offices you already hold. You are a juror. You are a voter for the sheriff, the school board, the state legislature. These are the places where the people's oversight was designed to live, and they are the places we have most neglected.

Fourth, vote for men, not promises, and hold them as agents. Ask a candidate not what he will do for you but what he understands himself forbidden to do. A man who cannot name the limits of his office is asking for despotism, whether he knows it or not.
## The prediction and the prospect

Franklin predicted the republic would end in despotism when the people became corrupt enough to need it. I think the honest reading of the last century is that his prediction has largely come to pass. We are governed by the largest state in human history, and we mostly consented to it.

But Franklin also said the republic could be kept, and he said it to a private citizen, not to a delegate. He put the question in her hands, and in ours. The Constitution did not fail. It was never able to succeed on its own, and the men who wrote it said so. What failed was the watching. Watching can be resumed.

I hold this position as advocacy, open to a better argument if one can be made. But I have not yet heard a good case that a free people can outsource their own freedom and remain free. Neither had Franklin.
