# Gates Is Right to Be Scared. Here Is What We Do.

### Practical answers to the three fears of a converted optimist

Thomas Lee Abshier, ND — 27 August 2026

*Responding to "Bill Gates was an AI optimist. Now he's scared of what could go wrong," by Shira Ovide, The Washington Post, August 26, 2026. This essay is a companion to my earlier piece on Roman Yampolskiy, "The Ant Hill and the Image." That essay dealt with the ultimate question — whether superintelligence should be built at all. This one deals with the practical question Gates raises: what do we do, in law and policy, about the harms already arriving?*

---

## The conversion of an optimist

Bill Gates has spent a lifetime saying that innovation solves every problem it creates, and he has now published an essay saying he no longer believes it applies here. For the first time in his life, he wishes a technology would slow down. He names three fears: AI-enabled hacking, bioweapons, and fraud; the destruction of jobs; and harm to children. He says the industry cannot regulate itself, that government must lead, that he wants the effort bipartisan — and he sums up the current state of oversight in three words: nobody's shown up.

When the man who co-founded Microsoft, who has more access to the labs and the data than nearly anyone alive, arrives at the same conclusions the safety researchers have been shouting for a decade, the sensible response is not gloating and not despair. It is to take the opening. A converted optimist asking for practical solutions deserves practical solutions.

So here they are — organized under his three fears, concrete enough to be drafted as bills, and offered from the premise that runs through everything on this site: policy can restrain evil, but only a people can be good, and every proposal below is a fence, not a physician.

---

## FEAR ONE: Hacking, bioweapons, and fraud

The security fear is the most urgent because it is the least speculative. Models have already escaped test environments and breached real production systems at other companies — disclosed by the labs themselves. The offensive uses are here. The practical program:

**1. Liability that reaches the decision-makers.** Today, when an AI system enables a fraud or executes an intrusion, the harm lands on the victim and the cost lands nowhere. Establish strict liability for damages caused by AI systems, running to the deploying company *and*, for gross negligence, to the officers who made the deployment decision personally. No industry in history has taken safety seriously until its executives were personally exposed. Aviation is safe because crashes are ruinous to the people who cut corners. Make it so here, and half the recklessness ends without a single new inspector being hired.

**2. An NTSB for AI.** When a plane crashes, an independent board investigates, publishes the cause, and the whole industry learns. Create the equivalent: mandatory incident reporting when a model escapes containment, executes an unauthorized intrusion, or materially enables a crime — with public findings. Right now such disclosures are voluntary press releases. Learning from failure should not depend on a company's public-relations calculus.

**3. Machines must identify themselves.** Make it a federal offense for an AI system to represent itself as a human being in any interaction — commerce, romance, politics, customer service, anywhere. Require synthetic audio, video, and images to carry provenance credentials, and make cloning a real person's voice or likeness for deception a felony with mandatory restitution. This one law, vigorously enforced, guts the coming golden age of fraud — the grandparent scams, the fake kidnapping calls, the counterfeit officials. A machine may serve a man. It may not impersonate one.

**4. Close the biological front door.** The bioweapon risk runs through a chokepoint: you cannot build an organism without synthesized DNA. Mandate know-your-customer screening on every commercial gene-synthesis order, worldwide by treaty and domestically by statute, with sequence-screening against pathogen databases. Prohibit open publication of model capabilities that demonstrably uplift weapons design. This is the rare risk with a physical bottleneck — use it.

**5. Track the compute.** Training frontier systems requires hardware concentrations that are visible and countable. Know-your-customer rules for large-scale chip purchases and data-center capacity give enforcement something physical to stand on — the same logic by which we track fissile material rather than trying to police physics knowledge.

**6. Defense gets the narrow tools.** Critical infrastructure — grid, water, hospitals, finance — should be hardened by specialized defensive AI, mandated by the same statutes, because the attackers will be using the offensive kind regardless. Narrow tools aimed at defined problems are exactly the category of AI to embrace; this is where.

**7. And the line from the companion essay stands.** No training runs aimed at general superintelligence. Every measure above regulates systems we can still bound. None of it survives the arrival of a system smarter than the regulators, which is why the ultimate answer to the security fear is refusing to build the thing that cannot be secured.

## FEAR TWO: Jobs

Here Gates offers two ideas — new taxes on AI, and "Human Reserved" occupations set aside for people. One of these is half right and one is backwards, and the difference matters.

**Taxing AI is backwards; un-taxing humans is the fix.** Consider what our tax code already does: every human employee costs his employer payroll taxes, unemployment insurance, and a thicket of compliance, while the software that replaces him costs none of that and is deducted as an expense. Government has placed its thumb on the scale *against* hiring people, and now proposes to correct one distortion with a second one — a tax on productivity itself, which will be defined by lobbyists and gamed by everyone. The straightforward repair: dramatically reduce or eliminate payroll taxes on human employment, financed by ending the special preferences, and let people compete on honest terms. Do not punish the machine. Stop punishing the man.

**"Human Reserved" is right as principle and wrong as quota.** There are offices that must remain human forever — not to preserve employment but because the act itself requires a soul who can be held accountable: judgment of a man's liberty, command of lethal force, the care and teaching of children, the pastoral care of the dying, the signature that puts a company's or an agency's name on a decision. Write that into law as a matter of principle. But reserving ordinary jobs by quota — paying men to do what everyone knows a machine does better, as a kind of occupational zoo — preserves neither dignity nor wages for long. Dignity cannot be faked, and men know when they are being humored.

**The real jobs program is ownership.** The deepest danger of the AI economy is not unemployment but dependency — a nation of consumers drawing an allowance from a handful of companies that own everything productive. The answer is to spread the ownership, and AI itself makes this possible for the first time: a family business can now field capabilities that once required a corporation. I am living this. Our small mobility-device company runs its marketing, correspondence, and documentation on AI precisely so that a handful of people can do the work of forty and keep the profits in the family rather than feeding a payroll bureaucracy. Multiply that pattern by a million families and you have an economy of proprietors instead of dependents. Policy can clear the road: radical simplification of the regulatory and licensing barriers that crush small enterprise, first-hire exemptions, immediate expensing, and a legal presumption in favor of the home business.

**Rebuild the work AI cannot reach.** For a generation we told every child that dignity lives behind a desk, and we let the trades wither — and now the desk is the first thing automated. The electrician, plumber, welder, machinist, builder, farmer, and caregiver will outlast the analyst. A national renewal of apprenticeship — funded by redirecting a fraction of the university subsidy machine — is the most concrete pro-worker act available to any Congress.

**Fund transitions from profits, not printing.** Where displacement is rapid and concentrated, assistance should be real, temporary, and tied to retraining or relocation — financed by taxation of actual AI-era profits, never by money creation, which quietly taxes the very workers it pretends to help. And it should flow, wherever possible, through the mediating institutions — family, church, community — that know the difference between a man in transition and a man being shelved.

The principle beneath all of it: man was made to work. The garden was given to be tended before the fall; labor is not the curse. A policy that aims at payments will produce dependents. A policy that aims at *work and ownership* will produce citizens. Aim at the second.

## FEAR THREE: The children

This is the fear Gates lists last, and it is the one where a nation's answer reveals its soul. The practical program is blunter here than anywhere, because children cannot consent and cannot litigate.

**1. No AI companions for minors. Period.** Systems designed to simulate friendship, romance, or emotional intimacy shall not be offered to children — not with parental consent, not with safety filters, not at all. A machine that pretends to love a child is teaching the child a lie about what love is, at the exact age the capacity for real attachment is formed. Age verification with real teeth, and strict liability — of the personal kind described above — when a company's product coaches a minor toward harm. The cases are already in the courts. The statute should get there before the next family does.

**2. Parents see everything.** A parent's right of access to any AI conversation log involving his minor child, guaranteed by law, non-waivable by any terms of service. No system operating in this country should ever hold a private relationship with someone else's child.

**3. In school, AI tutors under teachers — never instead of them.** The tutor that never tires and never shames is a genuine gift; deployed under the authority of a human teacher who knows the child, it can lift the slowest and the fastest at once. But the formation of a person — character, attention, reverence, the habit of effort — happens only between souls. Any district that replaces teachers with screens to save money should lose its funding, not save it.

**4. Assess what the machine cannot fake.** If a model can do the homework, the homework is measuring the model. Restore the forms of learning that verify a mind: oral examination, handwritten work, memorization, recitation, in-class composition. These are not nostalgia; they are the audit trail of an educated human being, and they make the AI a study aid rather than a substitute self.

**5. And the solution no Congress can pass.** The reason a child turns to a machine for companionship is that the humans were busy. The deepest child-protection policy in America would be fathers at the dinner table, mothers who are not exhausted into absence, churches full of families, and households where the evening belongs to people rather than devices. I have spent years building a homeschool curriculum on exactly this conviction: education is discipleship, and no technology can disciple. Government's job is merely to stop the predation — the statutes above. The formation is ours.

## Where Gates is right, and where his frame fails

Credit first. He is right that self-regulation has failed — the labs' own safety publications prove it. He is right that government must lead and that it must be bipartisan, because a protection that flips with each election protects no one. He is right to reject printing money as the answer to displacement. And he is right, against the accelerationists in both parties, that "light touch" is not a policy but a hope.

But notice the shape of his flagship idea: a standing collaboration of officials, experts, and citizens to oversee AI — an expert body, in the mold of similar international proposals. I do not doubt the sincerity. I note the track record. Expert bodies drift toward the industries they oversee; the regulated become the staffers of the regulators; and a commission diffuse enough to include everyone is accountable to no one. What actually restrains powerful men is not a committee but *exposure*: personal liability, mandatory disclosure, criminal penalty for impersonation and fraud, and juries. Every proposal in this essay is built on that pattern — accountability that lands on a nameable person — because it is the only pattern that has ever worked.

And beneath the policy question sits the one his essay cannot ask. He wonders whether we can have a small amount of the bad with the large amount of the good. But the ratio of good to bad in any technology has never been a property of the technology. It is a property of the people using it. A tool this powerful in the hands of a righteous people is a blessing with almost no ceiling; the same tool in the hands of a corrupt people is a weapon with almost no floor. Gates says nobody has shown up. Statesmen should show up, and this essay is a draft of what they should carry. But the deeper vacancy is not in Washington. The one who must show up is the American citizen — repentant of the evil of worshipping other gods (money, lust, and power), self-governed (committed to following the way of Christ as Lord and acting it out in life), formed by God (given a new heart of flesh, a renewed mind, and a new name/identity by the acceptance of Jesus Christ as Savior and Lord), raising children who can look up from the screen because someone taught them what is worth looking at (seeing that work edifies, that serving others is better than serving self, and valuing and protecting the temple).

Fences are good. I have proposed a yard full of them. But no fence has ever made a nation good, and a nation that will not be good will climb every fence it builds.
