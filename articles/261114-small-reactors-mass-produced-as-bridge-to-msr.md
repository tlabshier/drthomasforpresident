# 261114 - Small Reactors - Mass Produced as Bridge to MSR

# Small Reactors Now, Molten Salt Next:


### What the Military's Microreactors Can and Cannot Do for Data Centers and Water

**Thomas Lee Abshier, ND** | November 14, 2026


---


## The Question

The foundation of the New ERA Vision is abundant energy, and I have long held that the thorium molten-salt reactor (MSR) is the right destination: high temperature, low pressure, efficient use of fuel, and heat that can run industry, make fresh water from the sea, and power the computing that is reshaping our economy. But the MSR fleet I envision is still a decade or more away. Meanwhile, the U.S. military has begun putting small reactors on its bases. So the question is a fair one: can the small reactors being built for the Army and Air Force do now, or very soon, what we hope MSRs will eventually do — power data centers, desalinate water, and anchor local industry?

I stand on the side of building them, and building them now. But I want to be precise about what they can do, when, at what price, and at what scale, because overselling a good thing is its own kind of dishonesty. My answer, in brief: military-style microreactors are practical for remote sites, islands, critical facilities, edge-scale data centers, and small-to-medium desalination plants, beginning around 2028. They are not practical as the main power source for gigawatt-scale AI campuses, and they will not be "immediate." Their greatest value is that they are rebuilding the factories, fuel supply, regulatory pathways, and public trust that the molten-salt future will need.

## What the Military Is Actually Building

There are three main efforts, and their status as of this fall is concrete enough to judge.

**Project Pele.** This is the Department of War's (formerly Defense) prototype transportable reactor, built by BWXT. It is a high-temperature, helium-cooled design using TRISO fuel — uranium kernels coated in ceramic layers that hold their radioactivity at very high temperatures. It is designed to fit in four standard shipping containers and produce 1 to 5 megawatts of electricity (MWe). The fuel arrived at Idaho National Laboratory (INL) in November 2025. Formal system testing is planned as early as 2027, and BWXT says it expects to produce electricity at INL as soon as 2028. Pele was launched in 2019 with a five-year goal; it is running late, which is typical of first-of-a-kind nuclear work.

**The Army's Janus Program.** Announced in October 2025, Janus is the part designed to scale. On August 26, 2026, the Army selected five vendors and five installations, with contracts worth up to $2.2 billion:

The structure matters as much as the hardware. The companies own, build, and operate their reactors. The contracts are fixed-price and milestone-based: the Army pays only when a milestone is met and can stop funding a vendor that falls behind without a termination fee. Government funding covers construction and the first year of operation; after that the Army expects to buy power under ordinary power purchase agreements. The target is at least one reactor delivering usable electricity on an Army base by September 30, 2028. The Army does not expect all five to make that date. Over time it expects more than 20 reactors across military installations.

**Eielson Air Force Base, Alaska.** The Air Force intends to have Oklo build, own, and operate a roughly 5-MWe reactor that will supply both electricity and steam to the base, where the existing coal plant has a maximum capacity of 23 MW. The project was first announced with a 2027 target; the Air Force now estimates completion in 2030, and it still requires a Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) license.

Behind all of this sits the Department of Energy's Reactor Pilot Program, created by the May 2025 executive orders. It set a goal of three advanced reactors reaching criticality — a self-sustaining chain reaction — by July 4, 2026. Four did: Antares' Mark-0 (June 4), Valar Atomics' Ward 250 in Utah (June 18), Deployable Energy's Unity (June 30), and Aalo Atomics' test reactor (July 4). Westinghouse's eVinci completed zero-power criticality testing in August. These are real accomplishments; several reactors went from groundbreaking to criticality in under a year, something this country had not done in decades.

But proportion requires that I state the limit plainly: these were zero-power tests. None of those reactors is selling electricity to anyone. DOE itself describes the pilot program as research and development, not proof of commercial suitability. As of this writing, no American microreactor is delivering commercial power.

## The Scale Problem: Data Centers

Here is where enthusiasm most needs arithmetic.

U.S. data center capacity was about 62 gigawatts (GW) in March 2026, and S&P Global projects roughly 152 GW by 2030. That is about 90 GW of new demand in four years. The largest AI campuses now draw on the order of a gigawatt each; Epoch AI estimates the biggest one tracked is about 1,037 MW of computing load alone.

Now compare the reactors:


 	- One gigawatt from BWXT's 20-MWe BANR would take **50 reactors**.

 	- One gigawatt from 1-MWe units like Radiant's Kaleidos would take **1,000 reactors**.

 	- The entire Janus program, at more than 20 reactors mostly between 1 and 20 MWe, would add perhaps a few hundred megawatts at most — a fraction of a single large AI campus.


So microreactors cannot carry the main load of the AI buildout, and no honest advocate should say they can. The gigawatt campuses will be powered by the grid, by large reactors (including restarted plants like Three Mile Island Unit 1, now the Crane Clean Energy Center), by small modular reactors in the 50–300 MW range, and, in the meantime, by a great deal of natural gas.

Where microreactors do fit is at the smaller end and at the edge: data centers of 1 to 50 MW, sites far from transmission lines, and facilities that need power that cannot be cut. The commercial market is already reaching for exactly that niche. Equinix has an agreement with Radiant for 20 Kaleidos units. Aalo plans to pair its first commercial 50-MWe plant with an AI data center near INL. Deployable Energy has partnered with GridMarket to place its containerized 1-MWe units at data center sites. These are agreements, not operating plants, but they show where the fit is.

## The Price Question

The cost of a first-of-a-kind microreactor is high. There is still almost no real cost data, because almost none have been built; the best public estimates come from INL engineers working from the MARVEL test reactor. For a 20-megawatt-thermal design, they estimated about $234 per megawatt-hour (MWh) for the first unit and about $131/MWh once factories are turning out roughly ten units a year. Other INL cases published this year run from about $160 to several hundred dollars per MWh, depending on the design. For comparison, grid power for large industrial customers commonly runs well under $100/MWh.

That looks bad until you ask what a data center pays when the grid cannot serve it. S&P Global reports that all-in costs for on-site generation, including the backup and microgrid equipment a data center requires, now run about $160 to $170/MWh. Data centers are already paying that premium, mostly for gas turbines and engines, because power availability — not price — has become their binding constraint. A factory-built microreactor at $130–$160/MWh, with no fuel deliveries for years and no exposure to gas prices, would be competitive in that market. A first-unit reactor at $234 or more would not, except where reliability is worth almost any price.

The economic case therefore rests on one thing: building enough units on assembly lines to bring the cost down. That is precisely what the military's fixed-price, multi-unit purchases are designed to start.

## Water: A Better Fit Than Most People Realize

Desalination is where small reactors fit most naturally, because the scale matches.

The Carlsbad plant in Southern California, the largest seawater desalination plant in the Western Hemisphere, produces about 50 million gallons (190,000 cubic meters) of drinking water a day and draws roughly 35 to 38 MW of continuous power, using about 3.5 kilowatt-hours per cubic meter. Working from those figures:


 	- **One 1-MWe microreactor** could run a reverse-osmosis plant making about 1.8 million gallons a day — enough for a town of roughly 15,000 to 20,000 people at typical American residential use.

 	- **One 20-MWe BANR** could make about 36 million gallons a day — roughly three-quarters of Carlsbad.

 	- **Two** would run a Carlsbad-sized plant.


What would the water cost? A 2021 MIT study of a Carlsbad-sized plant put the non-energy costs (construction and operation) at about $0.79 per cubic meter. Adding microreactor electricity at INL's estimates gives:


 	- At $131/MWh (factory-built): about **$1.25 per cubic meter**, or about $1,540 per acre-foot.

 	- At $234/MWh (first unit): about **$1.61 per cubic meter**, or about $1,990 per acre-foot.


MIT estimated about $1.84 per cubic meter for a new stand-alone plant buying California grid power, and Carlsbad's actual water now costs around $2,700 per acre-foot. These are my calculations from published figures, with their assumptions about financing carried along, so I offer them as a reasonable range rather than a quote. But the direction is clear: in water-scarce coastal places with expensive power, microreactor-driven desalination is already in the same price range as what those places pay today.

There is a further advantage that points straight at the MSR. Gas-cooled and molten-salt reactors produce high-temperature heat, and INL estimates levelized heat costs far below their electricity costs — roughly $42 to $145 per megawatt-hour of heat in its 2026 cases. Thermal desalination methods can run on that heat, or on the leftover heat after electricity is made. This is why I see desalination as one of the strongest long-term uses of the MSR: one plant making power for the town and water for the farms. Natura Resources has already signed an agreement to pair a future 100-MW molten-salt reactor with thermal treatment of oil-field water in the Permian Basin.

The same logic applies to military and island sites. A base in the Pacific or in the Arctic that needs both power and water, and today depends on shipped diesel, is exactly the customer a containerized reactor was designed for.

## Where the Critics Are Right

I want to give the strongest form of the case against, because a vision that cannot survive its best critics is not worth holding.

**Diseconomies of scale.** M. V. Ramana of the University of British Columbia, a physicist and a leading critic, argues that larger reactors are cheaper per megawatt because materials and labor do not shrink in proportion to output, and that the history of nuclear construction gives no reason to expect costs to fall. MIT's Jacopo Buongiorno, who is an advocate of microreactors, lists the same diseconomies among the "potentially crippling uncertainties." This is a real problem. The only answer is factory production at volume, and that has not yet been demonstrated.

**Fuel.** Nearly all of these designs use HALEU — uranium enriched to between 5 and 20 percent. As of mid-2026, Centrus Energy's plant in Piketon, Ohio, was the only U.S. producer, with something over 900 kilograms delivered to DOE. A ban on Russian enriched uranium takes full effect in 2028. Until enrichment, conversion, fuel fabrication, and certified shipping containers all scale up together, fuel will limit how many reactors can be built, no matter how many are ordered.

**Security and regulation.** Many small reactors spread across the country mean many sites to guard. Critics warn that pressure to cut security staffing to make the economics work could make a sabotaged reactor dangerous. Rules for transporting fueled reactors, refueling them, and running them with few operators are still being written.

**Schedules.** Pele was a five-year program launched in 2019; it will produce power around 2028. Eielson slid from 2027 to 2030. First-of-a-kind nuclear projects run late, and I see no reason to assume these will be exceptions.

These objections do not overturn my position. They define it. They tell us microreactors will be expensive at first, limited by fuel, and slower than promised — which is exactly why the government, as a customer with a genuine need for resilient power, is the right party to buy the first units and carry the first-of-a-kind risk. The objections argue against hype, not against building.

## Why Microreactors Are the Bridge to the Molten-Salt Future

The word "immediately" in the original question deserves a careful answer. Microreactors will not be powering data centers or desalination plants in any quantity before about 2028–2030. But compared with an MSR fleet, which realistically arrives in the 2030s, they are years ahead, and they are building what the MSR will need:


 	- **Factories.** A reactor industry that has learned to build units on an assembly line can build molten-salt units on one.

 	- **Fuel supply.** Every HALEU order from a microreactor vendor helps justify the enrichment plants that early MSRs will also draw on, until thorium breeding takes over.

 	- **Regulatory pathways.** DOE and Army authorization outside the traditional NRC process, now proven on the pilot reactors, creates precedents any advanced design can use.

 	- **A workforce.** Operators, welders, inspectors, and engineers trained on microreactors are the people who will build MSRs.

 	- **Public trust.** A dozen small reactors running quietly on Army bases for several years will do more to change public opinion than any argument I can write.


The MSR program itself is moving. Natura's MSR-1 at Abilene Christian University, the first liquid-fueled reactor to receive an NRC construction permit, received about 4,700 pounds of molten salt from DOE in September and has DOE approval of its safety design agreement. It is a 1-megawatt-thermal research reactor that will not make electricity, and sources disagree on when it will first operate — the company has spoken of 2026, while a July Fortune profile put it at 2028. Either way, it will be the first fueled molten-salt reactor in this country since Oak Ridge in the 1960s.

## The Moral Ground

My test for any public policy is: Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? Would I want it done to me?

Providing a nation's defenders with power that cannot be cut off by a storm, a cyberattack, or a broken supply line is righteous; it is the duty of a government that asks men to stand watch. *"A prudent man foreseeth the evil, and hideth himself; but the simple pass on, and are punished."* **(Proverbs 22:3)** Bringing affordable fresh water to dry places and reliable power to communities is loving to our neighbors, and over the long run to the poorest nations of the world, for whom energy is the difference between subsistence and a decent life.

The third question governs how we do it. I would not want a reactor placed beside my home without my knowledge or consent, and so I support the Army's stated practice of not placing reactors where local communities object. Persuasion, transparency, and genuine local consent are the right way. Honest pricing is part of that respect: we owe our neighbors the true cost, including the first-of-a-kind premium, not a sales pitch.

## My Position

Military microreactors are practical now for the uses they were designed for, and practical within a few years for small data centers, island and remote communities, critical facilities, and desalination plants of town-to-city scale. They are not a solution for gigawatt AI campuses, and they will not be cheap until they are built in volume. I support the military programs, I support extending the same contractor-owned, buy-the-power model to water utilities and data centers, and I support federal effort to expand domestic HALEU and fuel fabrication, which is the real bottleneck. And I hold that all of this should be understood and funded as a bridge, not the destination. The destination is still the thorium molten-salt reactor: abundant, efficient, high-temperature energy that can make power, water, and industry plentiful for every neighbor.

## Sources and Their Limits

This article relies mainly on government announcements (Army, Air Force, DOE, INL), trade publications (POWER Magazine, American Nuclear Society Nuclear News, World Nuclear News), and industry trackers. Government and vendor sources are interested parties: they have reason to present schedules and milestones favorably, and their claims about future dates should be read as targets, not facts. Cost figures for microreactors are estimates made before any commercial unit has operated; the INL numbers come from engineers who openly note high uncertainty. The water-cost figures are my own calculations from published MIT and INL figures and inherit their assumptions. Critical voices (Ramana and others) are included, but the record I drew on leans toward mainstream and industry reporting; independent cost data simply does not yet exist, and it will not until reactors run for several years.
