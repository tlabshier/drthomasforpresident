# 261115 - Tarrifs for Compliance Equalization

# The Compliance Equalization Charge


### Either We Value Life, Health, and the Land, or We Don't

Thomas Lee Abshier, ND · 15 November 2026

## My Position

America requires its own businesses to keep workers safe and to keep air, water, and land clean. Those rules cost money, and we have chosen to impose those costs on ourselves because we believe life, health, and the land God gave us are worth protecting. Then we let goods into our market from producers who bear none of those costs. The result is that we punish Americans for producing in America under American morals, and we reward foreign producers for the very conduct we forbid at home.

I stand on this: **an import charge that equalizes the cost of our own safety and environmental laws is not a protective tariff.**** It is a moral charge.** It does not shelter an inefficient business from honest competition. It shelters an honest business from a government that has taxed it for doing right and then handed its market to those who do wrong. Either we value the environment and labor safety, or we don't. If we do, we cannot excuse buying cheaper goods made by violating the standards we require of our own citizens.

I call this a **compliance equalization charge**. It is legitimate only when it is fenced by strict safeguards, which this paper sets out.

## Four Reasons a Business Cannot Compete

When an American business cannot match the price of an import, the reason is usually one of these:


 	- **Labor costs.** Wages differ because standards of living differ between nations. This is ordinary comparative advantage, and it is not a wrong.

 	- **Management.** Some businesses are simply run less well than their rivals.

 	- **Capital and automation.** A shop of hand labor cannot match a factory of machines and, increasingly, artificial intelligence.

 	- **Sourcing.** Some producers have better access to materials, local resources, or supply arrangements.


A tariff that shields a business from any of these four is a protective tariff, and I oppose it. It makes every American consumer pay more so that a favored producer can keep a market he has not earned. That is exactly the legal plunder Bastiat condemned.

There is a fifth reason, and it is different in kind. **The government itself has imposed a cost on the American producer that his foreign rival does not bear.** This is the only case the compliance equalization charge addresses. It does not answer for low wages abroad, poor management at home, a lack of machinery, or a sourcing deal. It answers only for the cost that our own law created.

## Why It Is Not Plunder

Bastiat's test for plunder is whether the law takes from some to give to others. A protective tariff does: it takes from consumers to give to a favored producer. The compliance equalization charge does not, for two reasons.

**It restores equality before the law.** The law first placed a burden on American producers. Charging the same burden on imports that escaped it gives the American producer nothing his foreign competitor lacks. Every good sold in the American market then carries the same moral cost, wherever it was made. That is what a righteous law should do: *"Ye shall do no unrighteousness in judgment: thou shalt not respect the person of the poor, nor honour the person of the mighty."* **(Leviticus 19:15)** A law that burdens only the honest producer and spares the dishonest one respects persons.

**It refuses complicity.** When we buy a good made by poisoning a river or maiming workers, we share in the wrong, even if we never see it. Paul warned against exactly this: *"neither be partaker of other men's sins: keep thyself pure."* **(1 Timothy 5:22)** James spoke of the wages of workers cheated by those who live in comfort: *"Behold, the hire of the labourers who have reaped down your fields, which is of you kept back by fraud, crieth: and the cries of them which reaped are entered into the ears of the Lord of sabaoth."* **(James 5:4)** A nation that requires righteousness of its own producers and then buys freely from the unrighteous has not escaped the sin. It has only moved it offshore.

My three questions confirm this. *Is it righteous?* Yes: it applies one standard to all. *Is it loving?* Yes: it protects American workers from being punished for obeying good laws, and it presses foreign producers toward treating their own workers and land better. *Would I want it done to me?* If I obeyed the law at real cost and then lost my livelihood to a man who broke it, I would want the law to stand with me.

## Precedents

The principle is not new. It already stands in American law at its most extreme, and other nations are now applying it to lesser harms.


 	- **The forced-labor ban.** Section 307 of the Tariff Act of 1930 has long prohibited importing goods made wholly or in part by forced, convict, or indentured labor, including forced child labor. The Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act of 2021 strengthened it with a *rebuttable presumption*: goods from China's Xinjiang region are presumed to be made with forced labor unless the importer proves otherwise by clear and convincing evidence. There, America refuses the goods outright. The compliance equalization charge applies the same moral logic to lesser harms by pricing them rather than banning them.

 	- **The European Union's carbon border charge.** On January 1, 2026, the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism entered its definitive phase. Importers of steel, aluminum, cement, fertilizer, hydrogen, and electricity must now account for the carbon emitted in making those goods, so that the price charged on imports matches what European producers pay under their own rules. China responded by threatening retaliation.

 	- **The Foreign Pollution Fee Act.** Senators Bill Cassidy and Lindsey Graham introduced this bill in 2023 and again in April 2025. It would charge a fee on certain industrial imports based on how much more pollution their production causes compared with American production.


The European charge and the Senate bill are both limited to carbon. The principle I am defending is broader: it applies to any rule that protects life and health, including worker safety and pollution of shared air and water. It is also narrower, because it is fenced by the safeguards below.

## Safeguards

A moral tariff is the easiest disguise a protective tariff can wear. Critics of the Foreign Pollution Fee Act pointed out that both its sponsors represent states with large industries that would benefit from a tariff on foreign competitors. That does not prove the bill wrong, but it shows the danger. The charge must be built so that lobbyists cannot steer it. I propose six safeguards.


 	- **A published formula, not a negotiated rate.** The charge for each product is set by measuring the actual difference in compliance cost between American production and the foreign production, using published data and a public method. No industry gets a rate by asking for one.

 	- **Prove compliance and pay nothing.** Any foreign producer who shows, through independent verification, that he meets standards equivalent to ours pays no charge. This turns the charge from a wall into an invitation to do right. It reverses the burden of the forced-labor law: there the importer must prove innocence, but here the honest producer is rewarded for proving it.

 	- **Only rules that protect life and health qualify.** The charge may equalize only the cost of rules that pass the protection test: worker safety, and pollution of air, water, and land that others share. It may not equalize the cost of rules that protect an industry, a profession, or a bureaucracy.

 	- **Review the rule along with the charge.** If an American regulation is excessive, the charge would lock in the excess and make Americans pay for it twice, once at home and once at the border. Every rule that supports a charge should be reviewed on a fixed schedule. A rule that cannot justify its cost should be repealed, and the charge with it. "Either we value it or we don't" cuts both ways: once the cost of each rule is visible at the border, we must decide honestly whether it is worth what it costs.

 	- **No charge for the four ordinary causes.** Wage levels, management, capital, and sourcing are excluded by statute. The charge measures only the cost created by our own law.

 	- **Enacted by Congress, never by decree.** The power to tax imports belongs to Congress. The Supreme Court confirmed this in February 2026, when it held that a president may not impose tariffs under emergency powers.


## Objections Answered

**"Consumers will pay more."** They will, for goods whose low price came from harm done elsewhere. That price was never honest. The consumer was buying the injury of a worker or the poisoning of a river along with the product, and the charge only makes him pay the true cost.

**"It imposes our morals on other nations."** It does not govern any other nation. Each nation may make its own laws for its own people. The charge governs only what we will buy and on what terms. A man may refuse to buy stolen goods without claiming authority over the thief's country.

**"Poor nations cannot afford our standards yet."** That is a serious concern. The charge does not penalize low wages, which are the main advantage of poorer nations, and it rewards any producer who meets the standards, so it offers a path upward rather than a wall. Nations that invest in safer work and cleaner production are rewarded with access to the largest consumer market in the world.

**"It will be impossible to measure."** Measurement is hard. Analysts of the Senate bill warned that accurate estimates of pollution intensity for each product and country will be difficult to obtain. That is why the proof-of-compliance route matters: where the data are uncertain, the producer who wants to avoid the charge has every reason to supply verified data himself. A conservative default can apply when no data are given.

**"Other nations will retaliate."** Some will, as China has threatened against Europe. A nation that will not defend its own moral standards for fear of retaliation has already decided that those standards are not worth defending.

## Why the Concern Remains Urgent

American manufacturing has been hollowed out over decades, and a large part of the reason is that we exported not only jobs but harm. Production moved to places where workers could be endangered and land poisoned more cheaply, and we bought the results. Many Americans sense that there is something wrong with a system that congratulates itself on clean air at home while its stores are stocked with goods made in smoke. That sense is right. The money motive is plain: lower compliance costs mean higher margins for those who import. And the link between doctrine and behavior is plain too. A nation that believes its moral standards stop at the water's edge will eventually stop believing them at home.
