# 261031 - Wokism Part 6 - Justice, Crime, and Borders

# The False Gods of Wokism


## Part 6: Justice Reversed — Crime, the Border, and the Sword


by Thomas Lee Abshier, ND 11/1/2026


## Plain language summary


The woke religion applies its central doctrine, guilt by category rather than by deed, to crime and the border. The offender is treated mainly as a victim of the system, so the answer to crime becomes fewer police, less prosecution, and less punishment. The migrant who crosses illegally is treated as an oppressed person fleeing Western injustice, so enforcing the border becomes cruelty. Some of the reforms these ideas produced were tried in American cities between 2020 and 2024, and voters in many of those cities reversed them. The Christian answer begins where every part of this series begins: God lives every life from the inside. He lives the life of the shopkeeper who is robbed, the mother whose son is shot, and the migrant child trafficked across a desert. Mercy that ignores the victim is not mercy; it abandons the one God feels most at that moment. But God also lives the life of the offender and the stranger, so justice must aim at repentance and restoration, not revenge. The Cross holds both: sin fully answered, and the sinner offered a way home. On the border, Scripture teaches both that nations and their boundaries are God’s idea and that the stranger is under His particular protection. Love is owed to every man; trust is earned by every man. The remedies: enforce the laws we have, against employers first; prosecute violent crime; fix asylum by deciding cases quickly; never use prosecution as a political weapon, whichever party holds power; and restore the citizen jury as the conscience of the law.


---


## Rick Kirchner’s terms


Three entries in Rick Kirchner’s glossary belong here: *the crime softness*, *the border as political project*, and *the weaponized justice system*. His language was sharp, and in places sharper than the evidence allows. Not every city is “run by the people who loot them,” and not every Democrat wants open borders. But underneath each entry is a real concern, and I want to state it in a form that will survive the first critic.


This part also needs a distinction that the earlier parts did not. Race, gender, and the tolerance doctrine are articles of the woke creed. Crime and immigration are areas where honest people disagree about policy for reasons that have nothing to do with that creed. A man can want shorter sentences for nonviolent offenders, or more legal immigration, without believing that the offender is a victim of the system or that borders are a colonial invention. My argument is against the doctrine, not against every reform.


## The doctrine stated fairly


The woke doctrine of crime and the border holds that:


- **Crime is mainly caused by the system.** Poverty, racism, and the trauma of past incarceration drive people to crime, so the offender is first of all a victim.

- **Punishment perpetuates oppression.** Police and prisons disproportionately harm minority communities. The answer is to reduce them: “defund the police,” cashless bail, declining to prosecute lower-level offenses, and decarceration.

- **Borders are arbitrary and unjust.** National boundaries were drawn by colonial powers. People who cross them without permission are fleeing violence and poverty that the West helped create.

- **Enforcement is cruelty.** Deporting people who have built lives here, or detaining those who arrive, is inhumane. Cities should be sanctuaries.


The steelman goes like this: “America imprisons more of its people than almost any nation on earth. A poor man can sit in jail for months awaiting trial because he cannot pay bail, while a rich man charged with the same crime goes home. Black Americans are arrested and imprisoned at higher rates. Many migrants are fleeing gangs that would kill them. A just society asks why people commit crimes and why they flee their homes, not only how to punish them.”


There is truth in that argument, and a Christian must hear it. Bail that depends on wealth rather than danger is unjust. The number of federal crimes has grown so large that no citizen can know them all. Some migrants really are fleeing for their lives. *“Thou shalt neither vex a stranger, nor oppress him”* (Exodus 22:21).


But the doctrine draws a conclusion from these truths that Scripture does not.


## The God who lives the victim’s life, and the offender’s


As I have argued throughout this series, God does not merely watch our lives. He lives them with us, from the inside. *“In him we live, and move, and have our being”* (Acts 17:28).


Apply that truth to a crime. When a man breaks into a home, God lives the terror of the family inside. When a shopkeeper’s store is looted for the third time and he closes for good, God lives his loss. When a mother identifies her son’s body, God lives her grief. *“In all their affliction he was afflicted”* (Isaiah 63:9). A justice system that waves the offender through, out of sympathy for him, has ignored the person whose life God is feeling most sharply at that moment. That is not mercy. It is the abandonment of the victim.


Scripture speaks to this directly: *“He that justifieth the wicked, and he that condemneth the just, even they both are abomination to the LORD”* (Proverbs 17:15). The woke doctrine of crime does both. It excuses the offender because of his category, and it suspects the police officer and the citizen who defends himself because of theirs.


But God also lives the offender’s life. He felt the fatherlessness, the poverty, and the bad influences that shaped him, and He feels the sin he chose. *“I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked; but that the wicked turn from his way and live”* (Ezekiel 33:11). So justice must not become revenge. It must answer the wrong fully and leave the door open for the offender to repent and be restored.


That is what the Cross does. In the argument I have laid out in *The Premise Change*, sin is not waved away; it is answered in full, because God’s holiness cannot abide it and wrongdoing does not fade on its own. And the answer is offered back to the sinner as a way home. Justice and mercy are not opposites to be traded off. In the Cross they meet. A justice system worthy of a Christian people does the same in its own limited way: it holds the offender accountable for what he did, it makes the victim whole as far as it can, and it gives the offender a real path back.


The woke doctrine splits these apart. It offers the offender mercy without accountability, and it offers the victim nothing.


## The border: nations and the stranger


In *Enforce the Law. Count Honestly. Love the Stranger.* (August 2026), I set out what Scripture requires on immigration. Two truths stand side by side.


**Nations and their boundaries are God’s idea.** The same verse that says God *“hath made of one blood all nations of men”* goes on to say that He *“hath determined the times before appointed, and the bounds of their habitation”* (Acts 17:26). Nations are not a fallen invention, and the magistrate who bears the sword to enforce the law is doing a duty God assigned (Romans 13:4). Open borders are not a biblical position. They are an abdication of one.


**The stranger is under God’s particular protection.** *“The stranger that dwelleth with you shall be unto you as one born among you, and thou shalt love him as thyself”* (Leviticus 19:34). God ties His own reputation to how a nation treats the vulnerable outsider, and He does it in the very books that establish Israel’s borders and laws.


The two truths are reconciled by a principle I stated in that paper: **love is owed to every man; trust is earned by every man.** Every person on this soil bears God’s image, and I am commanded to deal justly with him, feed him if he is hungry, and remember that Christ died for him, whatever his papers say. But love does not require me to hand a stranger my keys, my daughter’s safety, or my nation’s vote. A father who cannot tell the difference is not loving his household. He is neglecting it.


And here again, God lives every life. He lives the life of the migrant who dies of thirst in the Arizona desert, and of the child sold by a smuggler. A border policy that invites millions to make that journey, in the name of compassion, has not been compassionate to them. In fiscal year 2022, U.S. Border Patrol recorded more than 850 migrant deaths along the southwest border, the most ever reported. The cartels that move people and drugs across the border earn billions from a border that does not hold. Compassion that enriches traffickers and fills the desert with bodies is not compassion.


## Testing the doctrine


### 1. Guilt by category, again


Part 1 showed that the woke religion moves guilt from deeds to categories. In crime, this means the offender’s group membership counts in his favor, and the officer’s or the victim’s counts against him. Scripture refuses this: *“Thou shalt not respect the person of the poor, nor honour the person of the mighty: but in righteousness shalt thou judge thy neighbour”* (Leviticus 19:15). Notice that the verse forbids favoring the poor in judgment, as well as the mighty. Sympathy for a man’s circumstances is right. Letting it decide his guilt is not.


### 2. Who is protected by fewer police?


The doctrine claims to protect minority communities. But the burden of violent crime falls most heavily on those same communities. Black Americans are victims of homicide at several times the rate of white Americans. When Gallup asked Black Americans in 2020, in the middle of the “defund” movement, whether they wanted police to spend more time, the same amount of time, or less time in their neighborhoods, 81 percent wanted the same or more.


When the national homicide rate jumped by roughly 30 percent in 2020, the largest one-year increase ever recorded, the victims were disproportionately the people the doctrine claimed to be helping. A policy that leaves the poor less safe in the name of protecting them has confused its slogans with its results.


### 3. Who pays for the open border?


The doctrine presents open borders as generosity. But someone pays. In my August paper, I pointed to the commission chaired by Barbara Jordan, a Black Democratic congresswoman and civil rights hero, which concluded in the 1990s that unchecked low-skilled immigration hurts low-wage American workers, disproportionately Black and Hispanic citizens, and recommended reducing it. The benefits of cheap labor go to employers. The costs land on the schools, emergency rooms, and neighborhoods of working people who were never asked. The people who decide the policy rarely live in the zip codes that absorb it. That is government without consent.


### 4. Selective enforcement corrodes all law


When a government decides which laws it will enforce, it has claimed the power to repeal laws by ignoring them. A legal system that is strict with the compliant citizen and lenient with the defiant teaches everyone that obedience is for fools. That is true when it is applied to the border, to a city’s shoplifters, or to a favored political ally. And it is the reason the third of Rick Kirchner’s terms matters.


### 5. The weaponized prosecutor


In *Legality Is Not Righteousness* (August 2026), I followed Jefferson’s warning that law is often the tyrant’s will. Tyranny does not usually break the law. It becomes the law. It walks in through the front door, holding signed documents, accompanied by officials, and titled something noble.


When prosecutors decline to charge violent offenders of one kind while pursuing political opponents of another, the law has become a weapon. I will not pretend this disease belongs to one party. Both have used prosecutors and agencies against their opponents, and the rule I apply is the one I applied to surveillance: if you would not hand a power to your enemy, do not hand it to your friend. The same standard binds an administration of my own party.


## Applying the test


**Is it righteous?** No. Scripture forbids justifying the wicked and condemning the just (Proverbs 17:15), and it forbids favoring anyone in judgment because of his category (Leviticus 19:15). A doctrine that excuses offenders by class and abandons the borders God established contradicts the character of the Judge.


**Is it loving?** No. It is not loving to the victim, whose wound it ignores. It is not loving to the poor neighborhoods left with less protection. It is not loving to the migrant lured into a deadly journey, or to the low-wage worker whose wages fall. And it is not loving to the offender, who is denied the accountability that might turn him around.


**Would I want it done to me?** If my home were broken into, I would want the man caught, the harm repaired, and the man given a chance to change. If I were a stranger coming to a new country, I would want clear rules, honestly enforced, and a quick and fair hearing. No one, measured by the self committed to righteousness, wants to live under a law that is enforced against some and not others.


## Why the concern is not overblown


A critic will say that crime has fallen since 2022, that border crossings dropped sharply in 2024 and 2025, and that the panic is outdated. Some of that is true. Homicides fell substantially after 2022, and southwest border encounters fell from record highs. But those changes came largely from reversing the policies the doctrine produced.


**Voters tried the experiment and rejected it.** In 2022, San Francisco recalled its district attorney, Chesa Boudin, who had campaigned on reducing prosecution. In 2024, Los Angeles voters removed George Gascón. Also in 2024, California voters passed Proposition 36 by a wide margin, partially reversing the 2014 measure that had reduced many theft and drug offenses to misdemeanors. Cities that cut police budgets in 2020 largely restored them. When the people who live under a policy reject it decisively, that is evidence.


**The border was not a hypothetical.** Southwest border encounters exceeded two million in fiscal year 2022 and again in 2023, the highest numbers ever recorded. Those numbers fell when enforcement policy changed, which is proof that policy, not fate, drives them.


**The spirit is reversed justice.** *“Woe unto them that call evil good, and good evil… which justify the wicked for reward, and take away the righteousness of the righteous from him!”* (Isaiah 5:20, 23). A doctrine that treats the offender as victim and the officer as oppressor has not merely made a policy mistake. It has inverted the moral order, and a nation that inverts justice will feel the consequences.


**It is paid for.** Cheap labor is profitable to employers who hire illegally, and smuggling is profitable to cartels. When an arrangement makes money for the powerful and passes the costs to the powerless, it will persist until someone stops paying the bill.


## Where the argument must be careful


Some reform is right. American bail too often depends on wealth rather than danger. The federal criminal code and the regulations behind it have grown so vast that ordinary people commit offenses they have never heard of, a point on which the civil-liberties left and I agree. Some police misconduct is real and must be prosecuted.


On immigration, most immigrants, including most who are here illegally, are not criminals beyond their immigration violation. Studies generally find that immigrants are incarcerated at lower rates than native-born Americans. Most fentanyl smuggled across the southern border comes through legal ports of entry, often carried by U.S. citizens. These facts matter, and I will not hide them.


None of them changes the argument. The case for enforcing the border does not rest on the claim that immigrants are criminals. It rests on the principle that a nation’s laws must mean what they say, that the costs of illegal immigration fall on people who were never asked, and that a lawless border feeds the traffickers who prey on migrants themselves.


## What I would do


**On the border**, from *Enforce the Law. Count Honestly. Love the Stranger.*:


- **Count honestly.** Publish a quarterly accounting of the unauthorized population, with the full methodology open to audit.

- **Enforce against employers first.** Make E-Verify universal, and prosecute the owners and officers who knowingly hire illegally. No one crosses a desert for a job that does not exist.

- **Secure the border and keep it secured,** with barriers, sensors, and manpower where each fits. Border and interior enforcement are one system.

- **Fix asylum by deciding cases quickly.** Fund the immigration courts until a claim is heard in weeks, not years. Grant genuine claims promptly; deny false ones promptly.

- **Publish removal priorities:** violent criminals first, then recent entrants, then those who have absconded from final orders.

- **No amnesty before enforcement,** and no permanent underclass living outside the protection of the law.

- **Two things I will not do:** deploy the military for domestic civil immigration enforcement, or permit masked, unidentifiable federal agents on American streets. And threatening an officer’s family should be prosecuted as the federal crime it is.


**On crime and justice:**


- **Prosecute violent crime, consistently.** A prosecutor who refuses to enforce the law against violent offenders should answer to the voters, and the voters have shown they will hold him to account.

- **Make the victim whole.** Biblical justice centers restitution: *“he should make full restitution”* (Exodus 22:3). The victim’s loss should be repaired, as far as possible, by the one who caused it.

- **Shrink the criminal code.** As I proposed in *Reforming the Administrative State*, rules should expire unless renewed, and no man should face a penalty a jury of his neighbors has not approved.

- **Restore the jury.** The citizen jury is the conscience of the law. When a law is unjust or absurd in a real case, twelve neighbors may refuse to enforce it. Jury nullification is not a loophole; it is the people’s oldest check on tyranny that parades as law.

- **One standard for the powerful.** Prosecutions of political figures, of any party, must meet the same standard as any citizen’s, no stricter and no looser.


## The better way


The woke doctrine of crime offers the offender mercy without accountability and offers the victim nothing. Its doctrine of the border offers the migrant an invitation without a law, and the citizen a cost without a voice. Both are the fruit of a religion that has no God who lives every life, and so must choose whose suffering counts.


The Christian answer does not have to choose. God lives the life of the victim and of the offender, of the citizen and of the stranger. His justice answers every wrong fully, and His mercy offers every wrongdoer a way home. That is what the Cross accomplished, and it is the pattern a Christian nation should follow in its courts and at its borders: firm law, honestly enforced, applied equally to all, and always with a door open to the one who repents.


## Where to push


- **Does “God lives the victim’s life” make punishment about the victim rather than about justice?** I say justice is answering the wrong fully, and the victim is the one who felt it. Show me where that leads to revenge.

- **Can a nation love the stranger and still enforce its border?** I say love is owed to every man and trust is earned by every man. Show me a case where honest enforcement is unloving.

- **Is jury nullification a safeguard or a danger?** Juries have nullified just laws as well as unjust ones. I hold that it is the people’s proper check, exposed to public view. Show me a better one.
