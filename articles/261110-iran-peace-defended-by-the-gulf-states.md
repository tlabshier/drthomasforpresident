# 261110 - Iran Peace Defended by the Gulf States

# A Peace the Region Can Keep


## Qatar's Channel, a Gulf-Led Watch, and the Way Out of the Iran War

*Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | A position paper · November 10, 2026*

**Resource:** Jack Posobiec, *Human Events* SITREP, November 10, 2026 (email newsletter), item 3.


---


## Plain language summary

After nine months of war with Iran, the administration is talking more about peace. Qatar has been carrying messages between Washington and Tehran. The Vice President has reportedly asked for a meeting with a senior Iranian leader. *Human Events* reports that the President has raised the idea of handing the job of watching Iran to a force led by the Gulf Arab nations, so that America no longer stands at the front. I support moving in that direction. The war has cost American lives, tens of billions of dollars, and the trust of the voters, and its stated aims cannot be won from the air. But a Gulf-led watch is only as good as its terms. Iran must not get a nuclear weapon, and that has to be verified, not promised. The Strait of Hormuz must be open. The Gulf states are not neutral referees: they have been attacked, and they have their own rivalries. American support should be real but limited. Any new offensive action should go to Congress first.


---


## Where things stand

The war began on February 28, 2026, with coordinated American and Israeli strikes on Iran. Iran retaliated across the Gulf and closed the Strait of Hormuz to most shipping. Ceasefires have come and gone. The United States imposed a naval blockade on Iranian ports in April and resumed it in July. By early November the war had cost at least $38 billion and nineteen American lives, and an AP survey of more than 100,000 voters found roughly six in ten Americans believed the decision to use force against Iran was wrong.

In the week since the midterm elections, the President has acknowledged that the war contributed to the Republican losses, while saying he does not regret it. The Vice President went further, writing that "the reason we lost is the war." On November 9, after meeting Qatar's Prime Minister at the White House, the President said the war was effectively over militarily and that he expected it to end fairly soon. He also said Qatar, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Bahrain, and Kuwait prefer that the American blockade continue, and that a nuclear-armed Iran remains non-negotiable.

Qatar has carried messages between Washington and Tehran throughout the war. In recent weeks its officials traveled to Tehran to revive the ceasefire, hosted Iranian negotiators in Doha, and publicly denied false reports that the talks had collapsed. Qatar has a personal stake: it has been struck in this region's wars by both Israel and Iran.

The SITREP reports two further developments that I have not been able to confirm independently. One is a Truth Social post by the President raising the prospect of a Gulf-led force, built on the Gulf Cooperation Council and perhaps other regional partners, to monitor Iran and take over America's leading role. The other is a letter from Vice President Vance, approved by the President, seeking a conversation with Mohsen Rezaei, a veteran Revolutionary Guard commander and senior Iranian official, with a meeting reported by Turkish media but unconfirmed. I treat both as reported, not established. The argument below holds either way, because the question is what America *should* do.

## Why I support this direction

My standing position is that the warrants for war are an actual attack or a real threat of attack, in defense. A war to remake another nation's government by force is not a warrant. Liberation of a people's hearts and minds is the work of persuasion and the Gospel, not bombs. Measured by that standard, the question now is not how to win a war of regime change. It is how to secure America and our partners against the real threat, an Iranian nuclear weapon and Iranian attacks on shipping and neighbors, at the least cost in lives.

Three reasons favor a Gulf-led arrangement.

**First, the war's aims cannot be achieved by continuing it in its present form.** Air power and blockade have done great damage to Iran's military, but no air campaign in history has produced regime change against an intact security apparatus, and Iran's strategy has always been to impose costs, not to win battles. Continuing a war that cannot reach its stated end, at nineteen American lives and counting, fails the test of counting the cost: *"For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost, whether he have sufficient to finish it?"* **(Luke 14:28)**.

**Second, the nations with the most at stake should carry the most of the watch.** The Gulf states live next to Iran. Their desalination plants, export terminals, and cities have been struck. They have every interest in a durable arrangement, and they have the wealth to pay for it. America should stand behind them with intelligence, naval support, and arms sales. A permanent American front line in the Gulf invites Iran to make every American sailor a target. Gulf leaders have also repeatedly persuaded the President to call off planned escalations, in May and again in August. They have shown that they want this war ended, not widened.

**Third, it is the peacemaker's path, and the peacemaker is blessed.** *"Blessed are the peacemakers: for they shall be called the children of God"* **(Matthew 5:9)**. *"If it be possible, as much as lieth in you, live peaceably with all men"* **(Romans 12:18)**. "If it be possible" is an honest qualifier. Peace with a regime that chants for our death may not be fully possible. But the duty is to go "as much as lieth in you," and we have not yet exhausted that. Speaking to an enemy is not surrender. It is how wars end without one side's annihilation.

Measured by the policy test: Is it righteous? Defending ourselves and our neighbors against a nuclear threat is righteous, and so is ending an unwinnable war that kills our sons. Is it loving? It is loving to the American families who bear the cost, to the Gulf peoples whose cities are targets, and to the Iranian people, who suffer most under both their regime and the bombs. Would I want it done to me? If my nation were at war, I would want its enemies to seek a settlement that left my children alive, and I would want my own government to do the same.

## The hard problems

Supporting the direction does not mean pretending the road is smooth. A Gulf-led arrangement faces at least five serious difficulties.

**The Gulf states are parties, not referees.** The GCC's secretary-general said this month that the Gulf states were not a party to the war and did not seek it. That is true of their intentions, but Iran has struck them, they host American bases, and, by the President's account, several of them prefer that the American blockade continue. Tehran will not view a Gulf force as a neutral monitor. Any arrangement needs inspection that Iran cannot dismiss as the work of its enemies. Here the International Atomic Energy Agency, or another body Iran has previously accepted, must have a role.

**The Gulf is not united.** Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Bahrain, and Egypt blockaded Qatar from 2017 to 2021. Oman has long kept its own channel to Tehran. A "Gulf-led force" could break apart over who leads it. American diplomacy should aim at a structure with clear command and shared costs, not a coalition in name only.

**Capability is uneven.** Monitoring a nuclear program and policing the Strait of Hormuz require surveillance, minesweeping, and naval power that some Gulf states lack. For years America will be the backbone behind the arrangement, even if it no longer leads it. The aim should be to transfer responsibility in stages, with each stage tied to demonstrated capacity.

**Iran's terms are steep.** Iranian officials have demanded an end to the war on every front, release of frozen funds, an end to the naval blockade, removal of sanctions, and compensation. Some of these can be traded in stages for verified steps; compensation cannot. The blockade should be lifted only in step with verified limits on enrichment and a reopened Strait.

**Israel.** The Vice President's statement after the election, that the Republican coalition wants to "change our relationship to Israel," has opened a debate that will outlast this war. Israel's objectives in Iran and America's are not identical. The Vice President said as much in June, when he described America's main objective as preventing an Iranian nuclear weapon. A regional arrangement must account for Israel's security without letting any ally, however close, decide when America goes to war. That question deserves its own paper, and I will give it one. Here I say only this: an alliance between nations is a friendship, and friends can differ without breaking faith. America's decisions about war must be made by Americans, through their own Constitution.

## Three voices in one administration

The SITREP describes three competing instincts within the administration: escalation, associated with Secretary Hegseth; sanctions, associated with Secretary Rubio; and diplomacy, associated with Vice President Vance. These are not three different goals. They are three tools, and a sound policy uses all three in order. Diplomacy is the main effort. Economic pressure, including a blockade if Congress authorizes it, gives diplomacy weight. Force is held in reserve against an actual attack or an imminent nuclear breakout. What the country has had instead is all three at once, with threats of renewed bombing announced on social media while envoys negotiate. That sends the enemy mixed signals and leaves our partners guessing. One reported Gulf official complained that Washington had not shared its objectives or plans with its allies. A peace the region can keep needs a single American voice.

## What I would do


 	- **Make the nuclear line explicit and verifiable.** No Iranian nuclear weapon, enforced by intrusive international inspection, is the one non-negotiable aim. Everything else is negotiable in stages.

 	- **Trade the blockade for the Strait and the inspectors.** Lift the blockade in phases, matched step for step with a reopened Hormuz and verified limits on enrichment.

 	- **Build the Gulf-led watch in stages.** America leads in the first phase, supports in the second, and stands behind in the third, with each transfer tied to demonstrated Gulf capacity and a clear command structure.

 	- **Keep Qatar's channel and Oman's, and open a direct one.** The Vance outreach, if real, is right. Wars end when the people who can end them speak to each other.

 	- **Go to Congress.** Any renewed offensive operation, and any continued blockade, should be authorized by Congress. A just policy can survive a vote. (I make the full constitutional case in my companion paper, "Who Decides on War?")


I hold this position open to a better argument. The strongest case against it is that pulling back now lets Iran rebuild before the threat is removed, so that the lives already spent are wasted. I take that seriously. My answer is that the only lasting way to remove the threat is verified limits backed by the nations that live next to it. A war that cannot end the threat, and only postpones it, is not a better guarantee.

## Sources and their limits

This paper relies on mainstream news reporting (AP, Reuters, Al Jazeera, Khaleej Times, Stars and Stripes, Israel Hayom, Fortune/The Conversation, Shafaq News) and on public statements by American, Qatari, and Iranian officials. Official statements by all three governments are the testimony of interested parties and are treated that way. I could not confirm independently two items reported in the SITREP: the President's post proposing a Gulf-led force and the Vance letter to Rezaei. The Turkish report of a scheduled meeting is unconfirmed even by the SITREP. Positions inside the administration (Hegseth for escalation, Rubio for sanctions, Vance for diplomacy) are the SITREP's characterization; they are consistent with public reporting but simplified. Figures for cost and casualties are published estimates and will change. Gulf and Iranian viewpoints reach me largely through English-language and Western-curated reporting, and that curation may hide what officials in those countries actually believe.
