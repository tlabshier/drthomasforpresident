# 261115 - Righteous Funding of Government

# How Shall a Righteous Nation Pay Its Bills?


### Funding the Common Defense Without Plunder

Thomas Lee Abshier, ND · 15 November 2026

## My Position

In my paper *Not Yours to Give,* I argued with Frédéric Bastiat that government may not take from one person to give to another, but it does have a God-given authority to tax for the common defense and for welfare that is truly general. This paper takes up the next question: **how should that rightful tax be collected?**

I stand on this: the tax that funds a righteous government should be small, visible, flat, and close to home. It should be small, because a government limited to defense and justice needs far less than the tenth God asks for Himself. It should be visible, because people restrain what they can see. It should be flat, because graduated rates are themselves a form of redistribution. And it should be close to home, because the government nearest the people is the one they can most easily correct.

On those principles, I propose four things. At the federal level, a low uniform revenue tariff plus a flat, capped national tax, ideally on consumption. At the state level, a sales tax. At the local level, a property tax that pays for local protection, with a man's home shielded from seizure. And, as the more radical option the Constitution itself allows, a return to apportionment, in which each state pays its population share of the federal budget and raises that share however its own people choose.

I test every policy with three questions: *Is it righteous?* If so, *is it loving?* And *would I want it done to me?*

## What the Constitution Allowed, and What the 16th Amendment Changed

A common belief holds that the Constitution limited Congress to tariffs, and that the income tax was forbidden until the 16th Amendment. The record is more interesting than that, and more useful.

Article I, Section 8 gives Congress power to lay and collect "Taxes, Duties, Imposts and Excises." That is broad: tariffs on imports, and excises on goods such as whiskey, came early. But the Constitution added a guard. Article I, Section 9 says that *no capitation, or other direct, tax shall be laid, unless in proportion to the census.* In other words, any direct tax had to be divided among the states by population. Massachusetts could not be made to pay more than its share of people, no matter how rich it was.

For most of the first century, Congress needed little beyond customs duties. To pay for the Civil War, it passed the first federal income tax in 1861. In *Springer v.* *United States* (1881), the Supreme Court approved that unapportioned tax, treating it as indirect. Then, in Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co. (1895), a sharply divided Court struck down the 1894 income tax. It held that a tax on income from property (interest, dividends, and rents) was a direct tax and had to be apportioned.

The 16th Amendment, certified in 1913, answered *Pollock*. It did not create a power the Constitution had forbidden. It removed the apportionment requirement for taxes on income "from whatever source derived." The founders' guard against taxing one state's people for the benefit of others was removed, and that is the real loss. Before 1913, a direct federal tax had to fall on every state in proportion to its people. After 1913, Congress could tax the country's productive regions without limit and spend the money anywhere.

## Was the 16th Amendment Really Ratified?

Many Americans have heard that the 16th Amendment was never properly ratified. The best-known form of the claim comes from William Benson's 1985 book *The Law That Never Was*. Its strongest version is this: the texts some states approved differed in wording from the text Congress sent them, several legislatures did not follow their own rules, and Kentucky's senate rejected the amendment even though Secretary of State Philander Knox counted Kentucky as having approved it.

The question under the question is a fair one. Can we trust that the most consequential tax power in our history was adopted honestly? I take that question seriously. But I grade evidence by its kind. The primary evidence is the record of the states' own notices and Secretary Knox's February 1913 certification. Every federal court that has considered Benson's claims has rejected them. The Seventh Circuit examined his book directly in *United States v.* *Thomas* (1986) and concluded that Benson had not discovered anything Knox had not already weighed. Courts have held that once the Secretary certifies ratification, the certification is conclusive. In 2007, a federal court went further and called Benson's selling of the argument a fraud.

The courts are part of the government whose power is in question, and I treat official rulings as testimony, not as the last word. Even so, the argument has failed in court for forty years, and taxpayers who rely on it are penalized for frivolous filings. My judgment is that the claim is unproven and legally dead. **I will not build any part of this platform on it, and I urge no one to stake his livelihood on it.** The honest road is the one the Constitution provides: if the income tax is wrong, repeal or replace it by amendment, openly, with the people's consent.

## How to Judge a Tax

A tax's purpose determines its limit. Scripture authorizes tribute to the magistrate *because* he restrains evil and protects the good: *"For this cause pay ye tribute also: for they are God's ministers, attending continually upon this very thing."* **(Romans 13:6)** So a righteous tax is one that pays for protection and nothing else, and is collected in a way that does not itself become an instrument of plunder.

I judge each kind of tax by five tests:


 	- **Size.** Can it be held small? Samuel warned Israel that a king would take *"the tenth of your seed, and of your vineyards"* and that *"ye shall be his servants"* **(1 Samuel 8:15, 17)**. If even a tenth to the king marked servitude, a government limited to defense and justice should need less than God's tithe, not more. Federal revenues today are about 17 percent of the national economy.

 	- **Visibility.** Can the people see what they pay? A hidden tax grows without consent.

 	- **Flatness.** Does every citizen pay at the same rate? Graduated rates and targeted credits turn the tax code into a redistribution tool.

 	- **Privacy.** Can it be collected without the government watching every paycheck, purchase, and bank account?

 	- **Proximity.** Does the government nearest the people collect it?

**A tithe to the government.** A flat 10 percent of income has the appeal of simplicity, and it is far below what we pay now. But it is still an income tax, with all the surveillance that requires, and it borrows the name of an offering that belongs to God. I would rather cap the government below the tithe than hand it God's portion.

**The income tax.** Graduated rates are redistribution built into the collection itself. Withholding hides the cost from the people who bear it, and the whole system requires the government to know every man's income. If an income tax is kept, it should be flat, with no deductions or credits (favors to the politically connected), paid by the taxpayer himself rather than withheld, and capped by constitutional amendment.

**A national sales tax.** A retail sales tax is visible at the register, requires no income reporting, and falls on spending rather than work and savings. Its weaknesses are real. It weighs most heavily on families who must spend everything they earn, and at high rates it invites evasion. The best-known proposal answers the first problem with a monthly rebate check to every household, but that rebate is itself a transfer. A low rate, with basic food left untaxed, is the honest answer.

**A value-added tax.** I rank this the worst option. It is collected at every stage of production and buried in the final price, so the citizen never sees it, and governments that adopt one find it easy to raise. A tax the people cannot see is a tax they cannot restrain.

**Tariffs.** A low, uniform tariff on all imports is a legitimate tax and was the early republic's main support. A protective tariff, set high to shelter favored industries, is what Bastiat called plunder. A third kind is a charge that equalizes the safety and environmental costs our own laws impose, which I treat in a separate paper. Congress has the power to set any tariff. The Supreme Court confirmed in February 2026 that a president cannot impose tariffs under emergency powers, because tariffs are taxes.

**The property tax.** Here I must be honest about a tension. The property tax makes ownership conditional: a man who cannot pay can lose his home, and in that sense he never fully owns it. That is a real wrong. Yet by the five tests, the property tax is the tax most closely tied to what it buys. It is local and visible, and it pays for the fire department, the sheriff, and the roads that protect the very property being taxed. The wrong lies in the seizure, not the levy. The Supreme Court began to correct it in *Tyler v.* *Hennepin County* (2023), ruling unanimously that a county may not sell a tax-delinquent home and keep the surplus above the debt. I would go further: no foreclosure on a family's primary home, and for the elderly, deferral of the tax until the home is sold or inherited. Then the tax becomes a lien that is settled honestly, not a sword hanging over the household.

**Apportionment.** The Constitution's original design was for Congress to bill each state for a direct tax in proportion to its population, and for each state to raise its share however its own people chose. Restoring this as the main federal revenue would put the size of the federal budget in front of every state legislature every year, and it would end the transfer of wealth between states through the federal tax code. It is the boldest option, and the most faithful to the founders.

## The Structure I Propose


 	- **Federal: a low uniform revenue tariff, plus one flat, capped national tax.** I prefer a retail sales tax with basic food exempt, because it requires no surveillance of income. If the nation chooses a flat income tax instead, it must have one rate, no deductions, no withholding, and a constitutional ceiling.

 	- **A constitutional cap on federal revenue.** The cap should be set well below the tithe and tied to the share of the economy, so Congress can't raise it by statute. The present payroll taxes would be wound down as Social Security is honored for those who paid in and replaced for younger workers by savings they own.

 	- **Federal spending limited to the four tests.** Funding defense, the courts, and genuinely national goods such as the interstate road network, as set out in *Not Yours to Give*.

 	- **State: a sales tax, set by each state's voters.**

 	- **Local: a property tax for local protection.** It pays for police, fire, roads, and schools as each community decides, with a homestead protection that forbids foreclosure on a family's primary home and defers the tax for the elderly.

 	- **The bolder option: apportionment.** Congress would bill each state its population share of the federal budget, leaving each state free to raise it as its people choose. I offer this as the most faithful option and the hardest to win, and I would welcome a national debate on it.


This structure passes my three questions. It is righteous, because it takes only what protection requires and takes it equally. It is loving, because it leaves each family free to give the rest from the heart. And I would want it done to me: I would rather pay a visible, flat share for a government that protects me than an invisible, graduated share for one that takes from me to favor others.

## Objections, and Why the Concern Remains Urgent

**"A sales tax is unfair to the poor."** It is heavier on the man who spends all he earns, and that is why basic food should be exempt and the rate kept low. But a flat tax on spending is not unjust. It asks each person to pay for protection in proportion to what he consumes. The poor are served best not by a graduated tax but by a small government, a growing economy, and neighbors who give freely.

**"Tariffs are paid by consumers."** They are, which is why a revenue tariff must be low and uniform. The same is true of every tax: someone pays. The question is whether the tax is visible and equal, and a uniform tariff is both.

**"Apportionment would burden poor states."** It would make each state pay its population's share of the national government, which is what the founders intended. A state that pays more per person than it receives is today subsidizing others through the federal tax code, and that is a transfer the four tests reject. The remedy for a poor state is growth and freedom, not a permanent claim on other states' earnings.

**"The income tax is settled law."** It is settled law. The people can change settled law through amendment, and that is the road I propose.

The concern is urgent because the present system hides its cost. Withholding takes the money before the worker sees it, deductions reward those with lobbyists, and borrowing pushes the bill onto children who cannot vote. Many Americans sense that something is unnatural about a tax code thousands of pages long, written to reward favored conduct and favored industries. That instinct is right. A tax system that people cannot understand is one they cannot govern.
