# Fishers of Men in Persia


### The Spiritual War Behind the Armistice

## Plain language summary

In my companion paper I argued that the war with Iran should have one clear endpoint: inspectors everywhere and the enriched uranium shipped out. That ends the shooting. It does not end the war. The men who rule Iran would still know how to build a bomb, and they would still believe that God has commanded them to bring the world under their rule. A truce with such men is an unstable balance. On their side is religious fire; on ours, mostly a wish for safety and quiet. Over decades, fire outlasts fatigue.

So the real war is spiritual. Bombs cannot kill a belief. Scattering a movement by force can spread it, the way breaking a tumor can seed it. The only lasting victory is a changed heart. I propose a campaign that starts where the Gospel can grow: among ordinary Iranians, many of whom have already drifted from the regime's religion, among Iranians abroad, and among the Persian-speaking peoples around Iran. Its message should compare the God of the Bible with the god of the regime and invite each man to judge for himself. And it must begin with repentance at home, because America's own abandonment of God is one of the reasons so many have turned to the strict discipline of Islam.

## An armistice is not a peace

The condition I proposed in "One Purse" is the right endpoint for the conventional war. It is not a surrender. When a regime opens its sites to inspectors, it gives up access to its hardware. It does not give up the knowledge in its scientists' heads, and it does not give up the will to use that knowledge. What follows is an armed watch: our cameras and their tunnels, our patience and their persistence.

Such a watch can hold for a time. North Korea shows that. But consider the two sides of the balance. The mullahs believe that their cause is the cause of God, that history ends in its victory, and that death in its service is a door to paradise. Most Americans who support the watch want only to be safe and to pay less for fuel. A conviction that a man will die for outlasts a preference he will vote for. The midterm elections have already shown how quickly the second can fade.

A complete surrender, and an actual peace, comes only when the men who rule Iran, and those who enforce their rule, give their hearts to Jesus as Lord and renounce the submission they now give to the god of domination. I do not expect that soon. But it is the only victory that does not have to be won again in every generation, and so it must be the true aim, with the armistice as a means of buying time for it.

## The Mahdi and the Messiah

To understand what holds the regime together, we have to understand what it hopes for.

Iran's official religion is Twelver Shia Islam. Twelvers believe that the twelfth Imam, Muhammad al-Mahdi, was born in the ninth century, was hidden by God, and still lives in hiding. At the end of the age, they believe, he will return and fill the earth with justice. The Islamic Republic's founding doctrine, *velayat-e faqih*, the guardianship of the jurist, holds that while the Imam is hidden, a senior cleric rules in his place. The Supreme Leader governs, in the regime's own understanding, as the deputy of the hidden Imam.

This is a remarkable mirror of the Gospel. We also believe that the world will know peace only when its rightful King returns and every knee bows to Him: *"That at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of things in heaven, and things in earth, and things under the earth"* **(Philippians 2:10)**. Both faiths hope for a world under one Lord. The question is which Lord.

And here is the striking thing. Jesus appears in their end-time story too. Both Sunni and Shia traditions hold that Jesus will return in the same age as the Mahdi and will pray behind him. In some Shia narrations, the returning Jesus breaks crosses, destroys churches, and kills Christians who will not believe in the Mahdi. In the Bible, Jesus returns as *"KING OF KINGS, AND LORD OF LORDS"* **(Revelation 19:16)**, and He prays behind no one.

So the two faiths do not merely name different gods. They tell two opposite stories about the same Man. In one, Jesus is a servant who follows another and comes to tear down His own Church. In the other, Jesus is God come in the flesh, who died for His enemies and rose again. Both stories cannot be true. That is the question to put before every Persian: when Jesus comes, who will kneel to whom?

I must add one distinction, because accuracy is a form of honesty. Not all Twelvers accept the regime's doctrine. The great seminary at Najaf in Iraq, under Grand Ayatollah Ali al-Sistani, has kept to an older tradition that does not give clerics the power to rule the state. Sistani has long been seen as a quiet rival to the Iranian model. The regime's theology of domination is one reading of Shia Islam, not the only one. That matters for strategy. It means the regime's claim to speak for every Shia believer is false, and many Shia already know it.

## Why force cannot kill an idea

The men at the center of the regime are not held there mainly by money or fear. They are held by belief, committed at the level of the will, where a man decides what he lives for. Allegiance at that level does not die by force. It can be killed with the man who holds it. It cannot be killed in him.

Scripture gives a hard lesson here. When Israel entered Canaan, God commanded the destruction of nations whose corruption He had judged. Israel repeatedly stopped short. After the war with Midian, the army spared the women and children, and Moses was angry, because those very women had led Israel into the worship of Baal at Peor **(Numbers 31:15–16)**. Saul spared King Agag and the best of the Amalekites' livestock, and lost his kingdom for it **(1 Samuel 15:9, 23)**. When the tribes left the Canaanites in the land, God told them those nations would be *"as thorns in your sides, and their gods shall be a snare unto you"* **(Judges 2:3)**.

I draw from this a lesson, and I want to be clear what it is. It is not that we should finish what Israel left undone. That command was given to one nation, at one moment, by God's direct word, and it is not given to us. Jesus told Peter to put away his sword **(Matthew 26:52)**. The lesson is the reverse: victory by force over an idea succeeds only if it is total, and total destruction of a people is neither possible for us nor permitted to us. Anything less leaves a remnant, and the remnant carries the idea.

Modern history teaches the same thing in its own way. When the so-called Islamic State lost its cities in Iraq and Syria, its ideology did not die. It dispersed into affiliates on other continents. Breaking an ideological center can weaken it, but unless it is converted or fully destroyed, its pieces can take root elsewhere, smaller and harder to find. A military campaign against the mullahs' belief would be such a campaign: costly, incomplete, and likely to seed what it set out to destroy.

The weapon that can reach the will is a different weapon. *"For the word of God is quick, and powerful, and sharper than any twoedged sword, piercing even to the dividing asunder of soul and spirit"* **(Hebrews 4:12)**. Paul calls it *"the sword of the Spirit"* **(Ephesians 6:17)**. It does not kill the man. It kills his old allegiance and gives him a new one.

## The periphery strategy

If the regime's center is a fortress, it would be folly to make its strongest wall the first point of attack. The men closest to the Supreme Leader live where any sign of doubt can mean prison or death. To evangelize them directly is to put both the messenger and the listener in mortal danger. A cult does not let the Word in through its front door, and a man inside it cannot easily carry the Word to others.

So I propose that we work from the outside in, in widening circles around the center.

**The people around the leaders.** The regime's religion is already losing its own people. A large online survey in 2020 by GAMAAN, a Netherlands-based research group, found that only about a third of Iranian respondents identified as Shia Muslims, though most still believed in God. Only about a quarter said they believed in the coming of the Mahdi, the very doctrine on which the regime rests its authority. Younger respondents reported more irreligion and more conversion to Christianity than their elders. Online surveys have limits, and other surveys find far higher religious identification. But the direction is plain. The soil around the fortress is not the regime's soil anymore. Many are spiritually hungry and know the state's religion has not fed them.

**Iranians abroad.** Millions of Iranians live outside Iran, in Turkey, Armenia, Georgia, Germany, the Netherlands, Britain, Canada, and America. Many fled the regime. They live free of its police, and they keep close ties with family at home. A converted Iranian in Istanbul or Utrecht can speak to his cousin in Shiraz with a credibility no American broadcaster can match. Much of the Persian church's growth has come this way.

**The Persian-speaking nations around Iran.** Persian, in its several forms, is spoken in Afghanistan and Tajikistan as well as Iran. Scripture, teaching, and testimony prepared for one can reach the others. These are less heavily defended nations, and what grows there will flow back across the borders.

**The wider world.** The regime's networks run through Iraq, the Gulf, Turkey, and beyond. Everywhere the regime's influence reaches, the Gospel can reach too.

Christ told Peter and Andrew, *"Follow me, and I will make you fishers of men"* **(Matthew 4:19; see Mark 1:17)**. That is the right picture. A fisherman does not cast where the fish are not. He casts where they gather to feed, and he offers food they want. The bait in this case is not a trick. It is the Bread of Life itself, offered with the hook of truth in it, to men already hungry for something the regime cannot give.

## The means of casting

In "Winning the Long War with Iran," I described the tools: Persian-language satellite television and radio, internet posts and secure apps, Scripture on cards and thumb drives small enough to hide, leaflets, and Bibles carried by high-altitude balloon. Much of this is already being done by Persian Christian ministries. Satellite broadcasters such as SAT-7 Pars, Mohabat TV, and the channels founded by Iranian pastors in America have broadcast into Iran for two decades. Estimates of the number of converts in Iran vary widely: some ministries count two million or more, while others estimate between half a million and 800,000. Iran is ranked among the worst persecutors of Christians in the world, and the faith is growing anyway.

The regime knows it. In July 2026 it made watching Persian-language Christian satellite broadcasts a crime for the first time. In June it demolished the Evangelical Church of Mashhad. A regime does not outlaw a television channel unless the channel is winning. These measures raise the risk to every viewer, and so they shape the means: content must be easy to receive privately, easy to hide, and never marked in a way that hands the interrogator a charge. That is why, as I argued before, the American government's role should be to build and defend the roads—satellite internet, anti-censorship tools, broadcasting capacity—while the message itself comes from Persian believers.

Person-to-person witness is always the best, and in Iran it is the most dangerous. The man who names Christ there may face fines, loss of work, ostracism, prison, or worse. Yet that is the ground into which the Word must go. The early Church grew in exactly such ground. The blood of the martyrs has always been seed, and the Persian church is proving it again.

## Comparing the two Gods

I believe the most promising message for this campaign is an honest comparison of the God revealed in the Bible with the god the regime serves. The two scriptures describe two different Gods, and the difference can be seen by anyone who looks.

I would build that comparison around the character of God, not around a contest of proof-texts. A long exchange of verse against verse can harden a listener into defending his side. But many Iranian converts describe being drawn first by the Person of Jesus: by dreams of Him, by the kindness of a Christian, by the peace He gave them in despair, and by disillusion with what the regime's religion had produced. The comparison should open that door. Here are the questions I would put:

**Does God seek you, or only judge you?** In the Bible, God goes looking for the lost sheep and runs to meet the prodigal son while he is still far off **(Luke 15:4–7, 20)**. He is a Father before He is a Judge.

**Can you know where you stand with God?** The regime's religion asks a man to weigh his deeds and hope. The Gospel says: *"These things have I written unto you that believe on the name of the Son of God; that ye may know that ye have eternal life"* **(1 John 5:13)**. Salvation is a gift received, not a wage earned.

**Does God command you to love your enemy, or to subdue him?** *"Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you"* **(Matthew 5:44)**. Compare that with the Medinan command to fight those who do not believe until they pay tribute and are brought low **(Qur'an 9:29)**, which is the verse the regime's ideology lives by.

**Did God come to die for you, or send others to die for Him?** The Qur'an denies that Jesus was crucified **(Qur'an 4:157)**. The Gospel rests on it: God Himself, in Christ, bearing the penalty for our sins. The regime sends young men across minefields and calls it martyrdom. Christ went to the cross Himself.

**Which Jesus will come?** The one who prays behind the Mahdi and breaks the cross, or the King of Kings before whom every knee will bow?

**What fruit does each faith bear?** *"Ye shall know them by their fruits"* **(Matthew 7:16)**. Look at the earth each faith builds, and ask which one you would choose to live in.

I have made this comparison myself, from a quick overview to a long study of the scriptures, theology, and cultures involved. I am convinced that a man who makes it honestly, free of fear and of a lifetime's indoctrination, will find the God of the Bible to be the true God. But I do not ask anyone to take my word for it. I ask every man to compare the two for himself, and I trust the truth to do its own work. A conversion compelled by argument alone would be as hollow as one compelled by the sword. The decision must be his.

## Repentance at home is the credential for the call

This campaign has a weakness, and the regime knows exactly where it is. When we call Iranians to the God of the Bible, they look at America and ask: is this what your God produces? They see our pornography, our broken families, our abortions, our confusion about what a man and a woman are, and our leaders' love of money. Many in the Muslim world have turned to the strict discipline of Islam precisely because it seems to stand against that excess. They see it as holiness, and they see the West as its opposite.

So the campaign must say two things plainly.

First, the libertine morality the West promotes is not Christianity. It is the abandonment of Christianity. A once-great Bible-believing nation has turned to worship other gods: pleasure, money, and self. That is no reason to reject the Bible. It is a warning of what happens to any nation that does. It did not go well for Israel when it turned to the Baals **(Judges 2:11–15; 2 Kings 17:7–18)**, and it will not go well for America. *"For my people have committed two evils; they have forsaken me the fountain of living waters, and hewed them out cisterns, broken cisterns, that can hold no water"* **(Jeremiah 2:13)**.

Second, forced holiness is not Godliness either. A morality enforced by religious police and the gallows may cover the body, but it cannot cleanse the heart. It produces hypocrisy at the top and fear below. It is as far from the Spirit of God as the libertine excess of the West. God does not want our hands scrubbed under threat; He wants our hearts given in love. If a man desires true holiness, let him live the Bible's commands for holiness, by the power of the Spirit, freely chosen. Let him not reject the Bible because America has.

Saying this does not make our call hypocritical. It makes it honest. We are not saying "be like us." We are saying "come to Him, as we must come back to Him." The same message goes to Tehran and to Washington: repent and return to the Lord. *"If my people, which are called by my name, shall humble themselves, and pray, and seek my face, and turn from their wicked ways; then will I hear from heaven, and will forgive their sin, and will heal their land"* **(2 Chronicles 7:14)**. So the campaign aims at two spirits at once: the spirit of domination in Iran and the spirit of indulgence in America. Both must give way to submission to the God of holiness.

## What I would do


 	- **Make the true aim explicit.** Every statement of our Iran policy should say that the armistice protects the world while the real work goes on: the freeing of hearts. A policy that names only safety will be abandoned when safety seems secure.

 	- **Work from the periphery inward.** Concentrate resources where the Gospel can take root now: ordinary Iranians already drifting from the regime's religion, Iranians abroad, Persian speakers in Afghanistan and Tajikistan, and the communities along the regime's networks.

 	- **Build the roads, and let Persian believers carry the message.** The government funds and defends the channels. Persian ministries and Iranian believers supply the content and decide what keeps converts safe.

 	- **Build the message around the character of God.** Compare the two Gods honestly, centered on the Person of Jesus, and invite each man to judge.

 	- **Speak the call to repentance at home in the same breath.** No call to Persia without a call to America. Our own revival is not a separate project. It is what makes our witness believable and our patience possible.

 	- **Honor the quiet Shia tradition.** Make plain that the regime's theology of rule is one reading of Shia Islam, rejected by many of the faith's own scholars, so that ordinary Shia believers do not hear our message as an attack on their families and history.


## The moral test

**Is it righteous?** Proclaiming the truth about God to a people held captive by a false religion is the most righteous act a nation can perform. It compels no one. It offers what every soul has a right to hear. Calling one's own nation to repentance at the same time is righteous too, because it refuses to judge others by a standard we do not keep.

**Is it loving?** Withholding the Gospel from a people dying without it would be the opposite of love. *"Thou shalt not hate thy brother in thine heart: thou shalt in any wise rebuke thy neighbour, and not suffer sin upon him"* **(Leviticus 19:17)**. Love tells the truth, offers it gently, and takes care not to endanger the one who receives it.

**Would I want it done to me?** If I were a young Iranian who had lost faith in the mullahs' god and did not know where to turn, I would want someone to tell me there is a God who seeks me, loves me, and offers me rest. I would want to hear it in my own language from my own countrymen, in a form I could keep hidden. And if I were an American, I would want a leader willing to tell me that my nation must repent too.

## Why the concern remains real

The danger this paper addresses is not hypothetical. The regime's devotion is real and long-lived. It has endured eight years of war with Iraq, decades of sanctions, and now nine months of American and Israeli strikes. This month Iran's Assembly of Experts called for a review of its defense doctrine, opening a door to reversing its stated ban on nuclear weapons. The regime has criminalized the very broadcasts that are drawing its people to Christ. A religion that answers a television channel with the law has already admitted where the real battle is.

And the balance of will is uneven. The regime's fervor is rooted in the conviction that God is on its side and that time is too. Ours rests, for most Americans, on a cost-benefit calculation that a single election season can overturn. Unless that changes, unless America recovers a faith worth enduring for, we will tire first. That is why the war cannot be won in the Gulf alone.

## The strongest objections

**That this is a crusade.** Some will say that an American president who calls Persians to Christ confirms the regime's charge that this is a religious war against Islam. My answer is that a crusade compels; this offers. No one will be bombed, taxed, or imprisoned for refusing the Gospel. The regime cannot say the same of its own religion.

**That it endangers converts.** It does, and that is why the content comes from Persian believers and why every means must protect the receiver. But the converts are already in danger, and they have asked the world not to be silent about them.

**That it insults Muslims.** Many devout Muslims live peaceably and read the hard verses of Medina in their historical setting. I respect their sincerity and distinguish them from the men who rule Iran. But I will not soften the claim at the center: Jesus is the Lord of every heart, and the fruit of the regime's religion is visible to anyone who looks.

**That changes of heart cannot be scheduled.** True. That is why the armistice must hold while this work proceeds. I do not rest our near-term safety on revival. I rest our long-term peace on it, because nothing else has ever produced one.

I hold this position open to a better argument.

## Sources and their limits

This paper draws on Scripture, on published descriptions of Twelver Shia doctrine, on survey research, and on reporting from Christian ministries and mainstream outlets. Estimates of the number of Christian converts in Iran come mostly from ministries that work there and have an interest in their work's success; independent estimates are lower, and no census is possible under persecution. The GAMAAN survey was conducted online, which may overrepresent younger, urban, and less religious Iranians; other surveys, including the World Values Survey, report far higher Muslim identification. Shia end-time traditions vary, and I have described the regime's reading and the Najaf tradition in general terms; Shia scholars would describe them with more nuance. Reports of the regime's 2026 crackdown on broadcasts and churches come from Christian advocacy organizations and the outlets that cite them. My view of the regime's inner beliefs is drawn largely from English-language and Western-curated sources, and that curation may hide what its leaders and people actually think.
