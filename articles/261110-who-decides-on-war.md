# 261110 – Who Decides on War

# Who Decides on War?


## The Constitution, the Hegseth Impeachment, and the Myth of the Small War

*Dr Thomas | A position paper · November 10, 2026*

**Resource:** Jack Posobiec, *Human Events* SITREP, November 10, 2026 (email newsletter), item 2, quoting attorney Andrew Branca.


---


## Plain language summary

The Constitution gives Congress the power to decide whether America goes to war, including small wars as well as big ones. The President also has the power to defend the country when it is attacked. Some commentators now argue that "war" means only total war, with a draft and rationing, so that anything smaller is the President's business alone. I think that argument is mistaken. The Founders wrote the opposite, the early Supreme Court held the opposite, and the 1973 War Powers Resolution was written to restrain presidents, not to license them. By the Founders' standard, the war with Iran needed Congress's approval. On Secretary Hegseth, I hold that "I was only carrying out the President's orders" has not been a defense in American law since 1804. The House should debate the articles and record its votes rather than table them. More important still, Congress should do the duty it has avoided all year and vote on the war itself.


---


## The question the impeachment raises

On Monday, Representative Thomas Massie of Kentucky filed eight articles of impeachment against Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth. Three concern the war with Iran: that it began and continued without congressional authorization, and that it went past the deadlines set by the War Powers Resolution. The others concern civilian casualties, the lethal strikes on suspected drug boats, the raid that seized Venezuela's head of state, and retaliation against a sitting senator for his speech. Massie is a Republican who lost his primary in May to a challenger the President endorsed. The incoming Democratic majority has signaled support, and the House must act on his privileged resolution within two legislative days.

The *Human Events* SITREP for November 10 presents the case against impeachment through attorney Andrew Branca. Branca is a careful lawyer, and his argument deserves a careful answer. It is the argument I want to take up here, because it reaches well beyond Secretary Hegseth. It is a claim about who in this Republic decides to kill and to send men to die.

## The argument I am answering

Stated as fairly as I can put it, Branca's case runs as follows. Congress alone can declare war. A declaration puts the nation on a war footing, with conscription, rationing, and the allocation of national resources. Short of that, presidents have repeatedly ordered military action without a declaration or a specific authorization, and that practice has continued since the War Powers Resolution of 1973. Hegseth has no war-making authority of his own; he carries out the commander in chief's directives. "High crimes and misdemeanors" is undefined, so impeachment depends on votes, and the articles amount to policy disagreement dressed as crime. Opposition to the President's military policy belongs at the ballot box.

Parts of this are true. Presidents have acted without Congress many times, and impeachment is as much a political judgment as a legal one. But the central distinction between "war" (total mobilization) and "the use of force on a smaller scale" (the President's domain) is not in the Constitution. Once that distinction is removed, the rest of the argument loses its support.

## What the Constitution actually says

Article I, Section 8 does not give Congress only the power to "declare War." In one clause it gives Congress the power to "declare War, grant Letters of Marque and Reprisal, and make Rules concerning Captures on Land and Water." Letters of marque and reprisal were licenses for limited hostilities: private ships authorized to seize enemy vessels, often with no general war at all. The Framers took the smallest kind of armed conflict known to them and placed it with Congress, in the same sentence as the largest. Whatever the clause means, it cannot mean that small-scale force belongs to the executive.

The same section gives Congress the power to raise and support armies (with no appropriation for that use running longer than two years), provide and maintain a navy, make rules for the armed forces, and call forth the militia. This text does not define war as conscription and rationing. Congress may do those things *because* it holds the war power. Branca's markers describe what total war feels like to a civilian in 1943. They do not describe the constitutional line.

The President's role is real, but it is defined. In *Federalist* No. 69, Hamilton explained that the President's authority as commander in chief would amount to "nothing more than the supreme command and direction of the military and naval forces." The British king, by contrast, could declare war and raise armies, and the Constitution gave those powers to the legislature. On August 17, 1787, the Convention changed Congress's power from "make war" to "declare war." Madison and Gerry gave the reason in the record: to leave the executive the power "to repel sudden attacks." The President conducts wars Congress has authorized, and he defends the nation when it is struck. He does not decide on his own to begin one.

Madison stated the principle in a letter to Jefferson in 1798, and I cannot improve on it: *"The constitution supposes, what the History of all Govts demonstrates, that the Ex. is the branch of power most interested in war, & most prone to it.** It has accordingly with studied care, vested the question of war in the Legisl."*

## What the early Republic did

We do not have to guess how the founding generation understood this, because they faced limited wars almost at once.

From 1798 to 1800 the United States fought an undeclared naval war with France, the Quasi-War. It was limited in every sense Branca describes: no draft, no rationing, no national mobilization. Congress authorized it anyway, statute by statute, specifying which ships could be taken and where. In *Bas v. **Tingy* (1800) the Supreme Court called this an "imperfect war," a limited war, and held that it was still war, authorized by Congress, under Congress's terms.

In *Little v. Barreme* (1804), the Court went further. Congress had authorized the seizure of American ships sailing *to* French ports. President Adams ordered the Navy to seize ships sailing to *or from* them. Captain George Little seized a ship coming from a French port, as his orders directed. Chief Justice Marshall held Captain Little personally liable for damages. The President's instruction could not authorize what Congress had not, and the officer who carried it out answered for it.

When Jefferson sent the Navy against the Barbary pirates, he told Congress he understood himself to be limited to defense without its authorization, and Congress passed an act in 1802 authorizing offensive operations. The founders' practice was consistent: even small wars went to Congress.

## Where "police action" came from, and what the 1973 law says

The idea that a President may conduct an undeclared war on his own authority is modern. Its birth certificate is Korea. In June 1950, President Truman committed American forces without asking Congress, and when a reporter asked whether this was a "police action," Truman agreed with the phrase. More than 36,000 Americans died in that "police action." Vietnam followed, under a congressional resolution (the Tonkin Gulf Resolution) that many members later said they never understood as a declaration of war.

Congress answered those two wars with the War Powers Resolution of 1973, which it passed over President Nixon's veto. It was written to restrain presidents, not to empower them. Section 2(c) states that the President's power to introduce forces into hostilities may be exercised only under (1) a declaration of war, (2) specific statutory authorization, or (3) a national emergency created by an attack on the United States, its territories, or its armed forces. It requires notice to Congress within 48 hours and the end of hostilities within 60 days (90 for a safe withdrawal) unless Congress authorizes more. Section 8(d) says expressly that nothing in the resolution grants the President any authority he would not otherwise have.

So to cite the War Powers Resolution as the beginning of a precedent for presidential war-making reverses its meaning. The resolution concedes the President's power to repel an attack, which the Framers also conceded. It reserves everything beyond that to Congress. Presidents have stretched it, but those stretches violate the law, not a reading of it.

## The strongest case on the other side

The best version of Branca's position is not the conscription-and-rationing test. It is the argument from long practice, and it deserves to be stated at full strength.

Since 1973, presidents of both parties have used force without authorization in Grenada, Panama, Kosovo, Libya, and Syria, among other places. In 1999, President Clinton continued the bombing of Yugoslavia past the 60-day deadline; members of Congress sued, and the courts dismissed the case. In 2011, President Obama kept American forces in the Libyan air war past the deadline, arguing that the operation did not amount to "hostilities." The executive branch's own lawyers have developed a test: an operation is "war in the constitutional sense," requiring Congress, only if its nature, scope, and duration amount to prolonged and substantial engagement that exposes American forces to significant risk. Congress, for its part, has funded these operations and has never once used the War Powers Resolution to end one. When Congress keeps paying for what it claims to forbid, the argument goes, the government's settled practice becomes part of the Constitution's meaning.

I take that argument seriously. Congress's cowardice is real, and it is bipartisan. But two things follow, and neither helps the defense of this war.

First, the executive's own test does not exempt the war in Iran. American and Israeli strikes began on February 28, 2026. The opening hours killed Iran's Supreme Leader and much of its military command. Iran answered with missiles and drones across the region and closed the Strait of Hormuz. The United States has blockaded Iran's ports since April 13 (resuming the blockade on July 14), and by early November the war had cost at least $38 billion and nineteen American lives. When the 60-day deadline arrived on May 1, the administration told Congress that hostilities had "terminated" because of a ceasefire, while the blockade continued. A naval blockade is the classic act of war; the Supreme Court said exactly that of Lincoln's blockade in the *Prize Cases* (1863). By nature, scope, and duration, this is war in the constitutional sense, under any test anyone has proposed except the one in the SITREP.

Second, the argument from practice proves only that the law has been broken without consequence. It does not prove the law was never there. A neighbor who has trespassed on my land for fifty years may have won the argument in fact, but he has not changed the deed. The remedy for a long run of unauthorized wars is not to declare them lawful. Congress must resume its duty.

## The moral center

The test I apply to any policy has three questions: Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? Would I want it done to me?

The constitutional allocation of the war power passes all three, and the presidential-war doctrine fails them. The question of war is the gravest a nation can decide. It means killing men made in God's image and sending our own sons to be killed. Scripture does not treat that decision as the property of one man's will. *"For by wise counsel thou shalt make thy war: and in multitude of counsellors there is safety"* **(Proverbs 24:6)**. Christ Himself took it as plain wisdom that a king sits down first and counts the cost: *"Or what king, going to make war against another king, sitteth not down first, and consulteth whether he be able with ten thousand to meet him that cometh against him with twenty thousand?"* **(Luke 14:31)**. The Constitution builds that counsel into law. It requires the people's representatives, who must answer to the families who will bury the dead, to share the decision and own it by name.

Would I want it done to me? If I were the father of a sailor enforcing that blockade, I would want to know that the war my son was fighting had been weighed and approved by the men I elected, not merely announced. That is not too much to ask of a citizen. It is the minimum the Constitution promises him.

My standing position on war, which I have stated before, is that the warrants for war are an actual attack or a real threat of attack, in defense. The President may act alone to repel the attack. Everything beyond the immediate defense should go to Congress. If a war is just, it can survive a debate and a vote. A President who believes in his war should want Congress on record behind it.

## What this means for Secretary Hegseth

The SITREP's defense of the Secretary rests on one sentence: he exercises no independent war-making authority and only carries out the President's directives. American law answered that defense in 1804. Captain Little also carried out the President's directive, and he paid damages because the directive exceeded what Congress had authorized. The principle has been reaffirmed in every generation since, down to the duty in military law to refuse a manifestly unlawful order. A cabinet officer is not a conduit. He took his own oath to the Constitution, and he answers for what he does under it.

Branca is right that "high crimes and misdemeanors" is not defined by statute. But it is not empty. Hamilton described impeachable offenses in *Federalist* No. 65 as those arising from "the abuse or violation of some public trust." Carrying on a war, which the Constitution reserves to Congress, if that is what happened, violates public trust in the most serious sense, and it is not a mere policy disagreement.

Nor is it unprecedented to impeach a cabinet secretary over how he carried out his department's policy. In 1876, the House impeached Secretary of War William Belknap for corruption. In 2024, a Republican House impeached Homeland Security Secretary Alejandro Mayorkas for his administration of border policy, and the Senate dismissed the articles. If Hegseth is impeached, he will be the third cabinet secretary in our history. Republicans who voted to impeach Mayorkas over border policy are poorly placed to say that impeaching a secretary over war policy is illegitimate in principle.

Here is where I part with *both* camps and take my stand.

To the Secretary's defenders: the "chain of command" argument fails. If the Iran war, the boat strikes, and the other conduct alleged are lawful, defend them on the law and the facts, article by article, as Massie has invited. Do not hide behind the President.

To the Secretary's accusers: impeaching the subordinate while refusing to vote on the war itself is its own kind of evasion. Since February, Congress has had every power it needed. It could have passed an authorization with limits, or refused one. It could have cut off funds. It did neither, because a recorded vote on a war carries political risk. Impeaching the man who carried out the policy, while never voting on the policy, lets members punish the executive for a decision they were unwilling to make themselves.

So my position is this. The House should not table these articles. It should debate them on the floor and record every member's vote, so that the people can see where each representative stands on whether a war fought without Congress's consent is lawful. And whatever happens to the articles, Congress should take up the question it has avoided for nine months: authorize the war in Iran on stated terms, or end it. That vote, more than any impeachment, would restore the Constitution's design. I remain open to a better argument. I have not yet heard one in the claim that a war becomes the President's alone when it is fought without a draft.

## Sources and their limits

The primary sources here are the constitutional text, *Federalist* Nos. 65 and 69, Madison's notes of the August 17, 1787 debate, Madison's 1798 letter to Jefferson, the War Powers Resolution (50 U.S.C. §§ 1541–1548), and the opinions in *Bas v.** Tingy*, *Little v. Barreme*, and the *Prize Cases*. These can be read directly and do not depend on any reporter's framing.

The facts of the current war and the impeachment rest on mainstream news reporting (AP, Reuters, NPR, ABC, CBS, NBC, Stars and Stripes, Axios, Newsweek, Task & Purpose, The Daily Wire, Fox News) and on the administration's own May 1 letter to Congress. I have treated the administration's claim that hostilities "terminated" as the testimony of an interested party, set against the continuing blockade. Casualty and cost figures come from published estimates and will change. Branca's argument is quoted as summarized by *Human Events*; I have not heard his full remarks, and he may have qualified them in ways the summary left out. The eight articles are reported secondhand. I have not seen the full text, and the merits of each specific charge, particularly the boat strikes and civilian casualties, deserve examination on their own evidence, which I have not done here.
