# 261121 - The Great Depression and the Conspiracies Around It

# The Crash, the Cabal, and the Love of Money


### What *The Greatest Story Never Told* gets right about 1929, what it gets wrong, and why the difference matters

**Thomas Lee Abshier, ND** | 21 November 2026


---


## Why I am writing about this book

My entrance into this topic was an article on News With Views, which contained the Truthlink to *[The GREATEST $TORY NEVER TOLD:](https://truthlink.info/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Greatest_story-never-told-with-cover.pdf) Winston Churchill and the Crash of 1929*, by an author who signs himself "Pat Riott," with a preface by "Phillip Dru III." Robert Motch, who wrote the article [What Every Daughter Should Know](https://newswithviews.com/what-every-daughter-should-know-3/), promotes it as one of the most informative books ever published, and he contrasts it with William Manchester's Churchill biography *The Last Lion*, which he calls disinformation.

I read all 148 pages. I think the book is worth reading, but not for the reason its promoters give. Its central accusation, that Winston Churchill came to America in the autumn of 1929 to engineer the crash as a financial war to bring the United States back under British rule, is contradicted by Churchill's own financial records. But the question beneath the accusation is real, and the honest answer is damning enough without the invented parts.

*"For the love of money is the root of all evil: which while some coveted after, they have erred from the faith, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows."* **(1 Timothy 6:10)**

The book opens with that verse, and I stand with it. The Great Depression was the fruit of the love of money and of the unaccountable power that money buys. Where I part from the book is on *how* that fruit grew. It was not one man's secret plot. It was something closer to the ordinary sin of a whole class of men, repeated across a decade, under institutions that let them do it, and then compounded by the failures of the very institution that was supposed to prevent a collapse.

## What the book claims

The book is built in five parts.


 	- **Cecil Rhodes and his secret society.** In his 1877 "Confession of Faith," Rhodes called for a secret society to extend British rule over the world and recover the United States for the Empire. His fortune, through Lord Rothschild and others, helped found the Rhodes Scholarships. The book treats Churchill as Rhodes's "man of action."

 	- **The Federal Reserve as a coup.** The Jekyll Island meeting of 1910, Paul Warburg's role, Colonel House's influence over Woodrow Wilson, the Federal Reserve Act, and the 1913 income tax. The book claims the Fed is privately owned, including by foreign-tied banks, and that Wilson was controlled through blackmail over his letters to Mary Peck.

 	- **Easy money.** The 1927 meeting of Benjamin Strong (New York Fed), Montagu Norman (Bank of England), Hjalmar Schacht (Reichsbank), and Charles Rist (Bank of France), after which the Fed cut its rate. The book says this cheap money was deliberately poured into Wall Street to inflate a bubble.

 	- **Churchill's 54 days.** The book's core is a day-by-day log of Churchill's trip, from his arrival at Puget Sound on 7 September 1929 (just after the Dow's peak of 3 September) to his departure from New York on 30 October, the day after Black Tuesday. Each day carries the Dow figure. The book's case is that Churchill, his brother Jack (a London stockbroker), Bernard Baruch, and the bankers they met coordinated the selling, profited from the fall, and celebrated at Baruch's mansion while ordinary Americans were ruined.

 	- **The warning.** The book was written about 1994. It predicted a mid-1990s collapse that would "dwarf" the 1930s, and it mocked the idea that the Dow could reach 5,000.


## The question under the question

When a story like this spreads, it is worth asking what it is reaching for. I think it is reaching for three things:


 	- **Was the Depression an accident of nature, or did men do it?**

 	- **Did the people with money and inside knowledge get out, or even profit, while ordinary people were left holding the losses?**

 	- **Is American money controlled by people the voters never chose and cannot remove?**


These are fair questions. The standard history often answers them in a passive voice: "the market fell," "banks failed," "credit contracted." The passive voice hides moral agents. The book is right to refuse it. My objection is that, having rightly asked *who*, the book then picks a culprit whose own records clear him of the specific charge, and it skips past the culprits whose guilt is on the public record.

## Grading the evidence

I sort the book's claims by the *kind* of evidence that bears on them, not by which outlet repeats them. I label a claim false only when primary evidence contradicts it.

### Contradicted by primary evidence

**That Churchill engineered the crash and profited from it.** David Lough, a retired private banker, wrote *No More Champagne: Churchill and His Money* (2015) from Churchill's own bank statements, broker records, and tax files, which the family opened to him. Churchill kept his statements and investment papers even though they recorded his debts and losses. Those papers show him trading heavily and badly in New York that October. In the single week of 8 to 12 October, he turned over about $200,000 in rapid trades, in and out of the same stock. He was positioned for a rising market; the fall caught him, and he lost a large share of his savings. Much of his writing in the 1930s was driven by the need to earn back those losses.

That is the opposite of what the book alleges. A man running a secret bear raid does not lose his savings. The book's own evidence even points the same way: it quotes Churchill writing to his wife from his railway car that the stock market news from his broker was "entirely satisfactory," which is what a man says when he is *long* in a rising market, not short before a planned collapse.

This also lessens the rival story in Manchester, that Baruch quietly made Churchill whole by mirroring his trades. In Manchester's telling, Churchill came to Baruch at the end of a bad day believing he was ruined, and Baruch revealed that he had secretly covered him. Churchill's records of heavy losses sit poorly with both stories, the one in which he was a profiteer and the one in which he was rescued. On this point Mr. Motch is half right: Manchester's anecdote should not be taken on trust either. But the correction does not run in the book's favor.

**That the Federal Reserve is "privately owned" and run for the profit of banks, including foreign ones.** The structure is stranger than most people know, but it is not what the book says. The Board of Governors in Washington is a federal agency. The twelve regional Reserve Banks are organized like corporations, and member banks must buy stock in them by law. But that stock is ownership in name only. It cannot be sold, traded, or pledged. It pays a dividend fixed by statute, and it carries no claim on profits. After expenses and that dividend, the Reserve Banks send their earnings to the Treasury, and that has meant nearly $97 billion in a single recent year. So the charge that the banks own and milk the Fed is false. But there is an honest residue, and I return to it below. Member banks elect six of the nine directors of each regional Reserve Bank. Bankers help choose the people who supervise bankers.

**That the mid-1990s would bring a collapse worse than the 1930s.** This was the book's own test, and it failed. The Dow passed 5,000 in 1995 and kept climbing for five more years. Scripture's test for a prophet is whether the thing he foretold comes to pass **(Deuteronomy 18:22)**.

### Supported by primary evidence

**Insiders profited while the public was ruined.** This is the strongest part of the book's indictment, and it is fully documented, not by a pseudonymous author but by the United States Senate. The Pecora investigation of 1932–34 put the bankers under oath. Albert Wiggin, chairman of Chase National Bank, was one of the men the book places at the 24 October bankers' meeting. Under oath, it came out that from September 1929 he had sold his own bank's stock short, more than 42,000 shares, and cleared roughly $4 million, while the bank he ran was spending its money to prop the price up. National City Bank had repackaged doubtful Latin American loans as bonds and sold them to small investors. Its chairman, Charles Mitchell, admitted to dumping poor securities on the public and to dodging his own income tax, and he resigned. The hearings also exposed stock pools that ran prices up and unloaded on latecomers, favored insiders who offered shares below market, and tax schemes for the rich.

That is a real betrayal by real men, and it is the record the book should have led with.

**Raskob's double game.** The book is right that John J. Raskob, the General Motors financier and Democratic chairman, told ordinary readers in the August 1929 *Ladies' Home Journal* that everybody ought to be rich by putting $15 a month into stocks. His biographer, David Farber, found that through much of 1928 and all of 1929 Raskob was short General Motors stock as a hedge against his own large holdings. One may argue hedging is prudence, not fraud. But a man who tells the public to buy while protecting himself against a fall has not loved his neighbor as himself.

**The secret meetings were real.** The Jekyll Island meeting of 1910 happened, was disguised as a duck hunt, and Warburg himself later wrote that the participants were pledged to secrecy. The 1927 central bankers' meeting also happened. Even then, Wall Street read it as an effort to relieve Britain's slump and steady France's currency. Afterward, the Fed eased. Here the book is following a serious line of argument (see below), not inventing one.

**Rhodes's ambition was real.** The "Confession of Faith" the book reprints is a genuine document. Rhodes did write of a secret society to extend British rule and to recover the United States. That it is real does not prove that anyone carried it out through the 1929 crash.

**Food was destroyed while children went hungry.** The book's dedication speaks of oranges burned with gasoline while children starved. This happened, though, in the New Deal years rather than in 1929, and under government planners rather than Churchill. Under the 1933 Agricultural Adjustment Act, the government bought and killed about six million young pigs and sows, and plowed under fields of planted crops to raise prices. It is a real moral scandal, and it belongs in a different chapter.

### Plausible but unproven: leads, not facts


 	- **Baruch sold out before the crash.** Baruch said so himself and was celebrated as the man who sold out before the crash. But that is his own testimony, and it is contested; *American Heritage* has called the story a fable and argued that he in fact lost heavily. Either way, it does not make him the general of a coordinated raid.

 	- **The 1912 election was split on purpose.** Morgan, along with partners George Perkins and Frank Munsey, bankrolled Roosevelt's Progressive campaign. That they also funded Taft to guarantee Wilson's win is asserted, not shown.

 	- **Wilson was blackmailed through the Peck letters.** The letters existed. The book asserts a $65,000 payment by Baruch without a document.

 	- **The 59 men who "rule the U.S.****"** The book's lists of interlocking directorates are real in their parts. Interlocking directorates show concentrated influence. They do not show a single directing will.


## The strongest version of the dissenting case

The best form of this book's argument is not in this book. It is in the Austrian school of economics (Ludwig von Mises, Murray Rothbard, and before them Benjamin Anderson of Chase), and it runs like this. Bankers created the Federal Reserve in secret. In 1925 Britain, with Churchill as Chancellor of the Exchequer, returned the pound to gold at its prewar rate, a rate too high for Britain's economy; Churchill later counted it among his worst decisions. Gold flowed from London to New York. To relieve Britain, Strong and Norman pressed for easier American money in 1927, and Rist and Schacht refused to join them, and Schacht in particular warned that credit had already expanded too far. The easy money of 1927–28 fed the speculation that blew up in 1929. Then, from 1930 to 1933, the Fed let a third of the nation's banks fail, and the money supply shrank by about a third.

On that last point, the mainstream and the dissenters now agree. Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz laid the blame for the depth of the Depression on the Fed, and in 2002 Ben Bernanke, then a Fed governor, told them on the Fed's behalf, "You're right. We did it. We're very sorry."

So the best case is real: a central bank, partly shaped by bankers and influenced by a foreign central bank's needs, helped inflate the bubble and then failed to stop the collapse. That is a scandal of policy and power. It does not need Churchill as a villain, and Churchill's own losses show he did not see it coming any better than the ordinary investor did.

## Why the concern remains plausible

I don't want to close by telling the reader to relax. The book's instinct that something is unnatural here is sound, for reasons I can state plainly.

**The money motive was real and enormous.** The men at the top had every incentive to sell to the public at the top and buy back at the bottom, and the Pecora record shows several of them doing exactly that, legally, because no law forbade it.

**The system rewarded it.** In 1929, insider trading, pools, and short selling against one's own shareholders were not crimes. The structure invited the behavior. Those laws, the Securities Acts of 1933 and 1934 and the Glass–Steagall separation of commercial and investment banking, came only after the damage. Glass–Steagall was repealed in 1999, and the 2008 crisis followed within a decade.

**The power was unaccountable.** A monetary decision that ruins millions should rest with people the voters can remove. In 1927 it rested with four central bankers meeting privately on Long Island. Today it rests with a more open board, but regional banks still have directors chosen partly by the banks they supervise.

**The comfort of the powerful was visible.** Whatever the date of Baruch's dinner, the scene of bankers dining with a famous statesman while men lost everything is not invented. The book's anger at it is the anger of the prophets: *"Woe to them that are at ease in Zion"* **(Amos 6:1)**.

So I stand with the book's moral conclusion: the love of money, joined to power that answers to no one, did great evil in 1929 and after. I reject its central particulars, because the evidence contradicts them.

## Why the difference matters

Someone might ask why I bother to correct a book whose heart is in the right place. There are three reasons.

**First, false witness is sin even against a bad man.** *"Thou shalt not bear false witness against thy neighbour"* **(Exodus 20:16)**. The book does more than accuse Churchill of greed. It makes him something like a murderer, a vulture hovering over men driven to suicide, and it does so on the strength of coincidences of date. Applied to this method, the test I use for every policy fails at once. Is it righteous to convict a man on a timeline? No. Is it loving? No. Would I want it done to me? No.

**Second, the wrong villain lets the right ones go.** Every page spent on Churchill's whisky flasks and railway car is a page not spent on Wiggin, Mitchell, the pools, and the Fed's failures of 1930–33, which are documented, sworn, and instructive. A reader who rightly rejects the Churchill story may wrongly throw out the true story with it.

**Third, some of the book's language has a history.** Calling a class of men "money vampires" and a "cabal," in a book whose cast leans heavily on Warburg, Schiff, Kahn, Baruch, and Rothschild, echoes an old pattern of blaming Jews as a people for the sins of some financiers. To be fair, the book names Morgan, Rockefeller, Mellon, and Churchill just as harshly, and I do not charge the author with that intent. But Christians should name individual men and their proven deeds, and refuse language that has been used to dehumanize a whole people.

*"He that is first in his own cause seemeth just; but his neighbour cometh and searcheth him"* **(Proverbs 18:17)**. A one-sided case always looks complete until someone examines it. Examining it here does not kill the book's concern. It purifies it.

## What I take from it

These are the lessons I draw for public life, offered as arguments and open to better ones:


 	- **Insiders should never be free to sell to the public what they are betting against.** That principle is now law. It should be enforced on members of Congress and senior officials, who still trade stocks while making policy that moves them.

 	- **The people should see what their central bank does.** A full audit of the Federal Reserve, and an end to bank-elected directors overseeing the banks that elect them, would answer much of the suspicion this book feeds on.

 	- **Deposit banking and speculation should be separated again.** The lesson of Pecora was learned once, forgotten in 1999, and paid for in 2008.

 	- **Money should be an honest measure.** *"A false balance is abomination to the LORD: but a just weight is his delight"* **(Proverbs 11:1)**. A currency that a few men can expand and contract by judgment, for the convenience of the powerful at home or abroad, is a scale someone can lean on.

 	- **The deepest remedy is not structural.** Laws can restrain the love of money, but they cannot cure it. A nation whose bankers, officials, and citizens fear God will build fewer bubbles and survive the ones it builds.

**Limits.** My sourcing leans on the documentary and mainstream record: Senate hearings, Federal Reserve publications, and biographies written with family cooperation. That record could hide things. The Churchill family chose to open Churchill's finances to Lough, and a family can choose what it opens. To my knowledge, Baruch's papers and his brothers' brokerage records have not been examined for the trades the book alleges. The Fed's own account of its structure is the testimony of an interested party, though the statute it rests on is public. The Pecora hearings looked only at what the committee chose to subpoena. On the other side, the book gives no footnotes for its daily narrative. Its author is anonymous, writing through the fictional grandson of a fictional character. It claims to rest on "thousands of pages of microfilm" but does not let the reader check one. I treat its specific claims as leads. Where primary records exist, I followed them; where they do not, I said so.
