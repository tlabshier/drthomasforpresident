# 261110 - 2026 Election Fraud Allegations Examined

# Verify, Don’t Trust:


### Examining the Fraud Claims of the 2026 Midterms


*Dr. Thomas — 10 November 2026*


A meme is going around. On the left is a map of Texas from 2024, almost solid red. On the right is a map labeled 2026, washed in purple and teal. Over the top: *“If anyone believes this you need your brain checked.”* Alongside it comes an essay by Amil Imani, “We the People Demand a Full & Complete Audit of this Election,” which asks whether 2026 was “a 2020 rigged election all over again.” He points to Texas, to Kansas and Mississippi, and to the familiar story of going to bed with a lead and waking up to a different country. In the conservative grapevine, I have also heard talk of a coming disclosure: that this election was a giant sting operation, and the truth is about to break.


I have no stake in any of these races. I do have a stake in whether Americans can trust the vote. If we cannot correct our government at the ballot box, the only correction left to the people is revolution. So I took these claims seriously enough to examine them.


## Where I Stand


I stand on the side of the citizen’s right to verify rather than trust. When mainstream media are silent about something, that does not prove it never happened. It only shows that mainstream, acknowledged evidence for it is lacking. I learned this in medicine. When a patient gets well, and the study shows no signal, the recovery gets filed under “placebo.” Evidence that does not fit the approved instruments gets a label instead of an examination. In politics, the label is “conspiracy theory.” A label is not an argument.


I also stand on this: I examined the specific claims made about this midterm, and they do not hold. The evidence against them does not rest on the media’s word. It comes mainly from two places: the people who lost, and paper ballots that Texas election officials are about to hand-count in public. Some uncertainty is real. Small-scale fraud at the margins can escape detection. Mississippi’s count is not final, and the Texas audits are not finished. I list these uncertainties below. They do not rescue the claims now circulating.


## The Question Under the Question


The real question here is not “Did Texas really flip?” It is this: *“The institutions that tell me my vote counted are the same ones that censored and misled me before. How can I know?”*


That is a fair question. Censorship in the COVID era was real. In 2024, Mark Zuckerberg told Congress that the federal government had pressed Meta to suppress COVID content, and that he regretted not pushing back. An institution that misleads you once loses its claim to automatic trust. So I will not ask you to trust the media about this election. I will ask a different question: who else knows, and what are they saying?


## A Rule for Weighing Evidence: Testimony Against Interest


In law, a statement that runs against the speaker’s own interest carries more weight than a self-serving one. When the winners say “there was no fraud,” that is interested testimony, and I discount it. When the losers say “we lost,” that is testimony against interest. It is the hardest kind of evidence to fake.


Consider who lost in Texas. For roughly three decades, Republicans have held the governor’s office, and they also hold the office of the Secretary of State, who is appointed by that governor. The Attorney General’s office, which prosecutes election fraud, has been Republican as well. So has much of the county machinery. These people had the motive, the lawyers, the subpoena power and the access to the records. Here is what they did:


- Governor Abbott conceded on election night, shortly after 10 p.m., telling his supporters he could not win mathematically.

- Lieutenant Governor Patrick called it “a wave election in Texas and across the country.”

- Senator Cornyn called James Talarico to congratulate him.

- President Trump said he would accept his party’s losses. He blamed Governor Abbott for turning “RINO” in his final months, not fraud.

- Conservative commentator Rogan O’Handley (“DC_Draino”) put it plainly: Republicans lost because they failed to fix what voters had sent them to fix.


To deceive these people, a conspiracy would have had to pass through machinery their own appointees ran. That is not impossible. But the claim needs evidence in proportion to its size, and so far none has been produced. Scripture sets the bar: *“at the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall the matter be established”* **(Deuteronomy 19:15)**.


## The Claims, One by One


I have graded each claim by the kind of evidence behind it, not by which outlet reported it. I call a claim false only where primary evidence contradicts it.


### 1. The map


**Verdict: False as presented.** Primary county results contradict it.


The 2026 map in the meme contains no red. Its darkest shading falls in the Panhandle, which is the most Republican region in the state. A map of the real 2026 county results looks nothing like that. Ken Paxton carried most of Texas’s land area. In Armstrong County, he won by more than 75 points, though fewer than a thousand votes were cast there. Talarico won where most Texans live. In Harris County, about 1.4 million votes were cast, and he carried it by 33 points. He also won the suburbs: 55% in Collin County, Paxton’s political home, plus wins in Fort Bend, Williamson and Brazoria, and a narrow flip of Kaufman. An honest 2026 county map is still mostly red by area, with the population centers in blue.


I could not trace the source or legend of the second map, so I cannot say what it actually shows. It may show turnout or some other measure on a different color scale. Whatever it is, it is not the 2026 Texas result, and comparing it with a red-and-blue winner map tells you nothing.


### 2. “We went to bed with a lead and woke up to a different country”


**Verdict: Did not happen in Texas. In Mississippi, a close count is under legitimate review.**


Texas reports its early votes first, soon after the polls close at 7 p.m. More than 7.4 million Texans voted early this year. The Republican statewide candidates trailed from the first returns, and Abbott conceded before most Texans went to bed. No Republican lead to lose overnight.


Mississippi is different, and it deserves close attention. At 3 a.m., with 95.6% of votes counted, Democrat Scott Colom led Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith by 43 votes. His lead has since grown to about 2,575 votes as affidavit ballots (Mississippi’s provisional ballots) and late-arriving mail ballots postmarked by Election Day have been counted. A voter who casts an affidavit ballot has five days to prove eligibility, for example by showing ID, and about three in four have been accepted. Hyde-Smith’s lawyers and the National Republican Senatorial Committee wrote to all 82 counties asking them to separate and preserve those ballots. Mississippi Secretary of State Michael Watson is a Republican.


That is what scrutiny done right looks like: preserve the ballots, examine them one by one, and litigate on evidence. I support it fully. The fact that late ballots lean one way is not, by itself, evidence of anything. Different kinds of ballots arrive at different times, and the people who use them lean differently. Specific ballots are evidence. A pattern in when they were counted is not.


### 3. “More votes cast than registered voters”


**Verdict: No documented 2026 instance found. Treat it as a lead, not a finding.**


I searched and found no specific county or precinct named in such a claim this year. Of all the claims, this one is the easiest to check. Every Texas county files an official canvass report showing the total number of voters, and registration counts are public. Texas has about 18.7 million registered voters. Some 1.2 million are on a “suspense” list because their address needs confirming, and they can still vote once they confirm it. Comparing the wrong pair of numbers, such as a stale registration snapshot against final turnout, can make an ordinary result look impossible. If you have a specific county and a specific number, name them. You can check it in an afternoon.


### 4. “Kansas and Mississippi defy intuition”


**Verdict: Surprising, but explained by evidence visible months in advance.**


The wave showed up in the data long before November. In the March primary, about 12% of registered Texas voters cast Democratic ballots, compared with about 7% in 2018. The national picture included an unpopular president, anger over the cost of living and tariffs, and an unpopular war. Texas had local factors too. Paxton carried heavy baggage: an impeachment and acquittal in 2023, and a $3.3 million settlement with former staffers. There was also a strong backlash against data centers.


Look at who won in the heartland. Adam Hamilton, the founding pastor of the largest United Methodist congregation in the country, became the first Democrat to win a Kansas Senate race since 1932, after a record $110 million campaign. James Talarico is a seminarian. Whatever one thinks of their theology (and I have my disagreements), these men won by speaking the language of faith to people of faith. That is a different story from “the heartland was overruled by a machine.”


## Tina Peters and the Problem of Selective Disclosure


This case matters to me because it tests my own principle. The account that circulated in conservative circles runs like this. Peters copied her county’s election server before state consultants came in to “fix” it. The copies showed the results had been altered. When she confronted this, she was prosecuted.


Here is the strongest version of her side’s case. The state’s May 2021 “trusted build” software update overwrote log files. Peters argued the law required those files to be kept for 25 months, and her experts’ “Mesa Reports” claimed that some 29,000 records were destroyed. The third report went further. It claimed that during the 2020 and 2021 counts, new databases were created and ballots were reprocessed. In Peters’s words, the records’ destruction prevented “any audit of the 2020 election.” These were serious claims, made in public.


Here is what happened to them.


- **They were not hidden.** Colorado Public Radio, Denver7, the Colorado Sun, and CBS Colorado covered them.

- **They were investigated by a Republican.** Mesa County District Attorney Dan Rubinstein, a Republican, investigated the third report. He found that the second database was created when an elections office employee started a new adjudication session mid-process. Dominion’s own instructions say not to do that. It was sloppy human handling, not outside manipulation. His office found no evidence of outside interference and concluded the report’s claims were false. One of the report’s authors declined to take part in the investigation.

- **On the deleted records:** Republican county clerks, including El Paso County’s Chuck Broerman, explained that a trusted build overwrites old files by design. The state keeps a copy of the old build, counties keep backups of each election, and the paper ballots are kept for 25 months, so any election can be recounted.

- **On the conviction:** A jury did not convict her for “confronting doctoring.” It convicted her of crimes centered on letting an outside consultant into the secure update under another man’s credentials. President Trump’s December 2025 “pardon” was symbolic, since a president cannot pardon a state conviction. Colorado’s Democratic governor, Jared Polis, commuted her sentence, calling nine years “extremely unusual and lengthy” for a first-time offender. She was released on June 1, 2026. On her release, Peters wrote, “I made mistakes, and for those I am sorry.”


Here, then, is my finding on selective disclosure in this case. The story was not suppressed. What was often left out, in the version passed along on social media, was the result of the examination. Lying by selective disclosure can come from either side. *“He that is first in his own cause seemeth just; but his neighbour cometh and searcheth him”* **(Proverbs 18:17)**.


Her side does have real points. The sentence was harsh, even by Democratic standards. Colorado also adopted rules that barred county clerks from hiring independent experts to examine their own equipment, which gave citizens who wanted outside verification good reason to be frustrated.


## Why the Concern Remains Plausible


I do not dismiss the people raising these questions, and neither should anyone else. Their unease has real roots.


- **The censorship was real.** Institutions that suppressed true information during COVID taught a generation not to take their word. That distrust was earned.

- **The machinery is opaque.** Proprietary software, adjudication screens, and restricted access put the counting out of the ordinary citizen’s sight. People reasonably distrust what they are forbidden to inspect.

- **Counts can be slow and decentralized.** In Mississippi, even the Secretary of State’s office could not say how many ballots remained to be counted. Uncertainty like that breeds suspicion, and the suspicion is not irrational.

- **Fraud does happen, and is sometimes caught late.** In 2018, North Carolina’s elections board threw out a congressional result and ordered a new election after an operative working for the Republican candidate ran an illegal absentee-ballot scheme. In a race decided by a few thousand votes, retail fraud at the margins could matter. That is exactly why Mississippi’s affidavit ballots deserve the scrutiny they are getting.

- **Paperless voting cannot be audited.** Texas law requires a hand-count audit after every election where paper ballots are counted by machine, but paperless machines are exempt. Without paper, there is nothing to recount.

- **The stakes and the money are enormous.** Control of Congress is a prize worth cheating for, and both parties know it. Both parties also cry fraud when they lose. In the March Texas primary, Jasmine Crockett said voters had been “disenfranchised.” The temptation to distort runs in every direction, so verification has to be independent of every party.


On the “sting operation” rumor: if such an operation existed, it would show itself in indictments and court filings, not in rumors. I find it unlikely. The president’s actual response was to accept the results and blame his own party’s governor. That is not how anyone behaves on the eve of a disclosure.


## What to Do Instead of Trusting


If you don’t trust the media, and I understand why, don’t trust the meme either. Go to the primary records.


- **Watch the Texas audits.** Texas counties must hand-count a sample of paper ballots and post the results on their county websites by November 24. A statewide risk-limiting audit, which compares randomly drawn paper ballots against the machine count, is scheduled for November 19–20. These are public procedures. If the hand count disagrees with the machine count, that is primary evidence no newsroom can bury.

- **Bring specifics.** Name the county, the precinct, the number, and the document. Firsthand reports are leads worth following, and they can be followed.

- **Serve.** Become a poll watcher or an election judge before 2028. The surest cure for distrust of the counting room is to stand in it.

- **Press for verifiable systems.** That means paper ballots everywhere, kept for the full retention period, audited by random hand counts, and open to credentialed observers from every party.

- **Do not pass along what you have not checked.** Election workers are our neighbors. Georgia poll workers Ruby Freeman and Shaye Moss won a defamation suit after video of them doing routine counting was used to accuse them of fraud. *“Thou shalt not bear false witness against thy neighbour”* **(Exodus 20:16)**.


## The Moral Test


I judge public conduct with three questions. Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? Would I want it done to me?


Would I want my county clerk, a neighbor who works long nights for modest pay, accused of stealing an election based on a map with no legend? No. Would I want my own honest concern dismissed as “conspiracy theory” without anyone looking at it? Also no. Both answers bind us. We owe every claim an examination, and once it is examined, we owe the truth our acceptance of the answer, whichever way it falls. *“A false balance is abomination to the LORD: but a just weight is his delight”* **(Proverbs 11:1)**. A just weight applies to the vote count, and it applies just as much to the accusation.


I hold this position open to a better argument. If the Texas hand counts disagree with the machines, or if the Mississippi ballots turn out as critics fear, I will say so here. Until then, I stand where the evidence stands.
