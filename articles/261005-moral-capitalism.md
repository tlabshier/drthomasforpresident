# Moral Capitalism and the Righteous Society

Oct 5, 2026 · @Thomas Abshier
## Plain language summary

Capitalism is the system that best gets people to work because a man feels his own family's needs far more than a stranger's. Communism asks for saints it does not have. But both communism and our government-managed capitalism share the same defect: they take giving out of the heart and do it by force, so no one's character is formed by it. The answer is moral capitalism: free exchange; a government limited to enforcing morality and defending the nation; the poor cared for by voluntary charity (the tithe); and a people whose hearts are changed by Christ. That vision, not a strategy, is the platform for 2028, under the motto "No King but King Jesus."

*Provenance: Claude drafted this essay in my voice from two recorded conversations on 5 October 2026 — a morning dialogue with Isak Gutierrez following the Christos Fellowship meeting of 4 October, and a later call with Joelle Ater. We edited the spoken material for coherence and arranged it by topic; we omitted side conversations. Factual corrections are gathered in the notes at the end.*
## The question the Fellowship left open

For two weeks the Christos Fellowship wrestled with capitalism, communism, and fairness. Charlie and Leonard argued one side, Michael the other, and the discussion was good. Charlie called it one of our best sessions. Yet I told the group that this essay was among the hardest I had tried to write, because I did not feel we had found the answer.

The conclusion we reached — that any system works only if the people running it are moral — is true. It is just not helpful. It does not say what the system is, where we stand in it now, or which parts work.

Isak named the problem the next morning. Capitalism is a free market, so anyone can play, and some play dirty. The rules matter, and so does who makes them. A government that takes a cut to look the other way is running a cartel. His instinct was right: the solution isn't to force a different system, but to fix the broken parts of the one that works. He admitted he could see what *could* be, but not what the solution actually looks like. What follows is our attempt to say it.
## Two questions: which system motivates, and which forms character

Two different arenas are at play here, and we have been mixing them. The first asks which system gets people out of the easy chair and into productive work. The second asks which system forms character — which one changes the heart.
### Motivation: the pin in my own hand

Communism requires saints. It needs a man who whistles in the coal mine because he can feel the warmth of a stranger's family as vividly as his own — Tiny Tim by the fire. A few people have that expanded sense. Jesus points to it, the Buddha's enlightenment points to it, and Conscious Point Physics suggests the connection between nervous systems is real. I believe it is. But for ordinary men it is abstract. I feel the pin in my own finger far more than the pain of the homeless man I walk past. That is not a defense of indifference; it is an honest report of what introspection shows.

Capitalism works with that reality, not against it. A man swings the axe because his wife and baby will be warm, he will get a paycheck, and he can go to the pub afterward. That is close to home, so it motivates. On widgets—on prosperity and production—capitalism wins, and I don't think the question is close.

The 1968 song "Easy to Be Hard" from the musical *Hair* asks whether people who care about strangers and social injustice care only about the bleeding crowd and not a needy friend. The lyric is muddled, but its sting is real: much professed care for distant causes is not felt care. It is a front. Isak tied this to the rich man giving publicly in the temple and the widow giving her two coins — all she had. There is the morality we display and the morality we practice alone.
### Character: what neither system nourishes

On the second question, communism might seem to win, since it asks only for giving. But its giving is compelled and abstract, so it produces no more transformed hearts than our tax system does. Here is the morning's central finding: **communism and big-government capitalism do the same thing. Both take away the experience of choosing to give.** What we actually want is a world where needs are met, *and* character is developed. Neither system, as practiced, does the second.
## Forced charity versus voluntary charity

### Charity is medicine for the soul

Isak said he believes in caring for everyone — that a sick man should be able to get care. I agree that this is a saintly goal. The question is who gives and how. Before Social Security and Medicare, county hospitals, churches, and charitable societies cared for the indigent. That voluntary system worked imperfectly but for a very long time. Then we decided to force people to give, and to have the state administer what was taken.

Forced giving is a robbery we have been taught to feel good about: we take your money at the point of a gun and give it to a worthy cause. What was actually taken was the chance to look inside and say, "I want to give to the widow and orphan." Giving by choice is a way of loving God, and it carries an eternal reward because it changes character. Compliance teaches only how not to get hurt. A heart of stone is replaced by a heart of flesh through the work of giving, and through prayer for the miracle of a changed heart—which, as Susan keeps reminding us, is the work of Christ.

A century of forced charity has produced a spiritually sick nation. Many people now say they care about the stranger, the oppressed, and the disadvantaged, but have never been required to give of their own substance. They march and signal instead.
### The proportions: provide for your family, tithe to the needy

Scripture gives two rules that set the proportions.* A man who will not provide for his own household has denied the faith* **(1 Timothy 5:8)**, and *he who will not work shall not eat* **(2 Thessalonians 3:10)**. *True religion is caring for widows and orphans* **(James 1:27)**. We are not told to work entirely for widows and orphans; the standard is the tithe, ten percent.

Our system has inverted this. In effect, it says: if you do not provide for someone else's family, you do not eat. So people pretend to work for strangers, and productivity falls. Giving ten percent freely is hard — every man wants to keep it all and then some — and that struggle is exactly where character is formed. If every working American actually gave ten percent to hospitals, recovery centers, orphanages, and shelters for battered women, the sum would be enormous. The wealth of the Latter-day Saints church shows what a people who actually tithe can build, though their bishop-reviewed system carries its own pressure.
### The poorhouse, rightly understood

What about the able-bodied man who is homeless, addicted, or mentally ill from his own choices? The biblical answer is stark but caring. The old poorhouses, run by churches and charitable societies, gave a crust of bread and thin soup — enough to live, not enough to stay comfortable. While there, the man heard the gospel, learned the principles of life, and was trained in a skill so he could return to productive work.

Our welfare system does the opposite. It pays more per child when no father is in the home, incentivizing fatherless children, uncommitted relationships, and dependency. It produced projects that became unlivable. That is a perversion of charity, not an expression of it. Isak added the practical point: even with a friend, you cannot always invite a homeless man into your house, because addiction can make someone a different person. Charity requires discernment, which institutions run by people who know the man can provide and a federal check cannot.
## Social Security, transfer payments, and counterfeit money

Social Security and Medicare are the two largest programs the federal government runs, and most working Americans pay more in payroll tax for them than in income tax. Social Security presents itself as a pension, but it is a transfer payment: today's workers pay for today's retirees. The first generation of recipients paid in little or nothing and received benefits; every generation since has carried that inheritance forward. I collect Social Security; Isak's generation pays for it.

The right arrangement is for the man responsible for his family to save for his own retirement and invest it in the economy. As the economy grows, his savings grow with it — more in, more out. The transfer system replaces that discipline with dependence.

The second distortion is the Federal Reserve. When the government cannot pay what it has promised, it creates money. Money created without corresponding production is, in principle, counterfeit: it represents goods that were never made. Sellers see more money chasing the same goods and raise prices, so the retiree's check buys less. Isak asked whether that is what inflated dollars are. Yes. Counterfeiting is wrong because there is no production behind the money, and the same defect holds when the government does it.

These are not failures of capitalism. They are failures of government — a hybrid of socialism and capitalism in which the state decides what each person needs and draws on what each person produces. Some of that claim is legitimate: a man has value, and the widow and orphan have a rightful claim on the tithe. But the able-bodied who will not work have no such claim.
## What capitalism is, and what the protesters actually hated

Capitalism is simple. A man risks capital — he borrows against his house, say — to make goods others will buy. If he succeeds, he repays the loan, earns a return, supports his family, and has enough to give to the widow and orphan. The system allows large payoffs to those who risk large losses. When people do it that way, it is good. The worker earns less than the owner because the owner took the risk of losing everything.

Our system is so regulated that it is hard to find the capitalism in it. Isak observed that big farms buy out small ones, big fish eat small fish, and government favors the giants. He asked whether centralization is capitalism's endgame. I think it is a perversion of capitalism. Regulation exists to stop people abusing the system, but when the regulator is bought, regulation becomes the tool by which the large crush the small.

I lived through the campus protests of the 1960s. What we hated, and called capitalism, was something else. The government took what capitalists produced, in taxes or in borrowing, and spent it bombing the people of Vietnam — in our name and without our consent. That was centralized authoritarian government, not men risking capital to make goods. We wanted good roads, secure borders, safe homes, and fires put out, delivered morally and at least cost. We did not want our production spent on wars we never authorized.
## The proper work of government

### Government is the moral policeman

The reason to have government at all, in the arena of justice, is to enforce morality. A man may be strongly motivated to rob a bank; gold fever is real. Government supplies the counterweight. Implicit in that is a requirement: the government must hold a correct standard of morality and apply it in proportion — not too much enforcement, not too little, not bought off by the mob, with judges who will not let the gangster walk. If no one chose wrong, we would not need this function.

The other arena — meeting the needs of the poor — belongs primarily to charity, not to the state, for the reasons above.
### The test for war

War is where government most directly spends its people's blood and treasure, so it needs the clearest test. Applying it:

- **Not just:** wars of expansion for land or living space (Hitler's claim to the Sudetenland as Lebensraum), wars to reclaim ancestral territory, and wars to keep a friendly regime in place so a fruit company can protect its profits. In the last case, sons and daughters die for someone else's bottom line.

- **Just:** defending the homeland. If an invader took San Antonio and the Alamo, I would send my sons to make a last stand, and that sacrifice builds character, because defending home is a form of providing for your family.

- **Possibly just:** stopping genocide or enslavement abroad. A people being wiped out for their land, their skin, their accent, or their faith are children of God. Protecting them is the widow-and-orphan principle applied among nations. This departs from strict non-intervention, so those called to it should fight— ideally a volunteer expeditionary force—under the libertarian test of whether a real aggressor is taking people and property.

Our current criteria are too loose: "they might be a threat" or "we do not like their leaders."
### Blood for oil, rare earths, and trade

"Blood for oil" has two sides. The pejorative side is real: we fight so oil companies pay less. But a nation hostile to us can also sit on a resource we need to survive—oil, or rare earth elements such as dysprosium that our magnets and industry depend on. When a regime chanting death to America controls such a resource, our survival is at stake.

Even then, the only lasting solution is not conquest. Taking another nation's resources is theft and piracy. The right relation is trade: we pay them fairly for what is in their ground, they prosper, and they buy what we make. No nation is an island. And the only durable foreign policy is missionary: changing hearts so former enemies become good neighbors who want us to do well, and whom we want to do well.

Isak called this loving your enemy — not refusing to fight, but actually resolving things. I added that it means training them to be good people too. If they hate us unrighteously, we must win them to love us righteously.
### Examining ourselves first

Isak then pressed the harder point. If other nations hate us partly *righteously*, we must look at ourselves. Much in America is corrupt. A young person abroad, seeing what we export, could reasonably conclude America is a terrible place. You cannot be a missionary while your own church is doing evil in the back room. Changing our hearts comes before changing theirs, if not equally, because it is the part within our control. He wondered whether that means an apology tour or simply righting our wrongs. I believe it means righting them.
## Christianity has not yet been done right

### A heritage we are spending down

America is not now a Christian nation in how we live. We are a mix of authoritarian government and capitalism with a New Age, multicultural, sin-friendly judgment, resting on a Christian heritage, a Christian legal system, and a Christian sense of right and wrong. Every relationship is skewed from what it should be: neighbor to neighbor, parent to child, spouse to spouse, worker to employer, employer and worker to government, and government to foreign government.

We were once overtly Christian. In August 1941, aboard a battleship at the Atlantic Charter meeting, Roosevelt and Churchill sang "Onward, Christian Soldiers" with the assembled sailors, expecting God to bless a righteous mission. Years ago I asked a man whether he was a Christian. He thought a moment and said, "Well, I live in America, so I guess I'm a Christian." That story is recorded nowhere, but it says who we were. Divorce was treated as nearly unforgivable; Roosevelt's advisers warned him that divorcing Eleanor would end his political career. Laws restrained adultery, fornication, and homosexual conduct.

The legal record mostly agrees — *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States* (1892) called this a Christian nation — until *Everson v. Board of Education* (1947), from which the modern separation doctrine grew. But my own memory is evidence too. We were spending spiritual capital the founders paid for, and we have spent much of it.
### Sexuality is the dividing line

On most questions, Christian and secular citizens agree. Both oppose bribing politicians; both oppose robbing your neighbor. Sexuality is what divides a Christian culture from a secular one. Today we try to make ourselves better by honoring what God calls sin and by giving other gods an equal voice in our moral system. We call it diversity, equity, and inclusion. That is self-destruction, and it makes us more worthy of the world's hatred, not less.

*Les Misérables* illustrates the drift. It rightly shows law applied without mercy. But its implied lesson—that theft is acceptable if you truly need bread—skips the real question: why wasn't there enough Christian charity for a hungry man to go to his church or his friends? Little by little, the culture taught that moral standards are gray: a little theft, a little adultery, a little dishonoring of parents, each excused by its situation. I believe that campaign has been Satan's.
### Not yet done right

Isak raised the honest objection. Look at what Christianity has produced — megachurch Christianity, abuse scandals, hypocrisy. Perhaps the system itself is flawed. Michael Sherman holds that view: he is open to everything, but he knows Christianity is wrong.

The old excuse for communism is that it has not been done right yet. For communism, that is an evasion. For Christianity, it is simply the fact. It is a correct system that we have not yet lived. What we point to as Christianity's failures are Christianity done badly — slogans without the substance.

Isak put the synthesis well. Each ideology has merit in its *spirit*. If socialism means "take care of each other," God is in it; its failure is centralization, where a spirit of power and a spirit of laziness take over. If capitalism means only taking and enriching yourself, it is a self-serving game; joined to the spirit of giving, it is beautiful. A religion that will not care for widows and orphans is worthless **(James 1:26–27)**. So the work is to look deeper at who we are, our connection to God, and our responsibility to people: love God, love people. When I asked whether this resolved his doubts, he said not entirely, but his understanding had widened. The question is no longer which system is right, but how to make it actually work.
## The elephant on the table: the administrative state

This is the largest unsolved question, and I do not yet have an answer. I note it here so the work is not mistaken for finished.

Washington's cabinet had four members: State, Treasury, War, and the Attorney General. Lincoln's was not much larger. The government did what the Constitution enumerated, and the Tenth Amendment reserved everything else to the states and the people. Federal revenue came mainly from tariffs and was spent on a narrow scope: foreign relations, defense, and the Treasury. Even the Barbary Wars were defensive in purpose — protecting American commerce from state-sponsored piracy on international waters.

Today there are fifteen executive departments — Energy, Commerce, Homeland Security, Education, Health and Human Services, and more — plus dozens of independent agencies. Each is effectively its own government. The President signs, speaks, and makes some decisions, but the departments do the actual governing, and their reach is funded by taxation and money creation.

Open questions for the next round of work:

- Which departments correspond to a constitutional, moral function, and which do not?

- For those that do not, what is the transition — returning functions to the states, to charity, to the market, or ending them?

- How do we unwind dependence (Social Security, Medicare) without abandoning people who were promised benefits and planned their lives around them?

- What does the President actually command, and what can he change without Congress?

## What this means for 2028

### A revival, not a strategy

What Isak and I worked out that morning: I hadn't heard from anyone, and I hadn't known it myself before saying it. I have been integrating this for forty years. It must be organized into a program, an agenda, legislation, and a paradigm—and presented as a serious campaign. No sneers, no apologies. I acknowledge plainly that the odds of winning are very small. Having said that, once, we go all in.

This is not a managerial campaign. It is a campaign of truth. If the vision is articulated completely and clearly, it can spread on its own merits, and strategy matters little. In that sense it is a revival. Historians have credited the First Great Awakening, with preachers like George Whitefield, with preparing the colonies for independence. A changed heart produces the tithe, the provided-for family, the cared-for elder, and the just war. Moral capitalism is a fruit of revival, not a substitute for it.
### The motto: No King but King Jesus

We weighed several names. "Make America Godly Again" was my 2024 slogan, but it echoes "Make America Great Again" — a slogan Reagan used in 1980 — and Isak pointed out it reads as a parody of a movement many voters distrust. It also implies we were once godly enough to return to. "A New World Vision" sounds globalist. "The Righteous Society" and "the Christian Nation" are accurate but abstract. "Christ in Every Heart" is the true end goal, but it sounds like a church service rather than a campaign.

We settled, for now, on **"No King but King Jesus,"** remembered as a Revolutionary-era cry. Isak noted it answers the recent "No Kings" protests: if there is no king, each man becomes his own highest power, which removes what actually unites us — a higher authority to whom we are all accountable. It is also humble. No candidate can declare his own humility without pride; this motto puts the President in his place. We elect something like a king, but what we actually want is no king but Jesus. I remain open to a better phrase.
### How we will run

- **Republican, as a write-in.** I compare every party against the Republican platform as written — not any candidate — and against the communist, socialist, Democratic, and Green alternatives.

- **No money.** No donations, no endorsements, no deals, no FEC filings. Anyone may promote the campaign, but it spreads only if people believe it. My 2003 Senate run taught me that a single unsolicited $100 contribution brought years of federal paperwork.

- **Self-funded media.** Revenue from Ideomotion may eventually fund short-form media. Joelle pointed out that reels and clips can now reach people for very little.

- **Ideas, not imprinting.** Modern campaigns win by conditioning reflexes with money. An ideas campaign is the opposite, and it should answer every question before any debate.

The practical tools are already underway: a categorized corpus with layered front matter (broad field, topic, keywords) so an AI can answer, "How would the Righteous Society handle this news?" and high-fidelity recording with OBS so these conversations can be clipped.
### Joelle's questions

Joelle asked the questions the public will ask, and they belong in the plan.

- **Scrutiny.** Everything I have written — millions of words on the president site, some dating from the 2003 Senate run — will be read for contradictions. Old versions remain in WordPress history; the public pages should state only what I believe now, with an invitation to correct anything I have gotten wrong. My business relationship with Charlie must be clean, documented, and properly taxed.

- **Church and state.** The biggest objection to the motto will be that I would impose my faith. The answer must be worked out carefully: advocacy with reasons, open to a better argument, never compulsion—the same principle that rejects forced charity.

- **Controversy.** She recalled Candace Owens's 2024 dispute over "Christ is King." We expect this motto to draw hate mail. My only concern is being misunderstood—never appearing to hold a position I do not hold.

- **The heart.** Joelle and I agreed the underlying question is how a heart is actually changed. My own came through an experience I could not reproduce for anyone and would not recommend. Susan's answer — Christ transforming the heart — is the general one. How that happens for a nation is still the question.

### Why I run

In 1986, in a personal-development seminar, I was told to propose a project that would change the world. Each time I proposed one, the leader said I was not thinking big enough, until I said I would run for President. It was half to end the exercise, but it took hold of my heart. I ran in Oregon in 1988, for the Senate in 2003, and as a witness candidate in 2024. My first real political education came in 1992, when I opposed my profession's endorsement against Oregon's Ballot Measure 9 and went from rising leader to pariah. I learned there that I would not be politically correct. This time, for the first time, I believe the platform is solid enough to deserve a public campaign.
## Editorial notes and corrections

These notes correct or qualify points from the conversations. Figures are from general knowledge, not checked against sources in this draft; verify before publishing.

- **Money creation.** In the conversation, the Fed's money creation the scale is trillions: M2 money supply grew by roughly $6 trillion in 2020–2021.

- **Social Security.** The first monthly benefits were paid in 1940; early retirees such as Ida May Fuller paid in a few dollars and collected thousands. The independent Social Security Administration runs Social Security.

- **Cabinets.** Washington's cabinet was State (Jefferson), Treasury (Hamilton), War (Knox), and Attorney General (Randolph). In Lincoln's, Seward was Secretary of State and Stanton Secretary of War; the Interior Department existed from 1849.

- **National parks.** Yellowstone became the first national park in 1872, under Grant. Theodore Roosevelt greatly expanded federal lands; Wilson created the National Park Service in 1916.

- **Jefferson and the Quran.** Jefferson owned a Quran (Sale's translation, bought in 1765). The documented episode is the 1786 meeting in London where Jefferson and Adams were told by Tripoli's envoy that the Quran justified attacks on Christian ships.

- **"Make America Great Again."** Isak confirmed in the conversation that Reagan's 1980 campaign used "Let's Make America Great Again."

- **"No King but King Jesus."** Widely repeated as a Revolutionary-era rallying cry, especially among Presbyterian patriots. Primary-source documentation is thin; describe it as "remembered as" rather than as a documented slogan.

- **Candace Owens.** Her 2024 controversy concerned the phrase "Christ is King," not "No King but King Jesus." Opponents read it as a jab at Jewish critics in the context of her dispute with the Daily Wire. Expect the same reading of our motto and answer it in advance.

- **FDR and divorce.** The episode with Lucy Mercer was in 1918, long before the presidency. The warning that divorce would end his political career is well attested.

- **"Onward, Christian Soldiers."** Roosevelt and Churchill sang it at a Sunday service aboard HMS *Prince of Wales*, August 1941, during the Atlantic Charter meeting.

- **Holy Trinity.** The 1892 case is *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States*. The congressional report referred to as "1864" is likely the 1853–54 House and Senate Judiciary Committee reports on congressional chaplains.

- **"Easy to Be Hard."** From *Hair* (1967 off-Broadway, 1968 Broadway); Three Dog Night's hit was in 1969.

- **Omitted.** The "Measure 9" referenced is Oregon's 1992 Ballot Measure 9, sponsored by the Oregon Citizens Alliance; it failed.
