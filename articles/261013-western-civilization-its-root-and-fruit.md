# 261013 - Western Civilization - its root and fruit

# Western Civilization Is a Fruit: Look to the Root


*Dr. Thomas — Position Paper, October 2026*


Bret Stephens wrote a column this week in *The New York Times* asking whether the Trump administration understands the civilization it says it is defending. The occasion was Secretary of State Marco Rubio’s speech at the Acropolis praising the West. Stephens set beside that speech several reports from the same week: that the Defense Secretary announced the Pentagon would livestream the execution of the Fort Hood killer; that the President chose to buy diesel fuel directly from Russia; and that the U.S. ambassador to Greece is under inspector-general investigation for accepting gifts in exchange for favors. His question was fair: is this what we mean by Western civilization?


I think he asked the right question and gave a good but incomplete answer. I want to take his answer seriously, show where I believe it stops short, and offer a test that I would apply to this administration, to its critics, and to myself.


## Stephens’s three pillars


Stephens says the West stands on three pillars.


The first is the quest for truth: honest self-examination, courage to question every prevailing belief, and the humility to admit we never fully possess the truth. He joins the Psalmist —* “Behold, thou desirest truth in the inward parts”* **(Psalm 51:6)** — to Socrates on the examined life.


The second is the worth and dignity of every human being, made in the image of God. From this, he notes, come the prohibitions on child sacrifice and murder, the equality of rich and poor, the Declaration of Independence, the Reconstruction Amendments, and Martin Luther King’s dream.


The third is the rule of law: law above the privileged’s convenience and the powerful’s whims. He cites Aristotle’s definition of law as reason without desire.


I agree with all three. They are real, precious, and a nation that abandons them will not remain free for long. But a pillar holds up a roof; it does not hold itself up. Something must stand under it. Stephens names the pillars and is silent about the ground.


## The ground under the pillars


Each of the three pillars rests on a prior conviction, and in each case that conviction is the same: that there is a righteous God who judges, and that every man, including every ruler, stands under His judgment.


**Truth.** Why should a man seek truth when a convenient lie serves him better? Socrates gave his life for the examined life, but he could not give the multitude a reason to follow him; Athens executed him by majority vote. The verse Stephens quotes answers the question Socrates could not: *Thou* desirest truth in the inward parts. Truth matters because Someone who sees the inward parts requires it. Where no such Judge is believed in, truth becomes a tool, used when useful and set aside when not. That is exactly the condition Stephens describes and deplores.


**Human dignity.** Stephens quotes Genesis 1:26 — man made **“in our image, after our likeness”** — and calls it an immense idea. It is more than an idea. If it is only an idea, it is as strong as the people who happen to hold it, and history shows how quickly they can stop holding it. If it is a fact about how God made man, then it binds us whether we like it or not, and it binds the strong in favor of the weak. That is why we must apply the dignity of the image of God without exception. If it covers the rich and the poor, the clever and the dull, it covers the unborn child and the condemned criminal as well. Stephens rightly names child sacrifice as something this pillar forbids; I would ask every reader, of either party, to follow that principle all the way to where it leads.


**Rule of law.** Aristotle’s definition — law as reason free of desire — is good, but reason itself can be bent by desire, and every tyrant has had lawyers. Law stands above the powerful only if a law stands above the lawmakers. The American founders knew this. The Declaration appeals to “the Laws of Nature and of Nature’s God,” and to unalienable rights given by a Creator, not by Congress. The same conviction underlies the jury’s ancient power to refuse to convict a man under an unjust statute — jury nullification — which I regard as a cornerstone of a free people. Twelve citizens may say to the state, “This law is not law, because it is not righteous.” That power makes no sense unless righteousness is real, above the state, and something ordinary people can recognize.


So the root is righteousness.* “Justice and judgment are the habitation of thy throne”* **(Psalm 89:14)**. *“Righteousness exalteth a nation: but sin is a reproach to any people”* **(Proverbs 14:34)**. Truth, dignity, and law are the fruits that grow from that root. Cut the root, and the fruit may hang on the branch for a season, but it will not be renewed.


## A test in the right order


Jesus was asked which commandment is greatest. He answered with two, and He gave them in order: love God with all your heart, soul, and mind; and the second is like it, love your neighbor as yourself **(Matthew 22:37–39)**. The order matters. Love of neighbor not governed by love of God drifts into whatever the crowd, or the ruler, currently finds pleasing.


From that ordering I take a two-step test for public acts:


- **Is it righteous?** Does it conform to what God has established and will judge?

- **If so, is it loving?** Would my neighbor, receiving this act in the public square, have reason to know he was loved?

- **Would I want it done to me?** I can feel the pain of what my neighbor would feel in my own body, mind, and heart. Do I want to experience that because it is righteous?


And the standard for “loving” is not my feelings or his, but the way Christ loved — truthfully, sacrificially, without flattery and without cruelty.


Let me apply this test to the three reports Stephens raised. I take them as he reported them; if any turns out to be inaccurate, the principle still stands, and the conclusion changes accordingly.


**A livestreamed execution.** Is capital punishment for a mass murderer righteous? I believe Scripture permits the civil authority to take the life of a murderer **(Genesis 9:6; Romans 13:4)**. So the first question may be answered yes. But the second question is where the act fails. Even when the law of Moses required the punishment of a guilty man, it limited that punishment *“lest… thy brother should seem vile unto thee”* **(Deuteronomy 25:3)**. Justice was never to become degradation. And God Himself says, *“I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked”* **(Ezekiel 33:11)**. To broadcast an execution for an audience turns the solemn act of public justice into a spectacle. It teaches citizens to take pleasure where God takes none. A just sentence can be carried out soberly. This one, as announced, is not.


**Buying Russian diesel.** Can a nation buy fuel from another nation? Of course. But it is not righteous to knowingly finance a war of aggression against a neighbor, and a man who supplies the money for a wrong shares in it. If the purchases are as large as reported, the act fails the first question, and we need not reach the second. This is one reason my platform begins with abundant domestic energy, including thorium molten-salt reactors: a nation that can power itself is not forced to choose between its fuel supply and its conscience.


**Gifts for favors.** This one is the plainest. *“Thou shalt take no gift: for the gift blindeth the wise, and perverteth the words of the righteous”* **(Exodus 23:8)**. If the allegation is proven, it fails the first question outright. If it is not proven, the ambassador deserves the presumption of innocence that the rule of law itself requires. Either way, the remedy is the same: an honest investigation, followed through without regard to party.


## The same test for the critics


Stephens grew up in Mexico City and writes that he knew the rule of law there was a sham, and that America now reminds him of the Mexico of his youth. That is a serious charge, and he has earned the right to make it from experience. But the remedy for a sham is truth, and truth includes proportion. When a writer piles a dozen grievances into one breathless sentence — some grave, some trivial, some characterized rather than demonstrated — he invites his reader to feel rather than to weigh. Exaggeration is a kind of lying by proportion, and it weakens the case against real wrongdoing by mixing it with lesser complaints. The first pillar he names, honest self-scrutiny, applies to the columnist too.


It applies to me as well. I do not offer this test because I have always passed it. I offer it because it is the right test, and because a nation that agreed on the test could argue honestly about how to apply it.


## What I propose


A government that wants to speak of Western civilization should first practice its root. In concrete terms:


- **Hold every official to the same law.** Investigations of gifts and favors should proceed on evidence, in the open, regardless of party, and the result should be published.

- **Carry out justice soberly.** Executions, where lawful, should be conducted with gravity and without spectacle. No broadcast, no audience for entertainment.

- **Stop financing aggression.** No federal purchases of energy that fund a war of conquest, and an energy policy that makes such purchases unnecessary.

- **Restore the citizen’s conscience to the courtroom.** Juries should be told the truth about their power to judge both the law and the facts.

- **Teach the root, not only the fruit.** Our schools can teach the pillars of the West honestly, including the convictions that gave rise to them, without compelling anyone to believe them.


Mr. Rubio is right that Western civilization is worth defending. Mr. Stephens is right that defending it requires understanding it. I would add one thing to both: you cannot defend a civilization while ignoring what it grew from. The West did not begin with a theory of truth, dignity, and law. It began with people who believed they would answer to God for how they treated the truth, their neighbor, and the law. That belief is the root. Water it, and the pillars will stand.


This is my position, and I’m open to a better argument. If you believe I have misjudged any of these points, I want to hear your reasons.
