# 261106 - Midterm Exit Poll Analysis by National Review

# The Voters Weighed Us


### What the 2026 exit poll says, and what a righteous party would do with it


*A position paper occasioned by Jim Geraghty’s “Why Republicans Lost” (National Review, The Morning Jolt, 6 November 2026)*


Thomas Lee Abshier, ND — 6 November 2026


**Source article:** [Why Republicans Lost — Jim Geraghty, National Review](https://www.nationalreview.com/the-morning-jolt/why-republicans-lost/)


---


## Plain language summary


On Tuesday, the Democrats won both the House and the Senate, flipping Senate seats in places like Kansas, Iowa, Texas, Ohio, and South Carolina. Jim Geraghty argues we do not need anonymous pollsters to explain it, because about 100,000 voters told us why in the exit poll. I agree, and I go one step further. The voters did not reject conservative principles. They judged conduct: a war with no stated end; an energy bill that came home to every kitchen; tariffs that hit their own neighbors; enforcement that fell on the powerless; a party too comfortable with men who hold contempt for their fellow citizens; and a leader most voters believe serves himself first. The right answer is not spin, and it is not surrender. It is repentance, which means admitting what went wrong, and a better plan, which I call The New ERA Vision: abundant Energy, Righteousness as the standard, and Accountability for those who govern.


---


## Why the explanation matters more than the loss


A party that loses badly will always be offered two comforting stories. The first is that the voters were deceived: the media lied, the questions were loaded, our people stayed home. The second is that a trusted insider has private numbers showing the problem was something other than what everyone can see. Mr. Geraghty rightly sets both aside. When roughly a hundred thousand voters in forty-five states sit down and answer a long questionnaire, the honest thing to do is read their answers.


I write this paper because how a people explains a defeat tests character before it tests strategy. Scripture gives us the pattern of a ruler who lost his kingdom by refusing to hear. When Rehoboam asked how to answer the people who had come to him burdened, the old men who had served his father told him plainly: *“If thou wilt be a servant unto this people this day, and wilt serve them, and answer them, and speak good words to them, then they will be thy servants for ever”* **(1 Kings 12:7)**. He went instead to the young men who had grown up with him, the friends who told him what he wanted to hear, and the kingdom divided under him.


The party now has its choice between the elders and the friends. The exit poll is the elders’ counsel.


## What happened, briefly


The facts of the result are not in dispute. Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress. In the Senate, they flipped at least seven seats: Kansas, which had not sent a Democrat to the Senate since 1932, along with Iowa, Texas, Ohio, Maine, North Carolina, and South Carolina. In the House, they will begin January with their largest majority in nearly two decades, including districts President Trump carried by double digits two years ago.


That is not a narrow loss to be explained by turnout in a few counties. It is a verdict.


## Who moved, and who did not


Before turning to the reasons, it is worth settling who actually changed their votes, because the answer rules out several popular excuses.


**Republicans did not simply stay home.** Party identification in the exit poll was 35 percent Democrat, 32 percent Republican, and 33 percent independent, against 31, 35, and 34 two years ago. That is a real shift, but each group remains roughly a third of the electorate, as it has been for years.


**Some Republicans crossed over, but not many.** About 8 percent of Republican men and 5 percent of Republican women voted for Democrats, while only 1 to 2 percent of Democrats crossed the other way. That matters at the margins, but it is not the main story.


**The independents decided it.** Two years ago independents split nearly evenly, 49 percent for Kamala Harris and 46 percent for Donald Trump, close enough that higher Republican turnout carried the day. This year they went Democratic by 58 to 38. Self-described moderates, who were 41 percent of the electorate, voted Democratic by about two to one.


**Demographics did not decide it.** The electorate was about 73 percent white, little different from the country that gave Republicans the White House and both chambers two years ago. This was not a country that changed its composition. It was the same people, changing their judgment.


The lesson is plain. We lost the voters who are winnable but never guaranteed: the people who do not belong to either tribe and who judge each party by what it does with power. Those are precisely the voters a governing party must earn every two years, and the voters a righteous party should most want to earn, because they judge on the merits.


## What they told us they were weighing


### The war


When asked how important the war with Iran was to their vote for Congress, 74 percent said it was crucial or a major factor, and that group voted Democratic by 64 to 34. Asked whether taking military action against Iran was the right decision, 63 percent said it was the wrong one.


I need to be clear about where I stand, because I have made the Iran war the lead issue of this campaign. I do not believe the answer is to walk away. A regime that has spent forty years exporting terror and racing toward a bomb does not become harmless because we grow tired of it, and a war abandoned halfway is usually a war fought again later at a higher price.


But I do not dismiss the 63 percent either, and I think the poll tells us what they objected to. Asked whether America should take a more active role in the world, a less active role, or about the same, voters split 28, 43, and 28. Both the “more active” and the “less active” voters went heavily Democratic. Only those who wanted the same role as now went Republican. Mr. Geraghty observes, dryly, that the administration seems to have found the precise posture that irritates both sides. I would put it more simply: the people could not see the plan. The hawk saw a war being fought without the resolve to win it, and the dove saw a war with no stated end. Both were reacting to the absence of a clear objective, a clear strategy, and a clear account given to the nation that pays for it.


And the nation has paid for it at the gas pump and the heating bill. Only 4 percent named foreign policy as the most important issue, but 39 percent named the economy, and 89 percent said the cost of living was crucial or a major factor in their vote. A war that closes the Strait of Hormuz does not stay overseas. It comes home in the price of every gallon and every grocery truck. That is why I have argued that energy is the foundation of national security. A nation that produces abundant, cheap energy of its own, which is what thorium molten-salt reactors would give us, cannot be held hostage by a strait half a world away. We went to war without first securing the home front, and the home front noticed.


### The tariffs


When asked whether President Trump’s tariffs helped or hurt the national economy, only 21 percent said they helped and 66 percent said they hurt. Asked about the economy in their own area, only 14 percent said helped and 64 percent said hurt. Sixty-three percent said higher prices were due more to the President’s policies than to factors outside his control.


This site has published the strongest case for tariffs I know, the argument that a nation should never follow rules its enemy ignores, and that a government which refuses to answer economic predation is not defending liberty but exposing its people to coercion. I still hold that tariffs can be a legitimate weapon aimed at a predatory nation. But a weapon is aimed. A broad tariff on goods from friends and rivals alike is not a weapon aimed at China; it is a tax on the American family, collected at the checkout counter.


Here the test I apply to every policy is useful. Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? And would I want it done to me? A targeted tariff that protects a critical industry from a hostile state can pass all three. A tariff whose cost falls on the widow buying groceries, and whose benefit falls on a negotiating posture she will never see, fails the third. Two-thirds of voters said, in effect, they felt it was done to them.


### Immigration enforcement


Fifty-eight percent said the administration’s immigration enforcement had gone too far, 20 percent said not far enough, and 22 percent said about right. Sixty percent disapproved of how ICE is doing its job.


Yet voters did not reject border security. The 14 percent who named immigration as their top issue voted Republican by 89 to 10, and voters still rated Republicans slightly better than Democrats on handling immigration, 43 to 41. The people want a secure country. What they rejected was the manner of the enforcement.


I wrote in September that the border numbers are real and that something essential was missing from every scorecard: mandatory E-Verify and the prosecution of employers who knowingly hire illegal labor. Enforcement has fallen on the powerless and spared the powerful. When the man who is seized is a fifty-year-old cook who has bothered no one for twenty years, and the business owner who hired him under the table goes untouched, the citizen watching does not see justice. He sees a just law administered with an unjust weight. *“A false balance is abomination to the LORD: but a just weight is his delight”* **(Proverbs 11:1)**. Enforcement aimed first at criminals and at the employers who profit from illegal labor would be both more effective and more righteous, and I believe the voters would support it.


### Contempt for fellow citizens


Mr. Geraghty raises something that the numbers alone do not show, and I agree with him. If figures associated with a party dine with, platform, and keep company with men who argue that Hitler was not the villain of the Second World War, or who rage against fellow Americans because of their ancestry, then the minority voter is being told something about how that party regards him. He will believe it.


This is not a question of strategy for me. It is a question of righteousness. God *“hath made of one blood all nations of men for to dwell on all the face of the earth”* **(Acts 17:26)**. A Christian party cannot hold its fellow citizens in contempt for the color of their skin or the country of their grandparents and still call itself Christian. The remedy is not a better outreach program. It is to show such men the door, plainly and by name, and to stop treating them as a constituency to be managed.


### The man at the top


Many voters treated this as a referendum on the President. Forty-four percent said their House vote expressed opposition to Donald Trump, compared with 16 percent who voted to express support. His approval stood at 37 percent. Sixty-two percent said he prioritizes his own personal interests over the country’s. Fifty-seven percent said he lacks the mental capability to serve effectively.


I take no pleasure in those numbers, and I gave this administration credit in September for what it has genuinely accomplished on the border and in the courts. But when six voters in ten believe the President serves himself first, that is not a messaging problem to be solved by better slogans. It goes to Accountability, the third pillar of what I am proposing. A leader is a steward, and the people judge the steward by whose interests he serves. Whether their judgment is fair in every particular, it is the judgment they rendered, and a party that wishes to govern again must give them reason to render a different one.


## What the poll does not say


It is just as important to notice what the voters did not reject.


More than half, 52 percent, said support for transgender rights in society has gone too far, against 26 percent who said not far enough. Only 18 percent called themselves democratic socialists. Forty-one percent called themselves moderate and 33 percent conservative, while 26 percent called themselves liberal. This is not a country that has embraced the left’s program. It is a center-right country that judged the party in power on its conduct and found it wanting.


That cuts two ways. To my own side: you cannot win on being right about the issues voters agree with you on while you fail them on the issues they are living through. A true principle does not save a party that is failing people on the cost of their groceries. To the Democrats: the people have handed you Congress, not a mandate for socialism. Read your own exit poll before you mistake the one for the other.


## What a righteous party would do now


I do not believe the Republican Party lost because it was too conservative, and I do not believe it will recover by becoming less so. I believe it lost because it failed the test of conduct, and the way back is the same as after any failure: honest admission, then a better course.


*“Righteousness exalteth a nation: but sin is a reproach to any people”* **(Proverbs 14:34)**. In practical terms, I would urge five things.


- **Own the result.** Read the exit poll in public and answer it point by point. Release any private polling leaders cite as the basis for their explanations, so citizens can weigh it for themselves. Anonymous sources are no more trustworthy when they flatter us.

- **Give the nation an account of the war.** State the objective, the strategy, and what victory looks like, and tell the people what it will cost and why it is worth it. A free people will carry a hard burden when they understand it. They will not carry one they are asked to accept on faith.

- **Secure the home front with energy.** Make abundant domestic energy, including the rapid development of thorium molten-salt reactors, the foundation of both economic and national security, so that no hostile power can tax the American family through the price of oil.

- **Aim the tariff, or holster it.** Use trade measures as targeted weapons against predatory states and critical-industry threats, and stop taxing our own people across the board.

- **Enforce the law with a just weight.** Mandatory E-Verify, prosecution of employers who knowingly hire illegal labor, and enforcement priorities aimed at criminals first. And show the door to anyone who traffics in contempt for fellow Americans because of their race or ancestry.


These are not concessions to the other party. They are what righteousness, love of neighbor, and the golden rule would have asked of us all along. I hold them as my position, and I hold them open to a better argument. But I do not think the voters were wrong to ask for them.

## Sources and their limits


The election results in this paper come from the major news projections published on 4 November 2026 (NBC News and others), which agree on the outcome in both chambers. The exit-poll figures come from Mr. Geraghty’s article, which reports a survey of roughly 100,000 voters in 45 states combining in-person Election Day interviews with mail, phone, and online responses to capture early and mail-in voters.


Three cautions apply. First, exit polls are weighted to match the final returns and are routinely revised in the weeks after an election; individual figures here may move by a few points. Second, I have not seen the full questionnaire, its subgroup sample sizes, or its weighting method, and the wording of a question shapes its answer. A question about “immigrants” that does not separate legal from illegal immigration, for example, measures something less precise than it appears to. Third, an exit poll records what voters say their reasons are, which is not always the same as what moved them. None of these cautions, in my judgment, changes the central finding. The margins on the war, the tariffs, enforcement, and the President’s standing are too large and too consistent with the result to be artifacts of method.


The account of the war and the energy shock draws on my earlier papers on this site, and the immigration argument on my September paper “A Just Weight.” Where I have relied on the mainstream record, I acknowledge that institutions curate it in their own interests. The voters’ own answers, however, are the closest thing we have to primary testimony, and I have tried to let them speak.
