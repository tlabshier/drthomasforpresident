# 261116 - Thorium - Copenhagen Atomics - Shipping Container-Sized Reactor

# Thorium in a Shipping Container: What a Danish Reactor Teaches America About Energy


*By Thomas Lee Abshier, ND*


**A position paper responding to [“Copenhagen Atomics designs container-sized reactor with enough thorium fuel for 100 years”](https://www.thecooldown.com/green-tech/copenhagen-atomics-container-sized-thorium-reactor-design/) (Ray Brennan, *The Cool Down*, 16 November 2026), which summarizes Dr. Ben Miles’s video tour of the company.**


---


## Plain language summary


A Danish company, Copenhagen Atomics, is building a nuclear reactor that fits in a shipping container, runs on liquid fuel, and is designed to turn thorium, a common and mostly discarded element, into its own fuel. The engineering problems are real: the neutron budget is tight, the hot salts corrode steel, and the design has not yet run on nuclear fuel. But the direction is right, and America invented this technology in the 1960s, then walked away from it. I propose that we treat thorium as a strategic energy reserve instead of a mining waste, open a clear licensing lane for liquid-fuel reactors, host thorium reactor testing at our national laboratories, and build the fuel supply chain these reactors need. Then let private companies, not taxpayers, carry the commercial risk. Cheap, abundant, clean power is the foundation of the New ERA Vision, and thorium is the best candidate I know of to supply it.


---


## I. What the Danes are building


The article describes a reactor its designers call “The Onion,” because its core is a set of nested shells of fluid. At the center is a molten fluoride salt carrying the nuclear fuel, at roughly 1,112°F. Around it is heavy water, held at about 176°F, which slows the neutrons so they are more likely to cause further reactions. The outermost shell is a “blanket” of molten salt containing thorium. The whole core is about eight feet across, produces 100 megawatts of heat, and the complete system, with its pumps, pipes, and heat exchangers, is designed to fit in a standard 40-foot shipping container.


The key to the design is the blanket. Thorium cannot sustain a chain reaction by itself. When a thorium atom absorbs a stray neutron, it decays over a few weeks into uranium-233, which *is* fissile. The uranium bred in the blanket is moved into the central fuel channel and burned there. The reactor starts on ordinary low-enriched uranium, and over two to three years it shifts to running mainly on the uranium it has bred from thorium. The company says its ten-ton thorium supply could last more than a century, though it plans to replenish the blanket every ten years.


The company is not a slide deck. It has run a full-size non-nuclear prototype at operating temperature, has kept a molten-salt pump loop running continuously for two years, and produces purified salts by the ton. In 2026 it signed a letter of intent with Rare Earths Norway to secure thorium, and this month it announced a partnership with Urenco to build a facility in Copenhagen that converts enriched uranium into the fluoride form molten-salt reactors need. Its first nuclear test, a critical experiment at Switzerland’s Paul Scherrer Institute, is now scheduled for 2028, and commercial units are targeted for the early 2030s. The company says it can mass-produce reactors on an assembly line at the rate of one a day, sell power under $20 per megawatt-hour, and build, own, and operate the units itself, so that the customer and the taxpayer carry no investment.


I take the engineering seriously. I also mark which parts are demonstrated and which are promised. The assembly-line rate, the price, and the century of thorium are company projections. The reactor has not yet run on nuclear fuel.


## II. The hard problems


The article names three engineering challenges. Each one is real, and each one has a plausible answer.


**The neutron budget.** Breeding from thorium with slow neutrons is the hardest form of breeding. Every fission releases a little more than two neutrons. One must go on to cause the next fission. Another must be captured by thorium to make the replacement fuel. Everything else that escapes the core, or is swallowed by the salt, the steel, or the fission products, is lost. The margin is thin. Copenhagen Atomics projects that only about 2% of its neutrons would leak from the core, which is what the layered geometry is designed to achieve. That figure comes from simulation. Whether the reactor actually breeds more fuel than it burns can only be settled by running one, which is exactly what the Swiss test is for.


**Corrosion.** Molten fluoride salts strip the protective oxide film from steel, and moisture in the salt drives reactions that dissolve metal from the vessel walls. The company measured commercially available lithium salts removing more than a millimeter of steel per year. The answer is salt chemistry: the video identifies moisture as the culprit, and pure lithium fluoride can coexist with bare steel. This is why the company has invested so heavily in salt purification. The design also limits the damage by treating the reactor module as replaceable. The company has said its price includes swapping modules every five years. A part that is designed to be replaced does not need to last fifty years.


**Heat across a narrow gap.** Keeping water at 176°F a short distance from salt at 1,112°F is a demanding thermal design challenge. The company separates the layers with graphite felt, whose fine carbon fibers slow conduction and repeatedly absorb and re-emit infrared radiation. Miles put the heat lost across that gap at about 35 kilowatts out of 100 megawatts, or roughly 1 in 3,000. The heavy water only moderates neutrons. It does not make steam, and it does not need to be under pressure.


That last point deserves emphasis. The great hazards of the reactors most Americans picture are high pressure and solid fuel that can melt if cooling fails. A molten-salt reactor runs at near-atmospheric pressure, so no stored pressure can blow radioactive material out of the building. Its fuel is already liquid, so nothing needs to be melted down. If power fails, a frozen salt plug at the bottom of the vessel melts and the fuel drains by gravity into tanks where the chain reaction cannot continue. These are not promises. They are the physics of the design, and Oak Ridge National Laboratory ran a molten-salt reactor for roughly four years in the 1960s.


## III. Why this belongs on an American platform


**We invented this, and we set it aside.** The Molten-Salt Reactor Experiment at Oak Ridge ran from 1965 to 1969 under Alvin Weinberg, and it ran on uranium-233 for part of that time. The program ended for reasons more related to budget priorities and a national commitment to other reactor types than to any failure of the concept. The Danish design and China’s program both descend from that American work.


**China has taken the lead we abandoned.** In November 2025, the Chinese Academy of Sciences announced that its 2-megawatt experimental thorium molten-salt reactor in Gansu Province had converted thorium into uranium fuel inside an operating reactor, the first time anyone has done so. China has not released detailed data, so I hold the specifics loosely, but the direction is unmistakable: China is building the industrial base for thorium power, and it has said it plans larger units. A nation that falls behind in energy falls behind in manufacturing, in defense, and finally in liberty.


**Thorium is lying at our feet.** Thorium is found on every continent and is roughly three times as abundant as uranium. It comes out of the ground with rare-earth ores, and under current rules that is a problem rather than a gift. Because thorium is mildly radioactive, a rare-earth processor that produces it takes on federal radioactive-materials licensing on top of environmental permitting, and must pay to dispose of the thorium. The cost and regulation of thorium disposal helped make the Mountain Pass mine in California uncompetitive, and it still weighs on the economics of the reopened mine. In other words, our regulations treat a century of fuel as a liability, and that liability helped push rare-earth processing to China. Fix the first problem, and we help fix the second.


**The federal government has just shown it can move.** A May 2025 executive order directed the Department of Energy to bring at least three advanced reactor designs to criticality by 4 July 2026. Four made it, the first of them about nine months after construction began. On 29 April 2026, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission’s new Part 53 licensing framework took effect. These are real reforms, and I credit them. But none of the four reactors is a thorium breeder, and none is a liquid-fuel reactor. The lane that matters most for thorium is still unpaved.


## IV. The moral case


I do not support a policy because it is clever or profitable. I ask three questions of every policy, in order: Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? And would I want it done to me?


**Is it righteous?** God gave man dominion over the earth and charged him to dress it and keep it. Dominion is not plunder, and keeping is not paralysis. Leaving a fuel that could power civilization for thousands of years buried in mine tailings, or paying to bury it again, is not caution. It is poor stewardship. Scripture honors the search for what is hidden in creation: *“It is the glory of God to conceal a thing: but the honour of kings is to search out a matter”* **(Proverbs 25:2)**. Thorium is one of those matters. Searching it out is the proper work of a free people.


**Is it loving?** Energy is the hidden cost inside everything a family buys: food, housing, heat, transportation, and the wages of every job that depends on manufacturing. When energy is expensive, the poor pay first and pay most. Where winters run below zero, an electric bill is not an abstraction. A reactor that can be built in a factory, shipped by truck, and run for decades on a common element offers cheap, reliable power without the smoke of coal and without the intermittency of wind and sun. That is love expressed as plenty. *“Where no oxen are, the crib is clean: but much increase is by the strength of the ox”* **(Proverbs 14:4)**. A nation that refuses every risk keeps a clean stall and an empty granary.


**Would I want it done to me?** This is the hardest test, and it requires honesty. Would I accept a thorium reactor in my own county? I would, on three conditions: that an honest test had proven it, that its safety rests on physics rather than on the vigilance of operators, and that its waste is managed openly. The molten-salt design meets the second condition by its nature. Copenhagen Atomics’ test at the Paul Scherrer Institute is meant to meet the first. The third remains to be shown at commercial scale, though the design’s ability to burn the long-lived elements in today’s spent fuel is one of its strongest promises. It could turn waste that must be guarded for a hundred thousand years into waste that decays in a few centuries. I made the case for treating spent fuel as a resource in an earlier paper on this site, and this design is one way to do it.


The biggest risk is proliferation, because uranium-233 is a weapons-usable material. The uranium bred in a thorium reactor is contaminated with uranium-232, whose decay products emit intense gamma radiation, making it dangerous and difficult to handle and easy to detect. That is a real barrier, but it is not a guarantee, so the fuel cycle must be safeguarded and inspected. I do not wave this away. I weigh it, and I conclude that a safeguarded thorium fuel cycle is a smaller danger than a world where cheap energy is controlled by regimes that answer to no one.


## V. What I would do


The federal role is to clear the road, not to buy the car. Copenhagen Atomics intends to finance, build, own, and operate its reactors without taxpayer investment, and that is the right model. Government should remove the obstacles it created and do the few things only government can do.


 	- **Reclassify byproduct thorium as a strategic energy reserve.** Thorium recovered from rare-earth processing should be stored as a national asset, not buried as waste at the processor’s expense. That one change lowers the cost of domestic rare-earth production, and banks fuel for the coming century.

 	- **Open a licensing lane for liquid-fuel reactors.** The rules of the past half-century were written for solid-fuel pressurized water reactors. A reactor whose fuel is liquid, which processes its fuel while running, and which breeds from a blanket needs rules written for what it is. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission should finish that framework, with firm deadlines, and the Department of Energy should extend its pilot program to thorium molten-salt designs.

 	- **Host the tests here.** Our national laboratories, Idaho first among them, should invite thorium molten-salt developers, American and allied, to run critical experiments on American soil. The first commercial thorium reactor should not be Chinese, and if the Danes are ready before we are, the second should be built in partnership with us.

 	- **Build the fuel chain.** Molten-salt reactors need uranium in fluoride form, highly purified salts, and lithium enriched in lithium-7. The United States has depended on foreign suppliers for lithium-7 for years. Copenhagen Atomics is building lines for all three in Denmark. America should have its own, privately built and federally permitted on a timeline measured in months, not decades.

 	- **Prove before promising.** The government should not pick winners or guarantee prices. It should insist that every claim, including the neutron budget, the corrosion rate, and the cost per megawatt-hour, be demonstrated in a real reactor and published. *“Prove all things; hold fast that which is good”* **(1 Thessalonians 5:21)**.


## VI. Where I stand


I stand for thorium molten-salt reactors as the foundation of America’s energy future. I hold that position open to a better argument, and I have tried to state honestly where its weak points are. Thermal breeding from thorium has not yet been shown to sustain itself in a commercial-scale reactor. The 2% neutron leakage figure is simulated. The corrosion problem is managed, not abolished. The prices and production rates are projections from a company raising capital. Any of these could slow the timeline.


None of them changes the direction. The physics of the molten-salt reactor makes it safe by its nature. The fuel is abundant, cheap, and already being mined. The technology is American by birth. And the alternative, continuing to treat a century of fuel as waste while a rival nation builds the industry we pioneered, is the one choice I am confident is wrong. Energy is the E in the New ERA Vision because a righteous society still needs bread, heat, and work, and abundant energy is how a free people provides them. I believe thorium is how we get there.
