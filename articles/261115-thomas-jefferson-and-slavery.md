# 261115 - Thomas Jefferson and Slavery

# Why Jefferson Did Not Free His Slaves: The Law, the Debt, and the Choices Inside the Trap

*By Thomas Lee Abshier, ND*

A Facebook post on November 12 answered a question many Americans ask: why did the man who wrote that "all men are created equal" not free the people he held as slaves? Its answer was that Jefferson wanted to free them but could not. When he had the money, Virginia law forbade it. When the law allowed it, he was buried in debt.

I stand largely with that answer. The "simple hypocrite" charge against Jefferson is wrong. The trap was real, and most of the post's history checks out. But the post went a step further and said history "clears up the paradox," and I cannot go that far. Jefferson was trapped partly by the law and the debt and partly by his own choices. Some of those choices were open to him, and he did not make them. That is the part of the story with the most to teach us, so I want to tell the whole of it.

## The question under the question

When people ask why Jefferson did not free his slaves, they are usually asking something deeper: was the Declaration a lie? If the author did not live by "all men are created equal," was the principle ever real?

My answer is that the principle does not depend on its author. It was true when Jefferson wrote it and true when he failed it, because it rests on the fact that every man bears the image of God. That is not the American founders' gift to us; it is God's. The abolitionists, Frederick Douglass, and Lincoln all held Jefferson's words up against the nation's practice and won the argument. A principle that can convict its own author is a strong principle.

So the question about Jefferson the man is not a question about whether equality is true. It is a question about what keeps a man from doing what he knows is right. That question applies to all of us.

## What the post gets right

**Virginia law blocked private emancipation for most of his early life.** Before 1782, a Virginia owner could not free a slave without permission from the governor and council. Jefferson entered the House of Burgesses in 1769. In his autobiography, written in 1821, he says that in his first session he asked the senior burgess Richard Bland to propose letting owners free their slaves, that he seconded the motion, and that Bland was denounced for it. As a young lawyer Jefferson also took freedom suits without a fee, and he lost them.

**He tried to change the law more than once.** In the late 1770s he helped draft an amendment to Virginia's revised laws that would have freed all children born to slaves after a set date. It was never offered, because he judged the legislature would not pass it. In his autobiography he wrote that *"the public mind would not yet bear the proposition."* In 1784 he wrote a clause into the ordinance for the western territories that would have banned slavery there after 1800. Jefferson wrote to Madison that the clause "was lost by an individual vote only." When the vote came, his two fellow Virginia delegates voted against him. If that clause had passed, slavery would have been shut out of the whole West, south as well as north of the Ohio River. Few "what ifs" in American history weigh more.

**The Wayles inheritance changed his finances for good.** In 1773 his father-in-law, John Wayles, died. The estate was worth £30,000, but Wayles was in debt to Farrell and Jones for £11,000. Martha and her husband Thomas Jefferson inherited the Willis Creek and Elk Hill plantations and a total of 135 slaves, including members of the Hemings family. They also inherited £4,000 in debt. (The post says "dozens" of slaves; the number was 135.) Jefferson sold land to pay his share. He was paid in Revolutionary paper money that soon lost most of its value, and after the war the Treaty of Paris let British creditors collect what they were owed in hard money. He had sold the land and still owed the debt, plus interest. He dealt with the Wayles debt for nearly thirty years.

**The 1782 law opened the door only partway.** Virginia allowed private emancipation in 1782, but freed slaves could still be seized to pay debts their owner had incurred before freeing them. An insolvent man could not free anyone. After 1806, a freed slave had to leave Virginia within a year or face re-enslavement, which meant freedom also meant exile unless the legislature made an exception.

**The Nicholas loan was the final blow.** In May 1818 Jefferson had endorsed two notes totaling $20,000 for money that Nicholas owed to the Second Bank of the United States, thus making Jefferson personally liable for that amount if Nicholas could not pay. The Panic of 1819 ruined Nicholas, and after Nicholas died in October 1820, the burden of this obligation fell directly on Jefferson, and he made almost no headway in paying it off prior to his own death on 4 July 1826. When Jefferson died, his debts came to more than $107,000. That is between one and three million dollars today, depending on how you convert it.

**His family depended on Monticello.** His daughter Martha's husband, Thomas Mann Randolph, was insolvent by the end of Jefferson's life. Monticello was the only home Martha and her children had.

**He sold his library to pay his creditors.** After the British burned the Capitol in 1814, Jefferson sold Congress his library of about 6,500 volumes, and that collection rebuilt the Library of Congress. He used the money to pay down his debts.

So the post's main claim is sound. By the 1790s, and certainly by the 1820s, Jefferson could not legally have freed everyone at Monticello. His will could not have done it either, because his creditors had first claim. In 1827, 130 people from Monticello's enslaved community were sold at auction to help settle Jefferson's debt. In the end he lost everything, and so did they.

## What the post leaves out

My editorial standard is to weigh evidence by what kind it is and to give the other side its strongest case. Here is what the critics would add, and where they are right.

**The 1769 story rests on Jefferson's own memory.** There is no record of Bland's motion in the House journals. The only source is Jefferson's autobiography, written fifty years later. I think it is probably true, since Jefferson had no clear reason to make it up and it fits the rest of his early record. But it is one man's account of his own good deed, and it should be treated that way.

**His emancipation plan included removing the freed people.** The post says his plan of the 1770s offered "education and training so freed people could succeed." That is true, but incomplete. The plan, as he described it in *Notes on the State of Virginia*, educated the freed children so they could be colonized outside the country, and it brought in white immigrants to replace their labor. "I have seen no proposition so expedient . . . as that of emancipation of those [slaves] born after a given day, and of their education and expatriation at a proper age," Jefferson wrote in 1814. In the same book he wrote about Black Americans as possibly inferior by nature. He offered that as a suspicion, not a settled conclusion, but it was a suspicion that served the interests of men like him. He never imagined free Black and white Americans living together as neighbors. That was a failure of vision, not of money.

**He went quiet in public.** After 1785, while still holding his belief in the injustice of slavery, he was publicly silent. As Secretary of State, Vice President, and President he had the strongest voice in the country. Apart from signing the 1807 ban on importing slaves, which the Constitution had already allowed for, he did not use that voice against slavery.

**He told Edward Coles not to free his slaves, and Coles did it anyway.** In 1814 the young Virginian Edward Coles asked Jefferson to lead an emancipation effort. Jefferson declined, said he was too old, and urged Coles to stay in Virginia and work for gradual change. Coles took his slaves to Illinois, freed them, and gave each family 160 acres. That shows a man with fewer resources than Jefferson had in his prime could do it if he was willing to leave Virginia and take the loss. Coles did not have Jefferson's debts, so the comparison is not exact, but it shows that the road was not entirely closed.

**He turned down the Kosciuszko bequest.** This is the hardest fact for the "he wanted to but could not" thesis. In 1798 the Polish general Tadeusz Kosciuszko wrote a will that named Jefferson executor and asked him to use Kosciuszko's U.S. assets to buy numerous slaves out of their servitude, provide for their education and purchase land for them to farm. That money would have paid for exactly what Jefferson said freed people needed. When Kosciuszko died in 1817, Jefferson declined to serve, citing his age, and asked the court to appoint someone else. In fairness, Kosciuszko had written later wills that clouded the bequest, the legal mess lasted decades, and the Supreme Court eventually gave the money to his European heirs. Jefferson may have been right that the bequest would not survive in court. Still, he was handed money set aside for the very purpose he named, and he did not try.

**He bought, sold, and divided families.** In his lifetime, he owned 607 men, women, and children, sold or gave away over 100 enslaved people, and purchased around 20 individuals. Some of those sales paid his debts. Monticello's historians note that he sometimes kept families together and sometimes knowingly split them up.

**His spending made the debt worse.** The Wayles debt, the paper-money disaster, and the Nicholas note were not his fault. But he also rebuilt Monticello over decades, imported wine and books, and entertained heavily. Jefferson spent $7,500 ($200,000 in current dollars) on wine in his first presidential term alone. Part of that was official hospitality the government did not pay for, as the post says, but not all of it. The post calls him an "incorruptible public servant," and on bribery and self-dealing the record backs that up. He did not get rich in office. But being honest is not the same as being careful with money, and his debts came from both bad luck and his own habits.

**The people he did free were Hemings family members.** The nine people Jefferson freed in his lifetime and will were all members of the Hemings family. The weight of evidence, including DNA results and the testimony of Madison Hemings, indicates that Jefferson fathered Sally Hemings's children, and those children were among the people he freed or let leave. Whatever else that shows, it shows that when he chose to free someone, he found a way, and the people he chose were mostly his own blood.

## Why the critics' charge still has force

I have said the simple hypocrisy charge is wrong. Here is why the charge keeps coming back, and why it is not just modern self-righteousness.

First, the trap was partly of his own making. The debts were real, but a man who believed slavery was an injustice, and who expected that *"God is just"* and that *"his justice cannot sleep forever"* (words he wrote in *Notes on Virginia*), lived for fifty years on that injustice and spent what it produced. He did not free anyone during his years of solvency, either by petitioning the governor and council for exceptions or by moving people out of Virginia the way Coles did.

Second, the economic interest was obvious. The people he held were his most valuable assets and the collateral behind his loans. When a man's beliefs point one way and his money points the other, and his actions keep following the money, people are right to ask which one was really in charge. Jefferson knew this. He described slavery to John Holmes in 1820 as holding *"a wolf by the ear"*: you can neither hold him nor safely let him go. That image describes his own position as much as the country's.

Third, the law he could not overcome was made by men like him. The Virginia legislature that blocked emancipation was a body of slaveholders acting in their own interest. Jefferson was one of its leaders. To say "the system trapped him" is true, but he was also part of the system.

## The moral lesson

On this site I test every policy against three questions: Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? Would I want it done to me? Jefferson knew the answers for slavery. He said it was unjust, he said it degraded the master as well as the slave, and he never claimed he would want to be held as property himself. His failure was not in knowing what was right. It was in finding the cost of doing it too high, and then finding, as the years went by, that the cost had become impossible to pay.

Scripture names that danger plainly. *"The rich ruleth over the poor, and the borrower is servant to the lender"* **(Proverbs 22:7)**. Jefferson, the master of Monticello, ended his life as a servant of his creditors, and the people he held were sold to pay them. Debt took away his freedom to do good. That is a lesson for nations as much as for men. A country buried in debt also loses the freedom to do what is right.

Scripture also makes clear that the time to do good is when you can. *"Withhold not good from them to whom it is due, when it is in the power of thine hand to do it"* **(Proverbs 3:27)**. And: *"Therefore to him that knoweth to do good, and doeth it not, to him it is sin"* **(James 4:17)**. Jefferson's window was narrow, and the law shut much of it. But the parts of it that were open, in his solvent years, in the Coles road, and in the Kosciuszko bequest, he let close. Each delay made the next step harder, until the debt made it impossible. Postponing righteousness works this way: the price of doing right goes up the longer you wait, and eventually you cannot pay it.

Finally, the lesson about law. Jefferson's 1769 effort and his 1784 clause failed because the men around him would not vote for them. A single vote in 1784 might have kept slavery out of the entire West. When a legislature forbids a man to do good, or a nation makes righteousness illegal, the blame falls on everyone who voted for it, not only on the man caught inside it. We judge Jefferson for not doing what was right; we should also judge the laws and the voters that made it so hard. That is why I keep calling citizens to look at what their own laws allow and forbid, and to change them before the debt comes due.

Jefferson was not a fraud, and he was not a saint trapped by others. He was a gifted, flawed man who saw the truth clearly, worked for it while it was cheap, and let it go when it became expensive. His words outlived his failures, and they did the work he did not do. My hope is that we learn from both.
