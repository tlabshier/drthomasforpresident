# Debanking: A False Balance in the House of Money


Oct 17, 2026 · @Thomas Lee Abshier, ND


## Plain language summary


On October 14, 2026, the federal regulator of national banks (the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, or OCC) reported that all nine of the largest national banks had policies, between 2020 and September 2025, that led them to close accounts or refuse credit to lawful customers because of their political or religious beliefs or their lawful lines of business. Most of the people cut off were conservatives, Christians, Trump supporters, gun businesses, and energy companies. Some were on the left.


**My position:** a man should not lose access to his money because of what he believes, says, or votes for. A bank enjoys privileges the rest of us do not — a government charter, federally insured deposits, and access to the Federal Reserve. Those privileges carry a duty to serve every lawful customer by one honest standard. A bank may refuse a customer for real financial reasons (fraud, bad credit, money laundering), but it must say so in writing and give him a way to appeal. It may never refuse him because of his speech, worship, or politics.


This rule must protect the man I disagree with as fully as it protects me. The tool used against conservatives under one administration can be used against anyone under the next, so Congress must write the protection into law and make it enforceable before a jury, not leave it to executive orders that change with each President.


*Provenance: Claude drafted this paper in my voice at my request on 17 October 2026, responding to Miranda Devine’s New York Post report on the OCC findings. I have marked the places where I am taking a position still open to a better argument.*


## What the report found


The OCC reviewed the nine largest national banks it supervises: JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Citibank, Wells Fargo, U.S. Bank, Capital One, PNC, TD Bank, and BMO. The President ordered the review in an August 7, 2025 executive order, “Guaranteeing Fair Access to Banking for All Americans.” The regulator examined roughly 100,000 complaints and a large volume of internal bank records and concluded that all nine banks had policies that could lead employees to deny or limit service on political, religious, or lawful-business grounds.


The examples are specific, and they come from the banks’ own documents:


- **WePay (owned by JPMorgan Chase)** closed the account of a merchant selling Trump and “Make America Great Again” merchandise in 2021, classifying it as material that promoted hate, violence, or racial intolerance. Its screening software flagged phrases such as “MAGA,” “Make America Great,” and “America First” in merchants’ profiles, websites, and transactions. Several of those terms were added to the screen after January 6, 2021.

- **WePay** also closed the account of a religious organization because the Southern Poverty Law Center (SPLC) had listed it as a hate group, and closed an account raising funds for a Christian’s legal challenge to an anti-discrimination law.

- **PNC** refused a line of credit to a successful media company because one owner’s foundation had given to Christian conservative groups and another owner was a conservative commentator. The employee who recommended refusal wrote that it was hard to justify aligning the bank with owners whose opinions ran contrary to the bank’s “beliefs/values.”

- **Wells Fargo** pressured a payment processor to drop a religious nonprofit over its views on marriage and declined loans to two firearms businesses, partly out of fear of pressure from the Biden administration and members of Congress.

- **Bank of America** dropped private-prison businesses citing “changing policymaker attitudes,” then tried to restore one account months into the Trump administration.

- **Capital One** refused service to partisan entities supporting one party’s candidates — including both an abortion-rights organization and a gun-rights organization — out of concern for how the association would look.


The banks most often cited negative press, protests, criticism from activist groups such as the SPLC, and fear of regulators or politicians. The OCC did not estimate how many customers were wrongly cut off or what it cost them. The banks say they have revised their practices; the Comptroller says those reforms are not enough. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has opened its own investigation. JPMorgan says WePay’s process did not meet its standards, that it corrected the problem, and that WePay no longer operates.


## Why this matters: money is the door to ordinary life


In 1900, a man could live his whole life paid in cash. Today he cannot. Wages arrive by direct deposit. Rent, utilities, insurance, taxes, and nearly every purchase online require an account and a card. A merchant who cannot process card payments has no business. A ministry that cannot receive online gifts loses most of its support. A man without a bank is not merely inconvenienced; he is shut out of the economy.


When a handful of institutions hold most of the nation’s deposits and run most of its payments, being closed out by them is not one store declining one customer. It is closer to being refused the use of the public road. That is why the question is not merely commercial. It is a moral question about using power over a neighbor.


## The moral test


I judge every policy by three questions, in order. Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? Would I want it done to me?


### Is it righteous?


Scripture speaks directly to unequal standards in commerce. “Thou shalt not have in thy bag divers weights, a great and a small… for all that do such things… are an abomination unto the LORD thy God” (Deuteronomy 25:13–16). “Divers weights, and divers measures, both of them are alike abomination to the LORD” (Proverbs 20:10). A bank that applies one standard to customers whose politics it shares and another to customers whose politics it dislikes carries two sets of weights. The ledger may balance to the penny, and the dealing is still false.


James condemns the same partiality in the assembly: honoring the man in fine clothes and telling the poor man to stand aside (James 2:1–4). The sin is judging a person by a mark unrelated to the matter at hand.


There is also the matter of false witness. Several of these closures rested on a private organization’s label — “hate group” — applied without hearing, evidence, or appeal, and then treated by the bank as established fact. To act on an unproven accusation against a neighbor, and to punish him for it, is to join in the witness. “Thou shalt not raise a false report” (Exodus 23:1).


Finally, the punishment was imposed without any trial. Under our law no man may be fined or deprived of property without due process. Debanking accomplishes a similar loss through private action, often at the urging or under the shadow of government, with no charge, no hearing, and no jury. Where government pressure is the cause, it is government punishing speech by proxy, which the First Amendment forbids it to do directly.


On these grounds, viewpoint-based debanking fails the first question. It is not righteous.


### Is it loving?


Love does not mean approving every view a neighbor holds. A Christian may be grieved by a customer’s opinions and still owe him an honest scale. Love in commerce is mostly plain justice: dealing with him by the same measure you would use for anyone, telling him the truth about why you are acting, and giving him a chance to answer.


The bank employees in the report generally did none of these things. Customers were often closed without a stated reason, and the real reasons appear only now, in documents obtained by a regulator years later. A neighbor who is cut off and told nothing is left to guess, to fear, and to suspect. That is not love; it is contempt delivered quietly.


### Would I want it done to me?


This is the question that keeps the argument honest. If I would be outraged to have my ministry’s account closed for teaching biblical marriage, I must be equally unwilling to see an account closed because its owner campaigns for abortion rights — a position I believe is gravely wrong. The report found that Capital One refused both kinds of customer. Both refusals were wrong for the same reason.


A protection that covers only my side is not a principle; it is a weapon I hope to hold. The tools used against conservatives between 2020 and 2025 — vague “reputation risk” rules, keyword screens, activist blacklists, and quiet regulatory pressure — will be available to whoever holds power next. The only durable defense is a rule that protects every lawful citizen, including the one I would vote against.


## Where the problem comes from


It would be easy to blame “woke bankers” and stop there. The roots go deeper, and a reform that ignores them will fail.


**Government privilege.** Large banks are not ordinary private businesses. They operate under government charters, their deposits are insured by the taxpayer through the FDIC, they borrow from the Federal Reserve, and in a crisis they have been rescued at public expense. An institution that accepts those privileges cannot also claim the full freedom of a private club to exclude whom it pleases. Privilege granted by the public carries obligations to the public.


**Government pressure.** Banks feared regulators. “Reputation risk” was for years a formal part of federal bank examinations, giving examiners a vague, unmeasurable standard by which to pressure banks away from disfavored customers. The OCC has now removed it from its examinations. The earlier federal “Operation Choke Point” (begun in 2013) pressed banks to drop lawful but disfavored industries. When the government cannot lawfully punish a citizen for his speech, it must not do so by leaning on his bank.


**Concentration.** When a few banks hold most deposits and run most payments, one bank’s decision becomes the market’s decision. A man refused by one may find the others using the same screens and the same blacklists.


**Automation.** The WePay screens were software. As artificial intelligence takes over account review, the cost of screening every customer for disfavored words, associations, and donations falls nearly to zero. What once required an employee’s judgment can now be applied to millions of people at once, silently. This is a near-term danger, not a distant one, and it is one reason this site has written about the surveillance state and about AI as a threat to freedom.


## What I propose


These are positions I am taking and will defend, held open to a better argument.


**1. A statutory duty of fair access.** Any bank or payment processor that holds federally insured deposits, uses the Federal Reserve’s payment systems, or holds a federal charter must offer its deposit, payment, and card-processing services to every customer engaged in lawful activity, on the same objective terms it offers anyone else. This should be enacted by Congress, not left to executive order or agency rule, so that it does not swing back and forth with each administration.


**2. The line between viewpoint and risk.** A bank may refuse or close an account for real, measurable financial reasons: fraud, unpaid debt, credit risk, money-laundering or sanctions violations, or a court order. It may never refuse because of a customer’s lawful speech, religious belief or practice, political association, donations, or the views of his customers. “Reputation risk” — the risk that someone will be offended — is not a financial risk and may not be a ground for refusal.


**3. Lending is judged, but by credit, not creed.** A loan is a judgment about repayment, and banks must keep that judgment. But the PNC case shows a credit decision made on the owners’ beliefs rather than the company’s ability to repay. Credit decisions must rest on financial criteria, written in advance and applied to all alike.


*Open question:* may a bank decline to finance a lawful activity it believes immoral — for example, a Christian-owned community bank declining to lend to a casino or an abortion clinic? My present view is that a small institution that takes no federal deposit insurance or charter privilege may hold and publish such a policy openly, in advance, applied to the activity and never to a person’s beliefs. A privileged national bank may not. I hold this view firmly enough to state it, and loosely enough to be persuaded otherwise.


**4. The customer’s right to know and to answer.** Any customer refused or closed must receive a written statement of the specific reason within a set period, with a path to appeal to a person who did not make the original decision. Where a legal reason forbids disclosure (such as a suspicious-activity report under anti-money-laundering law), the bank must certify to its regulator that such a reason exists, and the regulator must audit those certifications.


**5. No outsourced blacklists.** No bank may base a denial on a political or ideological designation from an outside advocacy organization. If a customer is dangerous, the evidence will appear in law enforcement records or court judgments, which are subject to due process. A label from an activist group is not.


**6. A remedy before a jury.** A customer wrongfully debanked should have a private right of action for damages, tried before a jury of his neighbors. Regulators’ fines go to the Treasury; a jury’s award goes to the man who was wronged, and a jury of ordinary citizens is the best judge of whether a bank’s stated reason was honest. This follows from my larger conviction, set out in my paper on the administrative state, that any penalty touching a citizen’s livelihood belongs before a jury.


**7. Protect the alternatives.** A free people should never depend on a single gate. Government must not abolish or restrict the use of cash for lawful purchases, must not create a programmable central-bank digital currency that could be switched off for disfavored citizens, and should welcome competition from credit unions, community banks, and honest private money, including gold-backed and other sound alternatives. Competition is a safeguard that does not depend on the goodwill of any regulator.


**8. Transparency now.** The OCC report names institutions and practices but not the number of people harmed. Those harmed deserve to know. Regulators should require each bank to identify accounts closed or credit denied under the policies the report found, notify those customers, and offer to restore service.


## A word on proportion


This is a serious wrong, and it should be named plainly. It is also important not to overstate it. The report documents policies and specific cases; it does not show a single coordinated plan, and it does not tell us how many people were harmed. Some closures may have had legitimate reasons that the summaries do not capture. The banks have changed some practices. President Trump’s own lawsuit against JPMorgan over his accounts remains an allegation until a court rules. The criminal charges against the Southern Poverty Law Center are charges, not convictions.


None of that weakens the principle. Even one family closed out of the banking system for its faith is a family treated by a false balance. But persuasion should rest on what is proven, and the proven record is sufficient.


## The deeper remedy


Law can restrain the abuse; it cannot make bankers just. The banks did this largely because they feared the press, activists, and regulators more than they feared doing wrong. A nation whose institutions fear man more than God will keep finding new ways to punish dissent. The lasting remedy is a people — bankers among them — who believe they will answer for every unequal weight, and who would rather lose a customer’s fee than wrong a neighbor.


A just law protects the dissenter. A righteous people does not need to be forced to.


## Open questions and editorial notes


### Questions still open


- **Small, mission-driven institutions.** Exactly which institutions should be exempt from the fair-access duty — those with no FDIC insurance and no Federal Reserve access only, or also small community banks below some size?

- **Payment networks and platforms.** Should the same duty reach card networks and payment apps (Visa, Mastercard, PayPal, Stripe) that are not banks but control the gates of commerce? My inclination is yes, for any firm above a size that makes it a practical necessity.

- **The anti-money-laundering problem.** Federal reporting law forbids banks from telling a customer about a suspicious-activity report. That secrecy can hide viewpoint discrimination. How much review is enough without tipping off real criminals?

- **Enforcement without a new bureaucracy.** I prefer the jury and the private suit to a new federal office. Is that enough, or does fair access also require regular regulatory audits?


### Editorial notes


- **OCC report.** *Guaranteeing Fair Banking for All Americans*, released October 14, 2026 (OCC News Release 2026-90). Its review period is 2020 through September 2025. The figure of roughly 100,000 complaints comes from press accounts of the report and should be checked against the report itself.

- **Executive order.** The August 7, 2025 order is Executive Order 14331, “Guaranteeing Fair Access to Banking for All Americans.”

- **Reputation risk.** The OCC has stated it removed reputation risk from its bank examinations; this should be confirmed for the FDIC and Federal Reserve as well before publication.

- **Operation Choke Point.** A Department of Justice initiative begun in 2013 that pressed banks to drop certain lawful but disfavored industries; it was ended in 2017. Details are from general knowledge and should be verified.

- **Pending legislation.** A “Fair Access to Banking Act” has been introduced in recent Congresses. Its current status, and whether a central-bank digital currency ban has been enacted, should be checked before publication.

- **SPLC.** The New York Post reports that the SPLC was indicted in April 2026 on fraud and money-laundering charges. These are allegations; the paper treats them as such.
