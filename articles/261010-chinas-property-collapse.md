# 261010 - China's Property Collapse

# Houses Built on Sand: What China’s Property Collapse Teaches America


*A position paper by Dr. Thomas | 10 October 2026*


---


**Plain Language summary.** For twenty years, Chinese families poured their savings into apartments, many of which were sold before they were built and some of which were never finished. The largest developer, Evergrande, collapsed in 2021 owing more than $300 billion, and in August 2026 its founder was sentenced to life in prison for inflating assets and hiding debts. A popular video about this crisis gets the outline right but exaggerates the details. This paper sets the facts straight, asks what God’s law says about what happened, and draws six lessons for American policy: keep honest books (including the government’s), keep a buyer’s money safe until he receives what he paid for, stop punishing savers, keep government out of the business it referees, hold fraud personally accountable before a jury, and protect the right of the people to protest. It closes with how Americans should regard the Chinese people in their suffering: without gloating.


---


## I. A video, and a question of proportion


A short video has been circulating that shows unfinished Chinese apartment towers collapsing in controlled demolitions, then shows crowds of angry depositors confronting police. The narrator tells us that Beijing is hiding this from the world, that the crisis could be “100 times worse” than America’s Great Recession, that the government met protesters with tanks, and that the whole affair was “just like a pyramid scheme.” He ends with a moral: whatever is founded on lies will crumble, like these buildings.


The moral is true. Much of the rest is overstated. Exaggeration is lying by proportion. A lesson drawn from a distorted picture teaches the wrong thing.


Here is what the record shows:


- **The demolitions are real, but they are not one story.** The most famous footage—fifteen unfinished towers dropped in under a minute—came from Kunming in August 2021, after a local developer ran out of money and another firm bought the project. It was not Evergrande. Separately, at the end of 2021, officials in Danzhou, Hainan, ordered Evergrande to tear down 39 buildings in its Ocean Flower Island resort because the building permits had been obtained illegally.

- **Beijing did not hide it.** The local Chinese press reported the Kunming demolition; Evergrande acknowledged the Hainan order in a stock exchange filing. Chinese censorship is real and serious, but this particular crisis has been reported worldwide for five years.

- **The protests were real and brave.** In the summer of 2022, buyers of unfinished homes announced they would stop paying their mortgages on more than 300 stalled projects in roughly 90 to 100 cities. In Henan province, depositors whose savings had been frozen at several small rural banks since April staged the largest protest of its kind in years; on July 10, 2022, about a thousand of them gathered at the central bank branch in Zhengzhou and were set upon by plainclothes men.

- **The tanks are doubtful, and the banks were a different scandal.** Fact-checkers traced the widely shared video of tanks “guarding banks” to Rizhao, in a different province, where armored vehicles were moving as part of a military exercise. The Henan bank freeze wasn’t caused by Evergrande; it was a fraud in which a private group colluded with bank staff to falsify records and siphon off depositors’ money. The two scandals happened in the same summer, in the same economy, for related reasons — but they are not the same event.

- **“100 times worse than 2008” has no basis I can find.** The truth is bad enough without it. China’s property downturn, which began in 2021, has run for five years. Most private developers have defaulted; state-owned developers now dominate the market; in smaller inland cities, resale prices are down nearly a quarter from 2020. As of August 2026, economists polled by Reuters still expected prices to fall about 3.4% this year and slightly again in 2027. That is not a sudden crash. It is a long, grinding loss of the savings of ordinary families — which is in some ways worse, because it does not end.

- **A Chinese court confirmed the video’s central accusation.** On August 20, 2026, the Shenzhen Intermediate People’s Court sentenced Evergrande’s founder, Hui Ka Yan, to life in prison. The court found that he and the company had committed large-scale financial fraud by inflating the group’s assets and concealing its liabilities. The court also imprisoned his two sons and more than fifty others, and fined the companies about $2.3 billion.


So the video’s outline is right: an industry built on borrowed money and false accounts collapsed, and the people who trusted it paid the price. The details, though, were inflated to make the viewer angry rather than wise. We can do better.


---


## II. What actually happened


Three things combined.


**First, a family’s savings had nowhere else to go.** Chinese households are commonly estimated to hold roughly 60 to 70 percent of their wealth in real estate. That is not because the Chinese are uniquely fond of houses. It is because the state kept bank deposit rates low, restricted moving money abroad, and offered a stock market that ordinary people did not trust. A family that wanted its savings to keep pace with a rising economy had one realistic choice: buy an apartment, and then perhaps a second and a third. Homeownership also carried social weight — in many families, a young man without an apartment was not considered ready to marry. Demand for housing became demand for *a place to store wealth*, which is a different thing from demand for a place to live. That is how whole districts came to be owned but empty.


**Second, buyers paid for homes before they existed.** Under China’s presale system, buyers paid a large down payment and began paying a mortgage on an apartment that was still a hole in the ground. The developer received the money up front. Developers like Evergrande used those funds not only to build the promised apartments but to buy more land, start more projects, and take more presale money — a cycle that worked only as long as new buyers kept arriving. When the government finally capped developer borrowing in 2020, the cycle stopped, and the money owed to suppliers, bondholders, and buyers far exceeded what the companies had. Evergrande alone owed more than $300 billion.


**Third, the referee owned a team.** In China, the state owns all urban land, and for years local governments funded a large share of their budgets by selling land-use rights to developers. Every rise in land prices brought revenue to the very officials charged with regulating the market. They had every reason to keep the boom going and little reason to ask hard questions about the books. When it ended, they had little money left to finish the abandoned projects.


The result is a nation of families still paying mortgages on unfinished homes, worth less than they owe, or both—with their savings tied up in an asset no one wants to buy.


---


## III. The moral test


I have argued on this site that public policy should be judged by an ordered test. **First: is it lawful — is it righteous, as God judges righteousness? Second, and only if so: is it loving — would the neighbor it touches recognize it as love?** Righteousness comes first because love that ignores God’s law becomes sentiment, and sentiment can be bent to excuse anything. Christ’s own love is the calibration: He did not overlook wrong, and He did not stop loving the wrongdoer.


Measured that way, the Chinese property system failed at nearly every point.


**False weights.** *“Ye shall do no unrighteousness in judgment, in meteyard, in weight, or in measure. Just balances, just weights… shall ye have”* **(Leviticus 19:35–36)**. *“A false balance is abomination to the LORD”* **(Proverbs 11:1)**. Inflating assets and hiding liabilities — the crime for which Hui Ka Yan was convicted — is the modern false balance. It is not a technical accounting lapse. It is a lie told to everyone who relies on the books, and it is what made the rest of the collapse possible.


**Taking without delivering.** To take a man’s money for a home and then spend it on something else, leaving him to pay for a building that will never stand, is theft dressed up as finance. The eighth commandment has no exemption for complex transactions.


**Joining house to house.** *“Woe unto them that join house to house, that lay field to field, till there be no place, that they may be placed alone in the midst of the earth!”* **(Isaiah 5:8)**. The prophet was describing the rich who accumulate dwellings not to live in them but to hold them. Whole Chinese neighborhoods of owned-but-empty apartments are a strange modern fulfillment: not one great landlord, but millions of families driven by policy to treat shelter as a savings account.


**Rulers who profit from the snare.** When the government that sets the rules is paid by the outcome it should restrain, justice is compromised at its root. *“Woe unto them that decree unrighteous decrees”* **(Isaiah 10:1)**. Evergrande’s executives deserved their judgment. But the officials who held deposit rates down, closed the exits, and fed their budgets on land sales built the house in which the fraud lived — and they have not been judged.


**The house on the sand.** Our Lord described two builders. One built on rock, and his house stood through the storm. The other built on sand, *“and great was the fall of it”* **(Matthew 7:27)**. The video’s closing line reaches for this truth, and it is the right one. A financial system built on the assumption that prices only rise, that new buyers will always come, and that no one will ever examine the books is a house on sand. The towers falling in Kunming are only the most visible part of it.


Only after answering the first question do we ask the second. Would the Chinese family paying a mortgage on an unfinished tower in Zhengzhou feel loved by this system? The question answers itself.


---


## IV. The mirror


It would be easy, and wrong, to stop there. Americans watching this video are invited to feel that China is “losing its ground” — that its trouble is our gain. Before we accept that invitation, we should remember 2008.


Our own housing crash was also built on false weights: loans written without verifying income, mortgages bundled into securities and rated as safe when they were not, and an implicit government guarantee behind the largest mortgage buyers that encouraged everyone to lend more than prudence allowed. When it fell, millions of American families lost their homes, and very few of the people who designed the system answered for it personally. China, whatever else one says about its courts, has now imprisoned the man at the center of its collapse. We did not imprison ours.


And we have our own versions of the three conditions described above. Savers who earn less than inflation are pushed toward riskier assets to keep up. Governments at every level depend on rising property values for revenue. And the federal government reports its finances in a way that leaves trillions in promised future obligations off the main ledger. The circumstances differ. The temptation is the same.


So the right response to China’s crisis is not gloating. It is self-examination.


---


## V. Six principles for American policy


I offer these as positions, with my reasons. If someone has a better argument, I want to hear it.


**1. Honest books, beginning with the government’s.** *Righteous ground:* the just weight **(Leviticus 19:35–36; Proverbs 11:1)**. Any institution that takes the public’s money — a corporation selling stock, a bank taking deposits, or a government taxing and borrowing — must report what it owes as plainly as what it owns. American law already demands this of corporations, and it should be enforced without favoritism. For the federal government, it means putting the full cost of promises already made — including the future obligations of Social Security and Medicare — on the face of the national balance sheet, where citizens can see it, rather than in footnotes few will read. A people cannot govern itself honestly with dishonest numbers.


**2. A buyer’s money stays his until he receives what he paid for.** *Righteous ground:* do not take what is not yet earned; do not steal. When an American pays in advance for something not yet delivered — a home under construction, a deposit on a condominium, a prepaid contract — that money should be held in protected escrow, out of reach of the seller’s other creditors, until the thing is delivered. Many states already require this for certain property sales; the principle should be uniform and should survive the seller’s bankruptcy. The Chinese presale system shows what happens when it is not: the buyer becomes an unsecured lender to a company he never chose to finance.


**3. Stop punishing savers.** *Righteous ground:* the just weight again — the dollar is a measure, and a measure that shrinks is a false one. When the government holds interest rates below inflation for years, it quietly transfers wealth from savers to borrowers and drives ordinary people into speculation just to stand still. That is what herded Chinese families into their fourth apartment. An honest dollar and an honest return on savings are not technical preferences. They are how a nation lets a careful man stay careful.


**4. The referee must not own a team.** *Righteous ground:* *“Thou shalt not wrest judgment… neither take a gift”* **(Deuteronomy 16:19)**. Government should not be financially dependent on the markets it regulates. That means no implicit federal guarantees backing private lenders’ risks, and a hard look at every budget—federal, state, or local—that quietly depends on asset prices continuing to rise. Where a public body profits from a boom, it will not restrain it.


**5. Fraud answered personally, before a jury of citizens.** *Righteous ground:* *“Because sentence against an evil work is not executed speedily, therefore the heart of the sons of men is fully set in them to do evil”* **(Ecclesiastes 8:11)**. When a corporation pays fines, its shareholders and employees pay them, not the men who lied. When executives knowingly falsify the books that the public relies on, they should answer as individuals. And they should answer before a jury of ordinary citizens, not before a court that serves the ruling party, as in China. The jury is the people’s own conscience in the courtroom, and I regard it — including its power to refuse an unjust law — as a cornerstone of a free society.


**6. Protect the right of the people to protest.** *Righteous ground:* the dignity of every person made in God’s image, and rulers’ duty to hear the cry of the wronged. *“Whoso stoppeth his ears at the cry of the poor, he also shall cry himself, but shall not be heard.” ***(Proverbs 21:13)**. The bravest moment in this whole story is the one the video rightly highlights: ordinary Chinese depositors and homebuyers standing in the street and saying, in effect, “Give us back what is ours.” In China, that took real courage and drew intimidation. In America, the First Amendment guarantees the right “to petition the Government for a redress of grievances.” It is a mechanism of accountability, not a nuisance, and we must protect it for causes we sympathize with and those we do not.


---


## VI. How to regard China’s suffering


*“Rejoice not when thine enemy falleth, and let not thine heart be glad when he stumbleth”*** (Proverbs 24:17)**. The Chinese government is a rival to the United States and, in its treatment of believers and dissenters, an oppressor of its own people. We should be clear-eyed about that. But the families paying mortgages on unfinished towers are not our enemies. They are neighbors who did what their system rewarded and were betrayed by it.


The righteous response has three parts. Compassion for the Chinese people, including prayer for them and for the persecuted church among them. Clarity about the system that wronged them — which is a system of state control, false accounting, and suppressed dissent. And humility about our own house, because the same sand lies under every nation that tells itself prices only rise, debts never come due, and no one will ever check the books.


Build on the rock. That is the whole lesson, and it applies to Washington as much as to Beijing.
