# 261111 - Frederick Bastiat - The Law

# Bastiat's *The Law*:


### When the Shield Becomes the Sword

Thomas Lee Abshier, ND · 11 November 2026

## A Pamphlet Written Against the Clock

In June 1850, Frédéric Bastiat sat in his family home at Mugron, in southern France, and wrote an eighty-page pamphlet called *La Loi*: *The Law*. He was forty-nine years old, a deputy in the National Assembly, and dying of tuberculosis. He would be dead in Rome by Christmas Eve of that same year. France had just passed through the Revolution of 1848, and Paris was full of plans to reorganize society by legislation: guaranteed work, state credit, public workshops, protective tariffs, and taxes designed to equalize fortunes.

Bastiat saw a single error in all of it, and he spent some of his last strength naming it. The error was this: men had forgotten what law is *for*. Law had been given to us as a shield to protect each person's life, liberty, and property. It was being turned into a sword, used by some to take what belongs to others. The prophet Isaiah saw the same perversion in his own day and pronounced judgment on it: *"Woe unto them that decree unrighteous decrees, and that write grievousness which they have prescribed; To turn aside the needy from judgment, and to take away the right from the poor of my people, that widows may be their prey, and that they may rob the fatherless!"* **(Isaiah 10:1-2)** The robbery Isaiah condemned was not done in the dark. It was written into decrees.

I stand with Bastiat. I believe his central argument is true, not merely as economics, but as morality, and ultimately as an expression of the command not to steal. In this essay, I will walk through his argument, explain why I think it holds, and show where I think it must be carried further than he did.

## The Foundation: Rights Come Before Law

Bastiat begins not with government but with the human person. Each of us receives from God three gifts that make up our earthly existence: life itself, the faculties that sustain it (our liberty to think, choose, and act), and the fruit of those faculties (our property). These gifts precede every legislature. In his words, *life, liberty, and property do not exist because men have made laws.* The other way around: because these things already existed and needed defending, men made laws.

Scripture pictures the good society in exactly these terms, as a people secure in the fruit of their own labor: *"But they shall sit every man under his vine and under his fig tree; and none shall make them afraid: for the mouth of the LORD of hosts hath spoken it."* **(Micah 4:4)** The vine and fig tree are a man's property; sitting under them in peace is his liberty; and the promise that none shall make him afraid is the protection of his life. That is Bastiat's program in a single verse.

From this starting point, Bastiat derives the purpose of law with almost mathematical simplicity. Every person has a natural right to defend his life, liberty, and property, even by force. Since each individual has that right, a group of individuals may combine their rights and organize a common force to do the same work collectively. That common force is law. Law, therefore, is *the collective organization of the individual right to lawful defense.*

Paul describes civil authority in the same defensive role. The magistrate carries a sword, but its edge is turned against the wrongdoer, not the innocent: *"For rulers are not a terror to good works, but to the evil... For he is the minister of God to thee for good. But if thou do that which is evil, be afraid; for he beareth not the sword in vain: for he is the minister of God, a revenger to execute wrath upon him that doeth evil."* **(Romans 13:3-4)** The sword of the state is lawful only as long as it is pointed at evil.

The consequence follows at once. A collective force can have no legitimate power that its individual members lacked. If I have no right to take my neighbor's wages to pay for my projects, I cannot delegate that right to a committee, a majority, or a government. The group's power is borrowed from the rights of its members, and a man cannot lend what he does not own. Law that stays inside this boundary produces justice. Law that steps outside it, using organized force to do what no individual could rightly do, stops being law in the true sense and becomes its opposite.

## Legal Plunder: Theft With a Seal on It

Bastiat's most famous contribution is a phrase: *legal plunder*. Plunder is the taking of what another person has produced, without his consent and without giving him equal value in return. Thieves commit ordinary plunder, and the law punishes it. Legal plunder is the same act carried out *through* the law, so that the law itself becomes the thief and punishes the victim who resists. The Psalmist asked whether God could ever be a partner to such a government: *"Shall the throne of iniquity have fellowship with thee, which frameth mischief by a law?"* **(Psalm 94:20)** Mischief framed by a law is as exact a definition of legal plunder as Bastiat ever wrote.

He gives a simple test for recognizing it. Look at a law and ask two questions:


 	- Does it take from some people what belongs to them and give it to others to whom it does not belong?

 	- Does it benefit one citizen at another's expense by doing something the beneficiary could not do himself without committing a crime?


If the answer is yes, the law is plunder, whatever its name and however noble its stated aim. Bastiat insists that such a law be repealed promptly, because one such law, left standing, invites the next. Every group that is plundered learns to seek its own share in return.

The Law of Moses set the same standard of impartiality. It forbade the judge to tilt the scale in either direction, toward the powerful or toward the poor: *"Ye shall do no unrighteousness in judgment: thou shalt not respect the person of the poor, nor honour the person of the mighty: but in righteousness shalt thou judge thy neighbour."* **(Leviticus 19:15)** Law that takes sides between classes, whichever side it takes, has stopped judging and started plundering.

He names the forms. Protective tariffs force consumers to pay more so that favored producers may profit. Subsidies, guaranteed profits, state credit, and the "right to work" at public expense all move wealth by force from those who produced it to those who did not. He even turns his test on the United States, which he otherwise admired: he identifies its slavery and its tariffs as the two places where American law had become an instrument of plunder. Slavery plundered a man's very person and labor; the tariff plundered his earnings. On the first, Scripture had already ruled with the utmost severity: *"And he that stealeth a man, and selleth him, or if he be found in his hand, he shall surely be put to death."* **(Exodus 21:16)**

Why does a nation fall into this? Bastiat finds two motives. The first is *greed*, the ancient human preference for living at others' expense rather than by one's own work, now made safe and respectable by passing through a legislature. Jesus warned against it directly: *"Take heed, and beware of covetousness: for a man's life consisteth not in the abundance of the things which he possesseth."* **(Luke 12:15)** The second is what he calls *false philanthropy*: the sincere wish to do good, combined with the belief that good may be done by compelling it. The greedy man and the philanthropist arrive at the same law by different roads, and the philanthropist is the more dangerous, because he feels no guilt.

## What Plunder Does to a People

I believe Bastiat's insight is moral, not economic. When law and morality point in opposite directions, the citizen is forced into a cruel choice: he must either lose his moral sense or lose his respect for the law. Most people assume that whatever is legal is also right. So when the law commands or rewards plunder, it slowly teaches a whole nation that taking from a neighbor through the ballot box is not theft. Conscience is retrained by statute. A people who would never pick a pocket learn to vote for someone else to pick it on their behalf, and feel virtuous doing so. Isaiah named this inversion and the judgment that rests on it: *"Woe unto them that call evil good, and good evil; that put darkness for light, and light for darkness; that put bitter for sweet, and sweet for bitter!"* **(Isaiah 5:20)**

The second consequence is that politics swells to monstrous importance. If the law only protected rights, it would matter little who made it; every faction would want the same impartial justice. But once the law becomes the means of distributing other people's wealth, control of the law becomes the great prize. Every group must organize, lobby, and fight, either to win plunder or to defend itself from being plundered. Bastiat saw that the bitter struggles over suffrage and party in his own France flowed from exactly this. When the stakes of lawmaking are everyone's livelihood, elections become wars by other means. James traced such wars to their root long before: *"From whence come wars and fightings among you? come they not hence, even of your lusts that war in your members? Ye lust, and have not: ye kill, and desire to have, and cannot obtain: ye fight and war."* **(James 4:1-2)**

The third consequence follows from the second. Because everyone now has a claim on the law, the law becomes an endless contest of claims, and the citizen who simply wants to be left alone to work and give freely becomes the one person with no lobby. Bastiat observed that plunder tends to become *universal*: first a few plunder the many, then the many organize to plunder in return, until every class is living at the expense of every other, and all are poorer for it.

Here I add a point Bastiat implies but does not develop. The citizen caught between conscience and statute is not without recourse. When the rulers of Jerusalem commanded the apostles to stop preaching, Peter gave the answer that every free conscience must keep in reserve: *"We ought to obey God rather than men."* **(Acts 5:29)** In the Anglo-American tradition, the jury stands as the last place where the people's conscience can refuse to enforce an unjust law against a neighbor. I regard jury nullification as a cornerstone of a free society for exactly the reason Bastiat gives: when law and morality divide, someone must be allowed to side with morality without becoming an outlaw.

## The Organizers: Mankind as Clay

The longest part of *The Law* is an extended answer to the philosophers and reformers who stood behind the socialist programs of 1848. Bastiat quotes them at length, from Rousseau, Mably, Condillac, and Raynal to Robespierre, Saint-Just, and the reformers of his own day, and he finds in all of them the same picture of humanity. In their writings, the people are passive material, and the legislator is the artist who shapes it. Mankind is clay; the lawgiver is the potter.

Scripture uses the same image, but it gives the potter's wheel to only one Hand: *"But now, O LORD, thou art our father; we are the clay, and thou our potter; and we all are the work of thy hand."* **(Isaiah 64:8)** The organizers did not invent a new picture of humanity. They took God's place in an old one.

Bastiat turns their picture on its head with a question that still has no good answer. If human nature is so corrupt and incapable that a few wise men must mold it, where do those wise men come from? Are they not also human? If people cannot be trusted to govern their own lives, how can some of them be trusted to govern everyone else's? The organizers claim for themselves a wisdom and virtue they deny to the rest of the race. Their programs require the governed to be fools and the governors to be gods. Scripture allows no such exemption: *"For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God."* **(Romans 3:23)** And it warns against the trust the organizers demand: *"Put not your trust in princes, nor in the son of man, in whom there is no help."* **(Psalm 146:3)**

He also exposes a confusion that runs through their reasoning. Because a person opposes the state doing something, the organizers say he opposes the thing itself. If I oppose state religion, I am said to want no religion. If I oppose state-run education, they say I oppose education. If I oppose forced equalization of wealth, they say I am indifferent to the poor. Bastiat replies that he objects only to the *compulsion*, not to the good being sought. Religion, learning, and charity flourish best when they are free.

This is the heart of the matter. The organizers' error is not that they want good things. It is that they believe goodness can be manufactured by force, and that they are qualified to decide for everyone what good is.

## Liberty and the Harmony of Providence

What does Bastiat offer in place of the organizers' grand designs? Not another system, but the absence of one. He believes that the Creator built human beings with everything needed for their own development: reason, conscience, the capacity to work, and the desire to exchange freely with one another. When these faculties are left free and protected from violence and fraud, they tend toward harmony. Free people, trading, cooperating, giving, and learning from one another, produce a social order more just and more prosperous than any planner could design.

This is why Bastiat's economics cannot be separated from his faith. He trusted that the order God placed in human nature is good, and that the business of law is to guard it, not to replace it. The Creator's own verdict on His work stands behind that trust: *"And God saw every thing that he had made, and, behold, it was very good."* **(Genesis 1:31)** Bastiat's closing appeal is in effect a prayer: that those who have tried so many schemes of compulsion would finally try liberty, which is itself an act of faith in God and in His work. *"Now the Lord is that Spirit: and where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is liberty."* **(2 Corinthians 3:17)**

Liberty in Scripture is never license, and Bastiat did not mean it as license. Paul joins freedom and service in a single sentence: *"For, brethren, ye have been called unto liberty; only use not liberty for an occasion to the flesh, but by love serve one another."* **(Galatians 5:13)** The free society is not one in which no one cares for his neighbor. It is one in which every act of care is a free gift of love rather than a levy collected under threat.

The law Bastiat envisions is therefore modest. It is negative rather than positive in its work: it prevents injustice rather than manufacturing virtue. It keeps the peace so that people may do good freely. It does not ask what kind of society the lawgiver would like to build. It asks only whether anyone's life, liberty, or property has been violated, and if so, it restores justice. Such a law is simple, understandable, and stable, because it rests on a principle every person already knows in his own conscience.

## Where I Stand, and Why

I stand with Bastiat's central thesis: the law exists to protect each person's life, liberty, and property, and a law that uses force to take from one and give to another is plunder, whatever name it wears. I hold this first on moral grounds, and only secondarily on economic ones.

The moral ground predates Bastiat. The commandment is four words long: *"Thou shalt not steal."* **(Exodus 20:15)** It contains no exception for majorities. When Israel demanded a king like the other nations, Samuel warned them that the king's law would become exactly what Bastiat described: *"And he will take your fields, and your vineyards, and your oliveyards, even the best of them, and give them to his servants."* **(1 Samuel 8:14)** That is legal plunder, described three thousand years before Bastiat named it.

The test I apply to every act of law is this: would my neighbor feel loved by it? Paul gives the same measure: *"Love worketh no ill to his neighbour: therefore love is the fulfilling of the law."* **(Romans 13:10)** Protection of my neighbor's life, his freedom, and the work of his hands passes that test. Taking his earnings by force to fund my preferences, however worthy, does not. I believe God lives inside every person's experience. He feels what my neighbor feels. When the law reaches into one man's pocket for another man's benefit, God experiences that taking from the inside, in the one plundered. Love cannot be compelled; it can only be given. Paul understood this when he wrote that each should give *"as he purposeth in his heart, so let him give; not grudgingly, or of necessity: for God loveth a cheerful giver."* **(2 Corinthians 9:7)** Compelled generosity is not generosity. It is plunder told to be charity.

And now to answer the strongest objections:

**"This would abolish all taxation."** It would not, and Bastiat never claimed it would. Scripture itself acknowledges that the magistrate who guards the peace is owed his support: *"Render therefore to all their dues: tribute to whom tribute is due; custom to whom custom; fear to whom fear; honour to whom honour."* **(Romans 13:7)** A tax that funds the common defense of everyone's rights (courts, police, national defense) is each citizen paying his share of a service that protects him. It does not take from one to enrich another. The line is not between taxing and not taxing, but between protecting and redistributing. Drawing that line in practice is sometimes difficult. But a line that is difficult to see is still a line. Such are the boundaries of moral maturity that legislatures consider and courts adjudicate.

**"The poor would be abandoned."** This objection carries the most weight, and Scripture demands that we take it seriously: *"For the poor shall never cease out of the land: therefore I command thee, saying, Thou shalt open thine hand wide unto thy brother, to thy poor, and to thy needy, in thy land."* **(Deuteronomy 15:11)** The Law of Moses required landowners to leave the corners of their fields and the gleanings for the poor: *"And when ye reap the harvest of your land, thou shalt not wholly reap the corners of thy field, neither shalt thou gather the gleanings of thy harvest... thou shalt leave them for the poor and stranger: I am the LORD your God."* **(Leviticus 19:9-10)** But notice its form: the owner left the margin; the poor came and worked to gather it, as Ruth did in the field of Boaz. No royal treasury collected and redistributed it, and the tithe for the poor was answerable to God, not enforced by the king's sword. Even in the early church, where believers shared everything, the sharing was free. Peter told Ananias that his land had been his own to keep: *"Whiles it remained, was it not thine own? and after it was sold, was it not in thine own power?"* **(Acts 5:4)** Ananias was judged for lying, not for keeping. The obligation to care for the poor is real and binding on every one of us. Bastiat's point is that it binds our conscience, and when the state takes it over, it both weakens charity and turns the recipient into a claimant rather than a neighbor.

**"Bastiat's free order is too optimistic about human nature."** I take this seriously as well. *"The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked: who can know it?"* **(Jeremiah 17:9)** Free people still sin. Markets still contain fraud, and the strong still prey on the weak. But Bastiat's law does not ignore this; it punishes fraud and violence. What it refuses is the conclusion that because men are sinful, a few sinful men should be given power over everyone else's life. The deceitful heart does not become honest when it takes office. That remedy multiplies the disease.

## Why the Concern Remains Urgent

Bastiat's warning is not an antique. The mechanisms of plunder have grown more sophisticated since 1850. Currency inflation quietly transfers wealth from savers to debtors and to those nearest the new money. Scripture condemns the dishonest measure in every form: *"Divers weights, and divers measures, both of them are alike abomination to the LORD."* **(Proverbs 20:10)** Subsidies and regulatory favors are written by the industries that benefit. Entire classes of citizens have been taught to regard the public treasury as a source of income rather than a common trust.

The intuition many people feel that something is unnatural about a system in which the most profitable activity is lobbying is exactly the intuition Bastiat put into words. The money motive is real: wherever law can hand out wealth, organized interests will spend heavily to direct it. *"And thou shalt take no gift: for the gift blindeth the wise, and perverteth the words of the righteous."* **(Exodus 23:8)** Isaiah saw debased money and bought rulers as one sickness: *"Thy silver is become dross, thy wine mixed with water: Thy princes are rebellious, and companions of thieves: every one loveth gifts, and followeth after rewards: they judge not the fatherless, neither doth the cause of the widow come unto them."* **(Isaiah 1:22-23)** And the link between doctrine and behavior is visible. A people taught that the state is the source of rights will look to the state for every good thing, and will feel no shame in using it to take.

## Conclusion: Returning the Sword to Its Sheath

*The Law* is short enough to read in an afternoon, and Bastiat wrote it knowing he had little time left. Its argument is simple: rights come from God, not from government; law is the shared defense of those rights; and law that takes from some to give to others has betrayed its purpose and become plunder. From that simple argument flow consequences that every generation has had to relearn: the corruption of conscience, the inflation of politics into a war over spoils, and the arrogance of those who would mold mankind like clay.

I believe Bastiat's cure is still the right one. Not a better plan imposed by better men, but a return of the law to its proper work: protecting each person so that he may live, choose, work, and give freely. That kind of law is an act of love toward the neighbor, because it treats him as a person made in God's image and not as material for someone else's design. It asks of the state only what God asks of every man: *"He hath shewed thee, O man, what is good; and what doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?"* **(Micah 6:8)** Justice is the law's work; mercy is ours, freely given. I hold this position open to a better argument. After a hundred and seventy-five years, Bastiat's central question remains: by what right does a group take what no member of it could rightly take alone?
