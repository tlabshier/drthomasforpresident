# Whose Law? A Dialogue on Conscience, the Jury, and the Nation's Heart

*A position paper in the form of a conversation. The Questioner is not a straw man; he is the best case the other side makes, and several of his corrections are simply right. The Candidate takes a position and holds it open to a better argument.*

---

**Questioner:** You say jury nullification should be the cornerstone of the legal system. Most lawyers would call it a loophole at best and a menace at worst. Why put it at the foundation?

**Thomas:** Because it is the last place where a citizen can say to the state, "This law, applied to this man, is unjust, and I will not be the instrument of it." Every other check on government runs through government: courts review legislatures, legislatures fund courts, the executive appoints both. The jury is the one place a neighbor judges a neighbor, and the acquittal cannot be appealed. If the law is wrong, or right in general but wrong in this case, the jury is the final safeguard against a man being crushed by it.

**Questioner:** Then let me put the hard history to you first, because it's the first thing anyone will raise. Nullification acquitted John Peter Zenger and shielded people who defied the Fugitive Slave Act. It also let all-white juries acquit lynchers for the better part of a century. If the community's conscience is the last word, it is the last word when the community's conscience is wicked.

**Thomas:** I accept the history entirely. What I deny is that it settles the question. When a jury nullifies unjustly, it does something no court ruling does: it drags the heart of that community into daylight. The acquittal of a lyncher is a confession, written in the public record, that the county holds a neighbor's life cheap. That confession is horrifying, and it is also the only honest diagnosis available. A society that will not look at what its juries do cannot repent of it.

**Questioner:** The victim pays for that diagnosis. The lynched man's family does not get the benefit of the boil being lanced.

**Thomas:** No, and the paper that argues my position must say so plainly rather than let the metaphor carry the cost in silence. The injustice is real, it is borne by the innocent, and I will not pretend the exposure compensates the family. What I say is that the alternative did not spare the victims either. It only hid the confession.

**Questioner:** That's where I'd stop you. The alternative is not hypothetical. Those acquittals were not ended by sunlight; they went on for decades. They were ended when federal prosecutors took civil-rights cases away from local juries. The cure for bad nullification, on the historical record, was less nullification.

**Thomas:** Look more carefully at what the federal jurisdiction was. It was a wider community's conscience overruling a narrower one: the nation's jury, in effect, sitting in judgment on the county's. That is my principle operating at a larger scale, not its refutation. The nation said: your county cannot be trusted with self-government in this matter, so for now the judgment passes upward. I agree with that. Abuse of a privilege should be met with discipline. My objection is to what came after.

**Questioner:** Which was?

**Thomas:** That the discipline was made permanent and universal. One region's juries proved unfit, and the response was to treat every jury in the country as unfit forever. That is not correction; it is infantilization. A father who takes the car keys from a son who drove drunk is right. A father who never returns them, to any of his children, is no longer disciplining. He has decided his children are serfs.

**Questioner:** Here I have a factual correction that, oddly, helps you. Nullification was never eliminated. An acquittal is still unreviewable; no juror can be punished for one. What happened is narrower. In *Sparf v. United States* (1895) the Supreme Court held that judges need not tell juries they hold the power, and the courts later allowed a juror who announces he will exercise it to be struck for cause. The power survives. The right to be told about it was removed, and a juror's honesty about holding it gets him dismissed.

**Thomas:** Then the accurate sentence is sharper than the one I've been using. A nation in which a man may nullify only if he conceals that he would is a nation being trained to lie during jury selection. I have friends who told the truth when asked and were sent home for it. The lesson they received was not "you may not do this"; it was "you may not admit it." That is a worse lesson for the character of a people than a plain prohibition would be.

**Questioner:** So restore the instruction and stop striking jurors for candor. That's a modest reform, and I'd support it. But you want more. You've said a jury should be composed of men who hold the Bible's moral standard as their own, and that a prospective juror should be asked whether he does.

**Thomas:** Yes. A jury of one's peers has never meant a jury of one's fellow sociopaths. The phrase comes from Magna Carta and it meant one's equals in standing, and the Founders, steeped in the Christian moral vocabulary, meant men of ordinary moral competence. A thief did not expect to be judged by thieves. If we assemble juries with no regard to whether the jurors can tell right from wrong, we should not be surprised when they cannot.

**Questioner:** Then here is the seam in the argument, and it runs straight through your own hard history. The jurors who acquitted lynchers would have answered your question yes. Nearly all of them were professing, church-going Christians. Many understood their verdicts as defending a Christian social order. Your screen would not have removed one of them. The problem in those courtrooms was not the absence of the Bible as a standard; it was a people who held the Bible and read it to permit contempt for their neighbor.

**Thomas:** That is true, and it is the most important objection in this conversation, so I will not answer it quickly. Profession is not discernment. Christ said the same: "By their fruits ye shall know them," and "Not every one that saith unto me, Lord, Lord." A question in jury selection can examine profession. It cannot examine fruit, and only God examines the heart.

**Questioner:** Then the question is useless.

**Thomas:** The question is insufficient. It is not useless, because a man who refuses the standard outright has told you something. But you are right that it cannot do the work I want done, and admitting that moves the center of the argument. If the jury box cannot be made righteous by a qualifying question, then the work is not in the jury box. It is in the formation of the men who fill it, and that is not the state's work. It is the work of fathers, churches, teachers, and neighbors. The jury is downstream of the nation's heart. Change the heart and the jury follows; leave the heart and no screen will save the jury.

**Questioner:** I notice that is a very different paper from "put the Bible test in the courthouse."

**Thomas:** It is the paper I actually mean. The jury is the illustration. The subject is whether a nation can have justice without righteous men, and it cannot.

---

**Questioner:** Let's take up the larger claim behind it. You say law always imposes a morality. Most people in public life would say the state should be neutral among moralities and simply keep the peace.

**Thomas:** Neutrality is a morality that hides its name. Every anti-discrimination statute is a moral judgment enforced by the sword: it declares certain distinctions wicked and punishes those who make them. The Federal Register runs to tens of thousands of pages of behaviors the state has decided are wrong enough to fine or jail a man for, most of which he has never heard of. Hate-crime law goes further and punishes a man's motive. None of this is neutral. It is a creed, codified.

**Questioner:** Two qualifications. Criminal law has always weighed motive; premeditation and malice are motive. So "judging a man's heart" is not new. What is new, and what you can fairly object to, is that the state selected which motives count, and that selection is a moral choice. And on the Federal Register, you have unexpected company: the civil-liberties left has complained about overcriminalization for years. That is a bridge, not a wedge.

**Thomas:** I accept both. The point stands with the qualifications: "the state must not legislate morality" is never on the table. The only live question is whose morality, and whether the people can see it being chosen.

**Questioner:** Then answer it. Whose?

**Thomas:** The God of the Bible's. I do not say that as a slogan. I say it because every man of age carries a reflexive sense of attraction and revulsion, and that sense is the pattern of the Creator written in him, however dimmed. Moral principle is not established by edict; it is known in the heart. But a heart can be trained wrong, and a nation can call good evil and evil good. When it does, the nation answers for it.

**Questioner:** Your opponents claim the same inner compass. They feel revulsion at your positions as strongly as you feel it at theirs.

**Thomas:** They do, and that is why feeling is never the proof. A man's feelings are the indicator of his own state, not the demonstration of eternal truth. My positions must be justified by triangulation: the Bible as the cornerstone, the self-consistency of the position with that cornerstone, and the fit of its internal logic to the circumstances. Others may argue their vision in the public square. I disclose my allegiance rather than hiding it, and I argue for the way of the Lord as a man who has weighed the alternatives and, until convinced by a better argument, offers this as the best he has.

**Questioner:** That last sentence is the one I'd hold you to, because it is what separates you from the theocrat. Every theocracy in history, including the lynching South, believed its numbers and guns lined up with God's law. What check do you propose for the case where the Godly majority is wrong?

**Thomas:** The same one I began with. The open exposure of what a people does in its juries and its laws, and the humility to read it. I have said I cannot claim to speak for the Lord in every position; I can say I am trying to. That admission is not a grace note; it is structural. A leader who cannot be wrong cannot be corrected, and the Lord corrects nations through their own history when they will not listen to their prophets. He has been known to install an evil king to teach a people what they would not learn otherwise.

**Questioner:** On a candidacy website every reader will ask which king you mean.

**Thomas:** Let them ask. I mean the pattern, and the pattern applies to whoever fits it, of any party.

---

**Questioner:** Romans 13. You've cited it as the government's warrant to enforce Godliness.

**Thomas:** Paul says the ruler is God's servant, bearing the sword against the wrongdoer.

**Questioner:** Written under Nero, about a pagan state. It authorizes government to punish evil. It does not say government enforces the whole of Godliness, and the church that had the passage in hand spent centuries disputing how far it reached. Aquinas argued that human law should not attempt to prohibit every vice, because the attempt produces worse evils. The Baptists who pushed hardest for disestablishment did so on Christian grounds, having been jailed by Christian magistrates.

**Thomas:** I have never argued for prosecuting all vice, and I agree with Aquinas. My claim is narrower and I'll state it precisely. Government should punish the sins that make victims: rape, the abuse of children, fraud, violence. And government should not use its sword to compel a man to participate in what he believes is sin. A baker should not be forced to make the cake; a photographer should not be forced to shoot the wedding. When the state punishes him for declining, it is prosecuting a social sin, the sin of not conforming to the creed that love is love. That is a religion enforced by law.

**Questioner:** There the law has partly come your way. *Masterpiece Cakeshop* (2018) and *303 Creative* (2023) held that the state cannot compel expressive work. The unsettled ground is non-expressive service, a room, a meal, a seat, where public-accommodation law still binds. And note that the same statutes protect the Christian from being refused a room or a meal.

**Thomas:** I take the win and I accept the boundary. Expressive participation is where conscience lives; a meal is not a sermon. And I'll go further, because it follows from what I said about the jury: the state should judge a man by his fruits, not his beliefs. A man's doctrine of baptism, his view of the rapture, his Sabbath, his opinion of the demonic, none of it should be given the force of law. The intra-Christian imposition of doctrine is as much a symptom of immaturity as anything the secularists do. What the state may regulate is action, and speech only to the extent that it is action in embryo: a threat is a deed at the stage of conception, and may be stopped there.

**Questioner:** That is the true-threats doctrine and it is very nearly the harm principle. I'd point out that it protects the people you oppose exactly as it protects you. A man advocating his lifestyle is not threatening anyone.

**Thomas:** Then under my rule he is left alone, and I have said so. My quarrel is not with his existence; it is with compelled participation and compelled speech, with schools inducting children into his creed without their parents' knowledge, and with a public square in which the man who names what the Bible calls sin is the one who pays.

---

**Questioner:** Let's stay on that, since you've said it is the third rail. You've argued that the fury which greets any criticism of the sexual revolution is itself evidence of a pathology protecting itself.

**Thomas:** Evidence of its force, not of its falsehood or truth. A colony that has specialized into defense as well as offense is a mature colony. I make no proof from the reaction. I note the entrenchment as a measure of how much ground has been taken.

**Questioner:** Good, because the alternative reading is self-sealing: any pushback confirms the diagnosis, and your opponents run the identical move against you. On the substance, then: what does a candidate owe the man who experiences same-sex attraction, or the man who feels himself to be a woman?

**Thomas:** Compassion, first and without qualification. He is carrying a burden he did not choose. Whether the cause is biological miswiring, environmental chemistry, wounds of the heart, or something spiritual, he suffers, and the Christian's first duty to a suffering neighbor is comfort. We do not exalt a deformity, but we do not despise the man who bears it; we help him live the best life he can in the body God gave him. "Male and female He created them" is, to me, a statement of intent, and the reasonable prescription is to live within what one's anatomy declares. That may be a heavy cross. Many crosses are heavy.

**Questioner:** You've elsewhere used the word "delusion." Clinically it means a fixed false belief about external reality held against evidence, and it does not describe gender dysphoria; the person typically knows his biological sex perfectly well.

**Thomas:** I used it in the cowboy sense, not the clinical one: a strong conviction at odds with the facts of his body. I'll take the correction, because imprecision hands the other side an easy win and I want to be understood. The point is that a man in that condition needs help, not reassurance that the condition is his true self. If you are sick, you treat the sickness; you do not redecorate the room around it.

**Questioner:** And society?

**Thomas:** Society should not be reorganized around the outlier. Rare chromosomal conditions should be handled case by case, with compassion. National policy should not be built on them. The women's sports question is the plainest example: a fairness and safety question that needs no theology to answer, and one on which the ordinary voter already agrees with me. And children. Schools that encourage a child to question his sex, and that socially transition him without telling his parents, are inducting him into a creed in secret. I'll not call that grooming, because the word will be heard as an accusation of predation and I mean something else. I mean ideological induction of a minor behind his parents' backs, and that is sufficient to condemn it.

**Questioner:** On children you have the strongest evidence-based ground of the whole topic. Historically, most childhood gender dysphoria resolved by adulthood under watchful waiting, which is exactly why the European reviews pulled back from early medical transition.

**Thomas:** Then say that and let the evidence do the work.

**Questioner:** Reparative therapy, then. The outcome literature shows no reliable change in orientation and a consistent signal of harm; every major medical body opposes it, and about half the states ban it for minors.

**Thomas:** Begin with what I will not do: I will not force a man into therapy. To coerce him is to take his agency, and agency is the ground of repentance; a forced conversion is no conversion. A man who is not practicing his sin and not recruiting others to it should be left alone by the state, and surrounded by friends who counsel, encourage, and commiserate. What I object to is the reverse: a state that prosecutes the counselor when a man asks for help to change. That is the state deciding which direction of change is permitted.

**Questioner:** The bans mostly cover licensed practitioners treating minors, not adults seeking pastoral counsel. And the Supreme Court has taken up Colorado's ban on talk therapy in *Chiles v. Salazar*, so the line is being redrawn as we speak.

**Thomas:** Then let it be redrawn toward liberty. On the evidence I have a methodological objection, not a wave of the hand. The studies define success as a change of orientation. What a religious client usually seeks is a change of conduct and a measure of peace, and both are attainable; the studies do not measure what he came for. The harm signal comes largely from self-selected samples that cannot separate the therapy from the misery that drove the man to seek it. And I am suspicious of a field that declared the condition untreatable and stopped looking, when we have chased cures for cancer through a century of unreliable outcomes because the disease was worth defeating.

**Questioner:** Cancer research continued because cancer kills. The field stopped here because of a suicidality signal in the treated. You may argue the signal is confounded, and partly it is, but that is the argument you must make, not one you may skip. And your own case, a young man in the 1990s who left that experimentation behind and lives a healthy heterosexual life, is real, and it is a case, not a rate. Individual change is documented, especially in the young; a reliable population-level change under treatment is not.

**Thomas:** I accept the distinction. I know for certain of one man. I make no claim about rates. I claim that the door should not be locked from the outside.

---

**Questioner:** You've proposed, as the tip of your campaign's spear, a constitutional amendment declaring the nation's allegiance to the God of the Bible.

**Thomas:** Because it is the elephant in the room. Are we a Christian nation or are we not? The Founders left hints of their allegiance: "the Year of our Lord," the general principles of Christianity Adams named to Jefferson in 1813 as the principles on which independence was achieved. But they fell short of a full-throated declaration, and eighty years after *Everson* we are swimming in an ambiguity that honors every diversity and settles nothing. I would rather stand for what is right and let some feel excluded than keep everyone included in a morass.

**Questioner:** Several corrections, and they matter because your own standard is truth in proportion. "One Nation Under God" entered the Pledge in 1954 by act of Congress; the Pledge's author, Francis Bellamy, was a Baptist minister and a Christian socialist who did not write those words. *Everson* (1947) did not rule that America is not a Christian nation; it applied the Establishment Clause to the states while upholding bus fare for parochial-school children. *Holy Trinity* (1892) has the "Christian nation" line; the Treaty of Tripoli (1797) has the opposite. The record is mixed, and the amendment would be choosing a side of it, not recovering something unambiguous.

**Thomas:** Then the paper says the record is mixed and that I am choosing. That is what a position is.

**Questioner:** And the Founders' reticence was not mainly secular against spiritual. It was Christians afraid of one another. Massachusetts kept an established church until 1833. Baptists, Quakers, and Catholics were jailed or barred under Christian establishments, and they were the ones who fought hardest for the no-religious-test clause. In the North Carolina ratifying debates, Iredell defended the clause explicitly as opening office to "Jews, Turks, and infidels." So the amendment's first hard question is not "which God" but "whose Bible, whose reading." Catholics, Baptists, Presbyterians, and Latter-day Saints will not agree on what the standard requires. An amendment would have to name a reading, or leave it to the courts you do not trust.

**Thomas:** You make my point for me, and I'll not dodge it. I do not support the intra-Christian imposition of doctrine in any form; I have said a man's private doctrine should never carry the force of law. What I mean by allegiance to the God of the Bible is allegiance to the moral law all those communions share, the commandments, the two great commandments on which they hang, the standard of conduct by which a man wishes to be judged and by which he judges. That is what Adams meant by the general principles of Christianity, and it is a standard Baptists and Catholics can both stand under. Where they differ, the state is silent. Where they agree, the nation has a conscience.

**Questioner:** Then the arithmetic. Two-thirds of Congress, three-fourths of the states. It will not pass in any future you or I can see.

**Thomas:** I know. I am a realist. I do not see this candidacy as a path to power; I see it as a voice in the wilderness, "prepare ye the way of the Lord." Wilberforce stood up in Parliament for twenty years before the slave trade fell, and he was laughed at for most of them. Small stands, even outrageous ones, are seeds. The most I can expect is that the seed takes root somewhere. The paper should say so, plainly, because a candidate who implies the amendment is achievable would be lying by proportion, and I have said that hyperbole is a lie.

---

**Questioner:** Last question. You've spoken of the two parties as the pro-God and anti-God parties, and of the need for the one to be converted to the other. That will be read as partisanship.

**Thomas:** It will, and so I'll drop the party names, because they were a symbol and the symbol has failed. There are devout Christians who vote one way and unbelievers who vote the other, and there are men in my own party who are Republicans in name only. The line I mean does not run between parties. It runs between those who actually live the Book, who dedicate the heart to conduct by His standard, and those who do not, whatever they call themselves and whatever creed they profess.

**Questioner:** Then what is the action plan? You've conceded the jury screen cannot make jurors righteous, that most vice is not the state's to punish, that the amendment cannot pass. What is left?

**Thomas:** What was always the whole of it. The conversion of the nation: a people with renewed hearts, committed to the way of Christ and listening for the promptings of His Spirit. Everything else, the jury, the courthouse, the amendment, is downstream. Until the people are committed to Godliness, law is what Nietzsche said it was, a will to power: the most votes, the most guns, the most money. When those line up with the law of God, we will have righteousness, and when they do not, we will have what we have now. I cannot make that alignment by winning an office. I can only stand where it is visible, say what is true, and enroll whoever will listen. The government cannot do this work. Fathers, churches, and neighbors can, one heart at a time. That is the platform. It is not much of a platform for a politician. It is the only one for a witness.

**Questioner:** And if the people will not turn?

**Thomas:** Then the nation will learn by its history what it would not learn by its prophets. It has happened to every nation that walked this road. I would rather it did not happen to mine, and I'll spend what voice I have saying so.

---

*The Candidate's position, stated plainly: restore the jury's knowledge of its own power and stop punishing candor about it; punish the sins that make victims and no others; never compel a man to participate in what he believes is sin; judge men by their fruits and never by their doctrine; comfort the suffering without reorganizing society around suffering; lock no door from the outside; tell the truth about what can pass and what cannot; and spend the campaign on the only reform that reaches the root, which is the heart of the people.*
