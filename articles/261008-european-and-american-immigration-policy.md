# 261008 European and American Immigration Policy

# Borders, Neighbors, and Proportion


### A response to Amil Imani’s “Europe Chose National Suicide, Will America Follow Nov. 3rd?”


*Dr. Thomas Lee Abshier | 08 October 2026*


---


Amil Imani has written a forceful appeal to undecided voters ahead of the November 3rd midterm. His argument runs like this: Britain and France stopped defending their borders and stopped asking newcomers to become citizens in more than name; their cities fractured, their governments turned on the speech of their own people, and their economies weakened. America, he says, is on the same road, and the only way off it is a Republican majority. He ends with a slogan: *Vote red. Guard the border. Defend the republic.*


Mr. Imani grew up in Iran and has spent years warning about radical Islam. He has earned a hearing on that subject, and I agree with a good part of what he says. But I want to test his essay the way I try to test my own: first by asking whether it is true in proportion, and then by asking whether its prescriptions pass the moral test I hold every public policy to — *would a neighbor feel loved by this act in the public square?*


## What he gets right


A nation has a duty to know who enters it. This is not cruelty; it is the first condition of every other kindness a government can offer. A household that cannot lock its door cannot protect the children asleep inside, and it cannot offer a guest a safe room either. The same is true of a country.


The moral case for an enforced border rests on love of neighbor, and it runs in more directions than partisans on either side usually admit:


- **The citizen is a neighbor.** The worker whose wages are undercut by labor paid off the books, the family in a border town whose ranch is a trafficking corridor, the town whose emergency room absorbs costs no one budgeted for — these people are owed protection by the government they pay for. A policy that ignores them does not love them.

- **The migrant is a neighbor.** An uncontrolled border is not a gift to the poor. It is a business model for cartels, who charge desperate people thousands of dollars, abandon them in deserts, and hold some of them in debt bondage for years. Every person who dies of thirst in the Sonoran Desert, every woman assaulted on the way north, is a casualty of a border that pretends to be open. Order at the border is mercy to the people who would otherwise be preyed on crossing it.

- **The future citizen is a neighbor.** The immigrant who waits his turn, learns the language, studies the Constitution, and takes the oath is cheated when the line he stood in turns out not to matter.


Scripture holds both truths at once. Israel was commanded to love the stranger “as yourself, for you were strangers in the land of Egypt” (Leviticus 19:34) — and in the same law, “one law shall be to him that is homeborn, and unto the stranger that sojourneth among you” (Exodus 12:49). Welcome and one law for all. The stranger who settled in Israel was not exempt from Israel’s law; he was brought under its protection *and* its obligations. That is what assimilation means, and Mr. Imani is right that Europe largely stopped asking for it.


He is also right that some European governments have turned their attention from crimes against persons to offenses of speech. Britain arrests people in significant numbers each year under communications laws for things they post online. A government that is quicker to police a citizen’s words than to secure his street has its priorities inverted. America’s First Amendment is a genuine difference between us, and it is worth defending.


## Where he loses proportion


I have written before that hyperbole is lying by proportion. A statement can contain nothing false in any single phrase and still deceive, because it makes a serious problem sound like a finished catastrophe, or a partial cause sound like the only cause. Mr. Imani’s essay does this in several places, and it weakens a case that did not need the exaggeration.


**Europe has not committed suicide.** Britain and France face real, serious problems with integration, crime in certain districts, and official timidity about naming them. But they are not dead civilizations. Their people are voting out the governments that ignored them; immigration has become the leading political issue in both countries. That is not the behavior of a corpse. It is a democracy correcting itself — slowly and late, but correcting. Calling it an “irreversible decline” tells Americans that elections cannot fix things, which is a strange message in an essay urging people to vote.


**“Parallel religious courts” overstates what exists.** Britain has sharia councils, and they deserve scrutiny — especially for how they treat women in divorce. But they are voluntary arbitration bodies, not courts with power over English law. The honest version of the concern is strong enough on its own: social pressure can make a “voluntary” council anything but voluntary for a woman in a closed community. Say that, and say it plainly. Don’t inflate it.


**The description of Washington is outdated.** Mr. Imani writes as though a progressive administration currently halts deportations and weakens the border. Republicans have held the White House and both houses of Congress since January 2025, and border crossings fell sharply after that. Whatever one thinks of the previous administration’s record, an essay published in October 2026 that describes it as the present doesn’t tell voters where they actually stand. A reader who knows the facts will discount everything else in the piece, even the true parts.


**One party is not “the sole institutional fortress.”** This is the claim I disagree with most. No party is a fortress. Parties are coalitions of fallible men pursuing power, and both of ours have spent decades failing on immigration — one by wanting cheap labor, the other by wanting future voters, and both by declining to enforce the law against employers, which is where the magnet actually is. When we tell voters that salvation runs through a party label, we ask them to stop examining the man on the ballot. That is the opposite of citizenship.


## What I would propose


I offer these as positions I hold and will argue for, not as final answers. If someone shows me a better argument, I will change my mind and say so publicly.


**1. Secure the border physically and turn off the magnet.** Walls, sensors, and agents matter, but people come mostly for work. Universal, mandatory employment verification, with real penalties for employers who knowingly hire off the books, would do more than any wall. The moral basis: an employer who exploits an illegal worker wrongs both that worker and the citizen he displaced. Both are his neighbors.


**2. Enforce the law with due process.** Deportation of those who have no right to be here is lawful and necessary. But every person, citizen or not, is made in God’s image and is owed a fair hearing before the government acts against him. Speed and fairness are not enemies; we need more immigration judges, not fewer protections.


**3. Make legal immigration ordered, generous where we choose to be, and conditional on becoming American.** A nation may decide how many newcomers it can welcome and absorb. Those it welcomes should learn English, learn our civic foundations, and take an oath they mean. Citizenship is adoption into a family, and adoption carries obligations as well as privileges.


**4. Protect speech by protecting the jury.** Mr. Imani rightly notes that America still has constitutional safeguards Europe lacks. The most important one he omits is the citizen jury. When a government tries to punish a man for his opinions, twelve of his neighbors can refuse to convict, whatever the judge instructs. Jury nullification is not a loophole; it is the people’s final check on unjust law, and it is why British-style speech prosecutions would face an uphill road here. We should teach every citizen that he has this power and this duty.


**5. Ground prosperity in abundant energy.** Mr. Imani is right that crippled energy sectors leave families cold and poor. My answer is the one I ran on in 2003 and run on now: abundant, safe, cheap energy — above all thorium molten-salt reactors — as the foundation of a strong economy. A nation with cheap energy builds things, pays its workers well, and has less reason to import cheap labor.


## How to vote on November 3rd


I share Mr. Imani’s conviction that this election matters and that staying home hands the decision to others. But I would not tell you to vote by color. I would tell you to vote by character and by the neighbor test.


Look at the man or woman on your ballot. Ask whether he will enforce the law at the border *and* treat the people caught there as human beings. Ask whether he will hold employers accountable, or only the workers they exploit. Ask whether he defends your right to speak even when he dislikes what you say. Ask whether he tells you the truth in proportion, or tries to frighten you into a decision.


Then vote for the best one on the ballot, whatever his party. That is how a free people guards its border, its law, and its republic — not by trusting a label to do the thinking for them, but by doing it themselves.
