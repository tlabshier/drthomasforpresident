# Sunlight, Storage, and the Reactor — Outline Summary

*By Thomas Lee Abshier, ND · 17 November 2026*

**My position:** Solar will supply most new daytime electricity, but America's energy foundation must be firm power: existing nuclear now, natural gas and hydropower as the bridge, and the thorium molten-salt reactor as the long-term foundation. Solar and batteries are built on top of that foundation, not in place of it.


 	- **What Musk claimed.** He says solar will "round up to 100%" of civilization's energy. That may be true in the far future, but it's an overstatement for powering America in the coming decades.

 	- **What SpaceX is building.** Starmind is not power beamed down from space. It is AI computers in orbit that run on sunlight and send only data to Earth: the work moves to the power.

 	- **What solar has become.** Grid battery packs fell 45% in 2025 alone. In sunny places, solar plus batteries can now cover about 97% of the hours in a year at prices competitive with new coal or nuclear.

 	- **The weak spot.** Night is not the problem; long cloudy stretches and deep winter are. Research shows solar and wind can reliably reach about 80% of U.S. demand, but 100% needs weeks of storage. Texas has seen low-output stretches lasting months. Batteries cannot cover that, and pumped storage (like the Castaic project I saw built in 1973) covers hours and days, not weeks.

 	- **What the builders concluded.** The data center companies, which cannot afford outages, are buying nuclear power (Microsoft, Meta, Google) and building their own gas plants. They use solar to cut costs, not to guarantee reliability.

 	- **Orbital computing.** Moving computer work to wherever the power is makes sense. Orbital data centers face heavy cooling, radiation, and repair problems and are likely to cost far more than ground data centers for years.

 	- **The moral test.**


 	*Righteous:* honest accounting of each source's whole cost, including backup (Proverbs 11:1; Luke 14:28).

 	- *Loving:* reliable power protects the elderly, the sick, and the poor. Winter Storm Uri killed 246 Texans by the official count; reliability must be engineered whatever the source (Proverbs 22:3).

 	- *Would I want it done to me:* no one on a fixed income should pay for a data center's backup power or bet his furnace on the batteries outlasting the clouds.


 	- **What I propose.**


 	Keep existing nuclear plants running and allow restarts.

 	- No mandates or hidden subsidies; let solar win where it is truly cheapest.

 	- Make large new users like data centers supply their own reliable capacity.

 	- Use gas, hydropower, and pumped storage as the bridge.

 	- Make the thorium MSR a national priority: demonstration reactors, faster licensing, and solving the engineering problems in the open.

 	- Keep researching fusion, but do not wait for it.


 	- **Conclusion.** Build the house on the rock (Matthew 7:24–25): firm power as the foundation, and on that foundation, let solar build as high as it can.

# Sunlight, Storage, and the Reactor: An Energy Foundation a Nation Can Stand On

*By Thomas Lee Abshier, ND |17 November 2026*

**An essay responding to ["'That Math Is Obvious and Trivial': Elon Musk Says Solar Will Supply 100% of Civilization's Energy as SpaceX and Tesla Ramp Up Manufacturing"](https://www.msn.com/en-us/money/general/that-math-is-obvious-and-trivial-elon-musk-says-solar-will-supply-100-of-civilization-s-energy-as-spacex-and-tesla-ramp-up-manufacturing/ar-AA2hxjOG) (Benzinga, via MSN, 16 November 2026).**


---


## My Position

Elon Musk said this week that the share of civilization's energy we take from the Sun will "obviously ultimately round up to 100%," and that the math is "obvious and trivial." He backed the claim with real money: SpaceX has ordered equipment to manufacture 100 gigawatts per year of solar cells, and Tesla is building a $10 billion solar factory near Houston.

I take the claim seriously, and I agree with part of it. Solar power has become a gift to this country, and it will supply most of the *new* daytime electricity built over the next twenty years. But I do not believe a nation should rest its foundation on a source that can fail for weeks at a time during the rare long stretch of clouds. I stand on the side of building America's energy foundation on firm power, meaning power that runs day and night, summer and winter, whatever the weather. That means keeping our existing nuclear plants running, using natural gas and hydropower as the bridge, and making the thorium molten-salt reactor (MSR) the long-term foundation. Solar and batteries should be built on top of that foundation, wherever they are cheapest, but not in place of it.

Energy is the first letter of the New ERA Vision (Energy, Righteousness, and Accountability) for a reason. Cheap, abundant, reliable energy is what lets a family heat its home, a farmer pump his water, a factory employ a town, and a nation defend itself. A policy that gets energy wrong gets everything downstream of it wrong. So the question deserves care, not slogans from either side.

## What Musk Actually Claimed, and What SpaceX Is Actually Building

There are two ways to read "100% solar," and they should be kept apart.

The first is a statement about the very long run. If mankind spreads beyond Earth, the Sun is by far the largest energy source within reach, and in that sense nearly all energy will eventually be solar. That is true, and it is not a policy question for this generation.

The second is a statement about how to power America in the coming decades. Here the claim is an overstatement, for reasons I will lay out below.

When I first read about SpaceX's solar order, I assumed it was for satellites that collect sunlight in orbit and beam the power down to Earth. That idea has been studied for decades, and most engineers judge it uneconomical, because too much energy is lost converting sunlight to microwaves and back to electricity. SpaceX is doing something cleverer. Its Starmind satellites are [orbiting AI computers that run on solar power in space and send only their results back to Earth through Starlink](https://driveteslacanada.ca/news/spacex-singulus-contract-starmind-solar-cells/). Musk is not moving the power to the work. He is moving the work to the power. I will come back to whether that pays.

## Credit Where It Is Due: What Solar Has Become

Anyone who dismisses solar as a subsidy-driven toy is working from numbers that are ten years old. In 2025, [battery packs for grid storage fell to about $70 per kilowatt-hour, a 45% drop in a single year](https://www.ess-news.com/?p=7552), and lithium-ion packs overall are more than 90% cheaper than in 2010. That changes the arithmetic of the obvious objection, which is that solar works only in daylight, so you must build twice: panels to make the power and batteries to store it for the night.

Building twice is real, but both halves have become cheap. The energy think tank Ember calculates that in Las Vegas, [5 kilowatts of solar panels paired with a 17 kilowatt-hour battery can deliver a steady kilowatt around the clock](https://ember-energy.org/latest-insights/solar-electricity-every-hour-of-every-day-is-here-and-it-changes-everything/). It estimates that such a system can cover about 97% of the hours in a year for about $104 per megawatt-hour, which is less than its estimates for new coal and new Western-built nuclear. For the ordinary daily cycle in the sunny South and Southwest, solar plus batteries is now a serious competitor on price, without any need for subsidy.

That is good news, and a responsible energy policy should welcome it.

## The Weak Spot: The Long Cloud

The difficulty is not the night. Batteries handle the night. The difficulty is the rare stretch of overcast weeks, and the deep winter, when the Sun is low and the days are short.

In 2018, researchers from Carnegie, Caltech, and UC Irvine studied 36 years of hourly American weather data. They allowed themselves generous assumptions: a perfect continent-wide transmission grid with no losses. They found that [solar and wind could in principle supply about 80% of America's electricity, but that reaching 100% reliably would require several weeks of stored energy, or far more solar and wind capacity than is normally needed](https://carnegiescience.edu/geophysical-constraints-reliability-solar-and-wind-power-united-states). Spreading solar farms across the country helps less than one would hope, because a large weather system darkens a whole region at once, and night falls on the whole country together.

A Columbia University study of Texas, a state blessed with both sun and wind, found that [low-production stretches could run for as long as four months, and about twice that for a system relying on solar alone](https://news.climate.columbia.edu/2022/04/12/youve-heard-of-water-droughts-could-energy-droughts-be-next). The lead researcher was blunt about it: "You won't be able to manage variability like this with batteries."

I have seen the older answer to this problem with my own eyes. In the summer of 1973, as an intern with the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power, I watched the department build the Castaic project as a hydraulic battery: pump water uphill into the reservoir when demand is low, and run it back down through the generators when demand peaks. Pumped storage works, and it still carries much of the world's stored electricity. But it shifts power by hours and days, not weeks, and the mountains and water it needs are not everywhere.

The risk of a long cloudy stretch can be measured from decades of weather records. What cannot be done cheaply is to pay for covering the rarest and worst stretch with panels and batteries alone. That last few percent of reliability is where the cost explodes, and it is exactly the few percent on which lives depend.

## What the Builders Have Concluded

The clearest evidence comes not from advocates but from the companies that stand to lose the most if the power fails. AI data centers must run every hour of every day, and they are the fastest-growing source of new electricity demand in the country. The largest technology companies have studied the question with their own money on the line, and their answer is firm power:


 	- Microsoft contracted to [restart Three Mile Island Unit 1 under a 20-year agreement for 835 megawatts](https://www.softwareseni.com/what-the-nuclear-power-renaissance-means-for-the-future-of-ai-infrastructure).

 	- Meta signed agreements with Vistra, Oklo, and TerraPower that [could unlock up to 6.6 gigawatts of nuclear capacity by 2035](https://algeriatech.news/smr-big-tech-cloud-power-nuclear-deals-2026/).

 	- Google signed the [first U.S. utility power-purchase agreement for a Generation-IV advanced reactor](https://enkiai.com/data-center/meta-nuclear-terrapower-reactors/), Kairos Power's Hermes 2, which is cooled by molten salt.

 	- Several companies are building their own natural gas plants beside their data centers, such as [Meta's 400 megawatts at its New Albany hub in Ohio](https://enkiai.com/data-center/meta-nuclear-terrapower-reactors/).


These companies still buy solar, and they should. But they buy it to cut the cost of daytime energy. They do not trust it to keep the lights on in the third week of an overcast January. When the people with the most to lose and the best engineers reach the same conclusion as the weather data, I think the conclusion deserves respect.

## Moving the Work to the Power

There is real wisdom in SpaceX's approach, and in a humbler version of it here on Earth. A computer job is easier to move than electricity is. An AI training job can be paused at one data center and resumed at another, wherever power is abundant that day. Some companies already shift computing between regions to follow the power. That is an honest, low-cost use of geography, and it should be encouraged.

Whether orbital data centers will pay off is another matter. In space, the heat from the chips can only be shed by radiating it away, so large orbital computers need enormous radiators. Radiation degrades the chips, and nothing can be repaired once it is launched. An aerospace analyst at ABI Research estimated that [running a GPU in orbit for a year costs at least ten times as much as on the ground, even assuming a very optimistic Starship launch cost](https://pipedot.org/7683A). Musk owns the rocket company, which gives him a cost advantage no one else has, and he has a record of being right about the direction of technology while being late on timelines. My judgment is that orbital computing will find a real role in the 2030s, but it is not a substitute for a reliable grid on Earth.

## The Moral Test

Every position paper on this site is put to three questions. Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? And would I want it done to me?

**Is it righteous?** Righteousness begins with honest weights and measures: *"A false balance is abomination to the LORD: but a just weight is his delight"* **(Proverbs 11:1)**. Energy debates are full of false balances. One side quotes the price of a solar panel at noon and leaves out the cost of the cloudy month. The other side quotes the nuclear plant's reliability and leaves out decades of construction overruns. A righteous energy policy counts the whole cost of each source, including the cost of keeping the power on when the source is unavailable, and charges that cost to the party who causes it. Our Lord gave the rule for every builder: *"For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost, whether he have sufficient to finish it?"* **(Luke 14:28)**.

**Is it loving?** Reliable power is not a luxury for the comfortable. It is protection for the weak. When the grid fails in a hard winter, the ones who suffer first are the elderly, the sick on home oxygen, and the poor in poorly insulated homes. In February 2021, Winter Storm Uri knocked out much of the Texas grid, and the state's official count of deaths was 246. In fairness, that failure was not caused by solar; gas, coal, nuclear, and wind plants all froze or faltered. The lesson is not that one technology is evil. The lesson is that reliability must be engineered deliberately, whatever the source, and that a society that loves its neighbors pays for the margin that keeps them alive. *"A prudent man foreseeth the evil, and hideth himself; but the simple pass on, and are punished"* **(Proverbs 22:3)**.

**Would I want it done to me?** If I lived on a fixed income in a cold state, I would not want a policy that loaded the cost of a data center's backup power onto my electric bill. I also would not want a policy that bet my furnace on the hope that a cloudy month would never be longer than the batteries could cover. I would want the power to be there, and I would want each large user to carry the cost of his own reliability.

## What I Propose

These positions are advocacy, held open to a better argument.


 	- **Build on firm power.** Keep every safe, existing nuclear plant running, and license the restart of retired plants where the owners will pay for it. These plants are the fastest source of reliable, clean power available, because they already exist.

 	- **Let solar compete honestly.** No mandates that pick a winning technology, and no subsidies that hide a technology's true cost. Where solar plus batteries is cheapest, it will win on its merits, and in much of the South and Southwest it will. Where it is not, it should not be forced on the ratepayer.

 	- **Make large new loads responsible for their own reliability.** A gigawatt data center should bring or contract the firm capacity it needs, rather than passing the cost of its backup power to households. That is accountability, the third letter of the New ERA Vision.

 	- **Use natural gas and hydropower as the bridge.** America has abundant gas. It can be turned on at will, and it should carry the firm load until advanced reactors are built in numbers. Pumped storage should be permitted promptly where the land and water allow.

 	- **Make the thorium molten-salt reactor a national priority.** The MSR runs at low pressure, cannot melt down in the ordinary sense because its fuel is already liquid, and drains into a safe holding tank if it overheats. Thorium is abundant, and the reactor leaves far less long-lived waste than today's reactors. China already operates [a 2-megawatt thorium MSR and is targeting a 100-megawatt demonstration unit by 2035](https://rareearthexchanges.com/news/america-tests-thoriums-promise-as-china-moves-to-scale/). America designed and ran the first molten-salt reactor at Oak Ridge in the 1960s, and then abandoned it. We should finish the work we started: fund demonstration reactors, modernize the licensing process so that a safe design can be approved in years rather than decades, and solve the remaining engineering problems (corrosion, salt chemistry, and safeguards against diverting material to weapons) in the open.

 	- **Do not wait for fusion.** Fusion may someday work, and research should continue. But it is further from being both workable and affordable than the MSR, and a nation cannot heat its homes on a promise.


## Conclusion

Musk is right that the Sun is the great energy source of the long future, and he is right to build. I wish him success, because cheap solar panels and cheap batteries make every other part of the energy system cheaper. But the foundation of a house is chosen for the storm, not the sunny day. Our Lord described the wise man who built his house on the rock, so that when the rain fell and the floods came and the winds beat on that house, it stood **(Matthew 7:24–25)**. America's energy rock is firm power: nuclear today, the thorium molten-salt reactor tomorrow, and gas and hydropower to carry us between. On that rock, let solar build as high as it can.

## Sources and Their Limits


 	- **The source article.** The Benzinga report (via MSN) on Musk's statement and SpaceX's and Tesla's solar manufacturing plans, linked at the top. It relies on Musk's own posts and company announcements, which are interested-party statements.

 	- **Starmind.** [Drive Tesla Canada](https://driveteslacanada.ca/news/spacex-singulus-contract-starmind-solar-cells/) and other trade reports, drawn largely from SpaceX's own announcements. SpaceX is preparing a public stock offering, which gives it a reason to present its plans favorably.

 	- **Solar and battery costs.** [Ember](https://ember-energy.org/latest-insights/solar-electricity-every-hour-of-every-day-is-here-and-it-changes-everything/) and [BloombergNEF via ESS News](https://www.ess-news.com/?p=7552). Ember advocates for renewables, and its figures use global, largely Chinese, equipment prices; American prices are higher, and its estimates leave out much of the cost of new transmission lines. Its cost figure for nuclear reflects Western construction costs, which are far higher than China's.

 	- **Weather reliability.** The [Carnegie/Caltech/UC Irvine study](https://carnegiescience.edu/geophysical-constraints-reliability-solar-and-wind-power-united-states) (2018) and the [Columbia University study](https://news.climate.columbia.edu/2022/04/12/youve-heard-of-water-droughts-could-energy-droughts-be-next) (2022). Both are peer-reviewed models built on historical weather. Their results depend on how a shortfall is defined, and a few decades of records may not include the worst stretch that could occur.

 	- **Data center power deals.** Industry trackers and trade publications ([SoftwareSeni](https://www.softwareseni.com/what-the-nuclear-power-renaissance-means-for-the-future-of-ai-infrastructure), [Algeria Tech News](https://algeriatech.news/smr-big-tech-cloud-power-nuclear-deals-2026/), [EnkiAI](https://enkiai.com/data-center/meta-nuclear-terrapower-reactors/)) summarizing company announcements. Announced capacities are often maximums and future options, not power already delivered.

 	- **Orbital data center costs.** An ABI Research analyst writing in [IEEE Spectrum](https://pipedot.org/7683A). It is one rough cost comparison, and its result depends heavily on assumptions about launch cost and chip lifetime that could change.

 	- **Thorium MSR status.** [Rare Earth Exchanges](https://rareearthexchanges.com/news/america-tests-thoriums-promise-as-china-moves-to-scale/), reporting Chinese announcements that have not been independently verified in detail.

 	- **Winter Storm Uri.** The 246 figure is the Texas Department of State Health Services' official count; some independent analyses estimate the true toll was considerably higher.


My sourcing leans on the mainstream and industry record. Cost figures in particular are produced by parties with interests on every side: renewable advocates, nuclear developers, and the companies selling the equipment. Where I had to choose, I gave more weight to what companies have done with their own money than to what any party has projected.
