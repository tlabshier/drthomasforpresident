# 261031 - Delayed Justice by Administrative Closure

# Shelved Is Not Settled:


### Administrative Closure, the Supreme Court, and Justice Delayed


*Dr. Thomas Lee Abshier, ND  |  31 October 2026*


On October 27, the Supreme Court declined, in a one-sentence unsigned order with no noted dissent, to restore the power of immigration judges to “administratively close” deportation cases. Most Americans have never heard of administrative closure. It deserves attention because it touches three things I care about: the honesty of our laws, the fairness of our courts, and the promises our government makes.


I support ending the indefinite shelving of deportation cases. A case that sits untouched for a decade and a half is not being managed; it is being quietly decided without a decision. But I also hold that the way this practice was ended sets a bad precedent, and that ending it without keeping faith with the crime victims Congress promised to protect, and without giving the courts the judges they need, would trade one injustice for another.


## What administrative closure is


When the government seeks to deport someone, the case goes before an immigration judge. Sometimes that person has another matter pending elsewhere: a visa petition filed by an American spouse, an application for a U visa (a visa Congress created for crime victims who help police), or a criminal appeal that could change whether he is deportable at all. For decades, judges handled such cases by taking them off the active calendar. The case was not dismissed. They paused it, and it could be brought back.


The practice grew in immigration courts as a docket tool. In 2018, Attorney General Sessions ruled that judges had no general authority to do it. Attorney General Garland reversed that ruling in 2021, and in 2024 the Biden administration wrote the practice into a formal regulation. Government figures put the total at roughly 360,000 people granted administrative closure since 1990.


## How it ended


On the morning of June 22, 2026, Texas sued in federal court in the Northern District of Texas to strike down the 2024 regulation. Within hours, Texas and the Justice Department jointly asked the court to enter a consent judgment. Chief Judge Reed O’Connor signed it the same day. The judgment vacated the rule, barred the Justice Department from enforcing it, and forbade any future administration from issuing a similar rule without Congress’s express authorization. Both sides waived appeal.


Three cities (Baltimore, New Haven, and Columbus) and an immigrant legal-services group tried to intervene, calling the arrangement collusion. The Fifth Circuit refused them emergency relief, and the Supreme Court did the same on October 27.


It is important to be precise about what the Court did. It did not rule that administrative closure is unconstitutional or unlawful. It did not decide whether judges keep some residual authority outside the regulation. It declined to step into an emergency appeal. The merits question, whether the challengers may intervene at all, is still before the Fifth Circuit.


## Where the critics of administrative closure are right


According to immigration-court statistics from July 2025, about 361,600 cases sat in “inactive pending” status. The average case in that pool had been closed for about sixteen and a half years; the median, about eleven and a half.


Those numbers settle the central question for me. A tool meant to pause a case while another matter is resolved should not hold a case for sixteen years. Visa petitions are adjudicated. Criminal appeals end. When a pause lasts longer than a childhood, the pause has become the outcome. The person lives in the country without legal status, the government never decides his case, and Congress never voted for that result. That is amnesty by inertia, and it is not honest government, whatever one thinks of amnesty itself.


Due process runs in two directions. The man facing deportation is owed a fair hearing. So is the public, whose law says who may stay. A court system that indefinitely postpones decisions serves neither. A judge’s first duty is to judge.


## Where the method goes wrong


I am glad to see the indefinite shelving end. I am not glad to see it ended this way.


A regulation adopted through public notice and comment was erased by a lawsuit filed and settled that same morning by two parties who agreed with each other, before a judge who signed it that day, with appeal waived on both sides. Then the judgment reached forward to bind every future administration. Whatever the merits of the rule, that is not adversarial litigation. It is a policy change dressed as a court case.


Those who welcome this outcome should picture the same tool in other hands. A future administration and a friendly state could use a same-day consent decree to erase a rule protecting gun owners, homeschooling families, or religious employers, and bind the next administration as well. A principle that only feels right when our side uses it is not a principle. If administrative closure was unauthorized by law, the honest path was to repeal the rule through the same public process that created it, ask Congress to settle the question, and let the courts hear a real dispute.


## Keeping the promises we made


The sharpest problem concerns crime victims. Congress created the U visa so that immigrants without status who are victims of serious crimes would come forward and help police and prosecutors. The bargain was explicit: cooperate with law enforcement, and the nation will give you a path to stay. But Congress capped the program at 10,000 principal visas a year, far below the number of qualified applicants, so vetted victims wait years in line. Administrative closure was one of the ways those victims were kept from being deported while they waited for the government to process its own promise.


Reporting this year indicates the administration no longer treats a U visa applicant’s preliminary approval as protection from detention or removal. If that stands, a woman who reported her abuser, testified against him, and was certified by police as a cooperating victim can be removed before the government gets around to her file. That is not enforcing the law. It is breaking the government’s word, and it teaches every immigrant victim in America that calling the police is dangerous. Every criminal who preys on immigrant communities benefits from that lesson.


## The test


I ask three questions of any policy. Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? Would I want it done to me?


*Is it righteous?* Ending a practice that let deportation cases disappear for decades without a decision is righteous; it restores the rule that law means what it says and that judges decide. Ending a public regulation through a collusive same-day settlement is not; it substitutes maneuver for honest process.


*Is it loving?* Love for the citizen requires enforcing immigration law. Love for the immigrant requires that he be heard fairly and that promises made to him be kept. Throwing 361,000 old cases onto courts already carrying an enormous backlog, without new judges, invites rushed hearings, and rushed hearings produce errors in both directions.


*Would I want it done to me?* If I had lived here unlawfully for sixteen years, I would not be entitled to stay simply because the government was slow. But if I had reported a crime, helped convict the man who hurt me, and been told by the government that I qualified for its protection, I would expect it to keep its word. I would not want to be deported while my approved petition sat in a federal queue.


## What I would do


- **Let Congress decide, narrowly.** Congress should give immigration judges a limited, statutory power to pause a case only when the person has already filed for relief that, if granted, would end the deportation case, with mandatory review at set intervals and a firm outer limit. A pause should be measured in months, not decades.

- **Keep faith with cooperating crime victims.** No person holding a law-enforcement certification and a preliminary approval for a U visa should be removed while that petition is pending. Congress should raise the U visa cap or fund the processing needed to clear the backlog, so the promise can be kept in a reasonable time.

- **Hire the judges.** Speed and fairness are not enemies. The country needs more immigration judges and support staff, not fewer protections. Old cases returning to the docket should be scheduled in order, with the person given notice and time to obtain counsel.

- **Resolve the oldest cases first, honestly.** Cases with no pending relief should be heard and decided. Cases with real pending relief should be decided on that relief. Either way, the answer should be a decision.

- **Reject government by consent decree.** Congress should prohibit federal agencies from settling suits in ways that vacate regulations without notice and comment, or that bind future administrations, and should require that such settlements be opened to interested parties before entry.


## Conclusion


A nation that will not decide its own immigration cases is not merciful; it is evasive. Ending indefinite shelving is right. But righteousness is not only about outcomes. It concerns the means we use and the promises we keep. Let us enforce the law, decide the cases, fund the courts, and keep our word to the victims who trusted us. That is how a nation of laws treats both its citizens and the strangers within its gates.
