# Who Pays for What: Funding the Town, the State, and the Nation


*A position paper from Dr. Thomas for President*


## What the Oregon debate showed


On October 7, 2026, Oregon’s two gubernatorial candidates met in Medford for their second debate. The moderators asked Governor Tina Kotek whether she would propose or support changes to the state’s tax code in a second term.


In substance, she said taxes must provide adequate funding for essential services; the state might “tweak some things” to become more competitive with other states, as her Prosperity Council recommended; but any change must protect funding for schools, health care, and public safety against cuts from Washington. She pointed to a bill she signed this year to shield Oregon from those federal changes, and faulted her opponent for voting against it.


State Senator Christine Drazan answered that she would lower taxes. She said that when the governor speaks of “fighting for funding,” the plain translation is a new tax, and she listed them: a tax on nicotine pouches, an insurance tax, a payroll tax, a vehicle tax, a paddleboard tax. She said Oregonians tell her they cannot afford to live there, that businesses want relief, and that the governor’s tax increases have driven Oregon’s business climate from the top tier toward the bottom.


Both candidates deserve credit for what they got right. Governor Kotek is right that a state must pay for the duties it carries, and that a budget built on federal money is exposed when Washington changes course. Senator Drazan is right that a long list of small new taxes is a real burden, and that people’s complaints deserve to be heard rather than managed. In an interview earlier this year, she made the sharper point: Oregonians object less to paying taxes than to not seeing results from what they pay.


But listen to what neither candidate asked. They argued about *how much* to collect. Neither asked *which level of government should do what, and therefore which level should collect** for it*. The governor’s own answer revealed the problem: Oregon cannot plan its budget without first guessing what Congress will do. A state that cannot set its own course without asking Washington’s leave is not governing itself. It is administering a branch office.


That is the question I want to address. Not whether taxes should be higher or lower in one state, but how the town, the state, and the nation should each be funded, and why the answers should differ for each.


## The moral foundation


Every tax is a taking. It is collected under the threat of force, from a neighbor who did not individually agree to it. That does not make taxation wrong. It makes it serious. A taking can be justified only if a reasonable neighbor, looking at what was taken and what was done with it, could say: *this was done for my good, and I can see it.* That is the test I apply to every policy on this site: would my neighbor feel loved by this act in the public square?


That test yields four principles of honest public finance. I hold them as my position, open to a better argument.


**1. The nearest level that can do the job should do it, and pay for it.** The family comes first, then the church and voluntary associations, then the town, then the state, and only then the nation. This is not merely efficiency. The nearer the government, the more clearly the neighbor can see what he paid for and the more easily he can hold the officials to account. A man can attend the county commission. He cannot attend the Department of Education.


**2. Whoever spends the money should raise it.** When one level spends what another level collected, no one is fully accountable. The official who spends takes credit for the benefit; the official who taxes is far away; the citizen cannot tell whom to thank or whom to vote out.


**3. Taxes should be visible.** A man should know what government costs him. Withholding from paychecks, taxes buried in the price of goods, and fees hidden in a utility bill all hide the cost of government from the people who bear it. A hidden tax fails the neighbor test even when the money is well spent, because the neighbor never gets to judge the exchange.


**4. Tax what no man made before taxing what men make.** God gave land, minerals, water, timber, and the airwaves to all people in common. Charging rent for their private use takes nothing a man earned by his work. Taxing his wages, by contrast, takes a share of his labor before he has decided how to use it. I set out this principle at the federal level in my paper *Reforming the Administrative State*; here I apply it to all three levels.


## Why the levels have collapsed into one


Under the Constitution as written, the federal government was meant to be small and funded mainly by tariffs, while the states would carry most of the work of government. Madison wrote in Federalist 45 that the powers left to the states would extend to “all the objects which, in the ordinary course of affairs, concern the lives, liberties, and properties of the people.”


Two changes undid that design. The Sixteenth Amendment (1913) gave Washington a direct claim on every citizen’s income. And the federal government learned to use that money to buy authority it was never granted: it collects taxes from the people of every state, then sends part of the money back to the states with conditions attached. Federal grants now make up roughly a third of all state government spending (approximate; check the National Association of State Budget Officers’ annual State Expenditure Report). A state that accepts them must run its programs Washington’s way. A state that refuses them still pays the federal taxes, and simply watches its citizens’ money go to other states.


The Supreme Court has recognized the danger. In *South Dakota v. Dole* (1987) it allowed Congress to attach conditions to grants, but in *NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012) it held that threatening to withdraw all existing Medicaid funding from states that refused the expansion crossed the line into coercion, which it called “a gun to the head.” The line it drew still favors Washington. Every lesser condition remains lawful.


This is why Governor Kotek’s answer sounded the way it did. When a third of a state’s budget depends on federal decisions, every federal change becomes a state crisis, and every state election becomes an argument about Washington partly. Oregon’s voters are asked to choose a governor based on how well she will absorb shocks she cannot control.


## The pattern in one state


Oregon illustrates all four principles, both kept and broken.


**Visibility.** Oregon has no general sales tax, and both candidates said plainly in Medford that they oppose one. Yet in 2019 the Legislature passed the Corporate Activity Tax, a tax on the gross receipts of businesses with more than $1 million in Oregon commercial activity. A gross receipts tax is paid on every sale at every stage of production, whether the business makes a profit or not, and much of it ends up in higher prices for consumers. In effect, it is a sales tax the buyer never sees on his receipt. If Oregonians are to pay a tax on what they buy, it would be more honest to show it at the register, where they can judge it, than to bury it in prices, where they cannot. My point is not that Oregon should adopt a sales tax. It is that a state which refuses a visible consumption tax while collecting a hidden one has not spared its citizens the tax; it has spared itself the accountability.


**Consent.** In 2025 the Legislature raised the gas tax and transportation fees. Citizens gathered enough signatures to put the increase on the ballot, and voters rejected it. That is self-government working as it should: a tax the people can see, at a level close enough for them to act on.


**Limits.** Oregon’s constitution includes the “kicker”: when state revenue exceeds the official forecast by more than two percent, the surplus is returned to taxpayers. It is one of the few rules in American public finance that forces a government to give back money it did not plan to need. Every state, and the nation, would benefit from a rule like it.


**Inheritance.** Oregon taxes estates worth more than $1 million, the lowest threshold of any state. Senator Drazan would repeal the tax; Governor Kotek would consider changing it if the state lost no revenue. The moral question is simpler than the fiscal one.* “A good man leaves an inheritance to his children’s children”*** (Proverbs 13:22)**. What a man built and saved from already-taxed income belongs to the family he built it for. A tax that falls on a family farm or business at the moment of a death takes from the family at its weakest point. I would end estate taxes at every level.

Four features of this arrangement matter more than the details.


**Schools.** Children’s education is first the duty of their parents. Where a town helps fund schooling, the money should follow the child to the school or home his parents choose, so that the family remains the deciding party. Governor Kotek opposed this in Medford, arguing that it takes public money out of public schools. My answer is that the money belongs to its purpose, educating the child, not to an institution.


**Competition among states is a feature.** When a business leaves Grants Pass for Arizona, that is information. A state that taxes heavily for poor results will lose people and enterprise to states that do better. Federal grants blunt that signal by spreading costs across the whole country. Under the arrangement above, each state’s results would be its own, and its voters would know whom to credit or blame.


**The land-value charge needs a homestead protection.** A land-value charge can hit a farmer, rancher, or retired widow hard if they own valuable land but have little cash. The answer is a homestead exemption or a deferral paid from the estate when the land is sold, so no one is taxed off the land he lives on and works.


**Charity carries what government should not.** Care of the poor, the sick, the widow, and the orphan belongs first to the family and the church. As I proposed earlier, a charity credit would let citizens give directly to charities that serve those needs, with government welfare spending falling as giving rises. Most of what all three levels now spend goes to transfer programs, so each level’s budget depends on how fully this duty returns to the people.


## What I would do as president


A president cannot rewrite state tax codes. But Washington created much of this tangle, and Washington can start untangling it. Here is what I would propose.


- **Turn back functions and revenue together.** For each grant program that funds a duty belonging to the states, Congress would end the program on a published schedule and reduce federal taxes by the same amount, so that the states can raise that money themselves, under their own voters’ control, if they choose to continue the program. A state’s people would decide what to keep. No state would be left holding a federal program without the tax room to fund it.

- **No new conditions on existing money.** Until turnback is complete, I would veto any bill that adds new conditions to grants the states already depend on. A condition attached after a state has built its budget around the money is the kind of pressure the Court called coercion in *NFIB v. Sebelius*, whatever its size.

- **No unfunded mandates.** If Congress requires a state or town to do something, Congress pays for it from federal revenue in the same bill. The Unfunded Mandates Reform Act of 1995 tried to discourage this, but it only requires notice and can be waived by a vote. I would ask Congress to make the rule binding.

- **A tax receipt for every citizen.** Each year, every taxpayer would receive a single statement showing what he paid to the federal government and what it was spent on, in plain categories. States and towns would be invited to join it. A man who can see the whole bill can judge whether he was served.

- **Honest money at the top.** Only the federal government can borrow without limit and have its debt bought with newly created money. That is the root of federal expansion, and through the grant system it spreads to the states. A balanced federal budget outside declared war or emergency, and a ban on the Federal Reserve financing deficits, are the preconditions for everything above.

- **The federal transition I have already proposed.** Over a decade, federal revenue would shift from income and payroll taxes to rent on the national commons and modest tariffs, with Social Security and Medicare honored in full for those who planned their lives around them.


## The right question


Oregon’s voters will choose between a governor who wants to protect present funding and a challenger who wants to lower taxes. Both positions are understandable. But both accept the current arrangement: the state cannot plan without guessing at Washington, the citizen cannot see much of what he pays, and no official at any level fully answers for the whole.


The better question is not *how much we should tax,* but *who should do it, and who should pay for it.* When each level of government does only its proper work, funds it from its own visible sources, and answers to the people nearest to it, the amount of tax tends to take care of itself. The neighbor can see what he paid, see what he received, and decide at the ballot box, at the town meeting, and in the jury box whether he was served in love.

## Notes on the debate claims


- The tax bill Governor Kotek referred to is Senate Bill 1507 (2026). According to the Oregon Capital Chronicle, it disconnected Oregon from several business and auto-loan provisions of the 2025 federal tax law, which preserved roughly $300 million in state revenue, and also expanded Oregon’s earned income tax credit. Both descriptions are partially accurate: it raised taxes relative to what the federal changes would have produced and lowered them for low-income workers.

- Senator Drazan’s list of new taxes matches the reporting. Published reporting could not confirm her figures of a $30 billion increase in taxes under Governor Kotek and a 77 percent increase in business taxes in one year, and this paper does not rely on them.
