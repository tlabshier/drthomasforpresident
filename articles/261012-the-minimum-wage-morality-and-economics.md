# 261012 - The Minimum Wage - Morality and Economics

# Honor Cannot Be Mandated: The Minimum Wage, the Heart, and the Market


by Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | 12 October 2026


In Chapter 7 of *Transforming America*, I wrote that “a minimum wage law says something about what a man’s work is owed.” That sentence is true. Every law says something moral. A law against theft says your property is yours. A law against murder says your life is not another man’s to take. And a minimum wage law says that a man’s work is owed at least a certain amount.


But a true statement can be put in the wrong place. The question is not whether a worker deserves a fair wage. He does, and God says so. The question is who should decide what a fair wage is, and whether the government’s sword is the right instrument for deciding it. I believe it is not. This essay explains why.


## What God requires of the man who hires


The Bible is not silent about wages. It is direct, and it is severe.


*“Thou shalt not defraud thy neighbour, neither rob him: the wages of him that is hired shall not abide with thee all night until the morning”* (Leviticus 19:13). *“Thou shalt not oppress an hired servant that is poor and needy… At his day thou shalt give him his hire, neither shall the sun go down upon it; for he is poor, and setteth his heart upon it”* (Deuteronomy 24:14–15). *“Woe unto him that… useth his neighbour’s service without wages, and giveth him not for his work”* (Jeremiah 22:13). Malachi lists those who *“oppress the hireling in his wages”* alongside sorcerers and adulterers (Malachi 3:5). James warns the rich that the wages they kept back by fraud cry out, and that the cries have reached the ears of the Lord of hosts (James 5:4). Paul tells masters to give their servants *“that which is just and equal; knowing that ye also have a Master in heaven”* (Colossians 4:1). And Christ Himself said, *“The labourer is worthy of his hire”* (Luke 10:7).


Notice what these verses condemn: fraud, withholding, delay, using a man’s labor and not paying him, oppressing the poor because he is poor. They condemn the employer who breaks his word or takes advantage of a man’s weakness. They command the employer to be just, and they remind him that he has a Master who is watching.


Notice also what they do not do. They do not set a price. Nowhere does the Law of Moses fix a wage per day for every kind of work in Israel. Christ told a parable about this very question. A householder hired laborers at dawn for a penny a day, then hired more at the third, sixth, ninth, and eleventh hours. At evening he paid them all a penny. The men who had worked all day complained. The householder answered: *“Friend, I do thee no wrong: didst not thou agree with me for a penny?… Is it not lawful for me to do what I will with mine own? Is thine eye evil, because I am good?”* (Matthew 20:13–15).


The parable is about grace, not labor policy. But the frame Christ chose for it tells us how He saw the employer’s place. The wage was agreed between the two men. Keeping that agreement was justice. Paying more than the agreement required was the owner’s generosity, and it was his to give.


## The test: is it lawful? If so, is it loving?


I test every policy with two questions, in order. First, is it lawful: is it righteous, does it conform to what God has established? Second, if it is lawful, is it loving: would a neighbor, treated this way in the public square, feel loved by it, in the way Christ loved us?


Apply the first question to the employer. When he withholds an agreed wage, delays it, lies about it, or cheats on the measure, he has stolen. That is a crime against property, the same as taking money from a man’s pocket. The government is right to punish it. Wage fraud should be prosecuted, and the worker should be made whole. On this point I want to be clear: I am not arguing that the state has no role in wages. It has exactly the role God gave it, which is to restrain theft.


But when an employer offers a wage, and a man freely accepts it, no one has been robbed. The wage may be low. It may be lower than a generous man would pay. The employer may be sinning against love by paying it, if he could pay more and his worker is in need. But a failure of generosity is not theft. And as I argued in Chapter 5 of *Transforming America*, the state’s sword is for crimes against person and property. Everything else belongs to the culture: to conscience, family, church, neighbor, and the free choices of free men.


So the minimum wage law fails the first test. It treats a free agreement between two men as a crime. It takes a matter of the heart, generosity, honor, and the respect a man owes the one who serves him, and turns it into a matter for the police.


## What a minimum wage actually does


A minimum wage law does not make any man’s labor worth more. It makes it illegal to hire a man whose labor is worth less.


That distinction is the whole issue. An employer can pay a man only out of what that man’s work brings in. If a young man with no skills and no work history can produce ten dollars of value in an hour, and the law says he must be paid fifteen, he will not be paid fifteen. He will be paid nothing, because no one will hire him. The law did not raise his wage. It set his wage at zero and called it fairness.


The men who lose in this exchange are the least qualified: the teenager looking for his first job, the man coming out of prison, the man who dropped out of school, the immigrant who doesn’t yet speak English well, the man with a disability, the man re-entering work after years away. They are the ones whose first job matters most, because the first job is where a man learns to show up on time, take instruction, and finish what he starts. A minimum wage cuts off the bottom rung of the ladder, and the men standing on the ground are the ones who can’t climb.


How large is this effect? Here I want to state the evidence in proportion, because I do not want to win an argument by overstating it. Economists disagree about the size of the employment loss from modest increases. Some well-known studies, beginning with David Card and Alan Krueger’s study of New Jersey fast-food restaurants in the 1990s, found little or no job loss. Others found real harm. When Seattle raised its minimum wage to $13, researchers at the University of Washington found that low-wage workers lost enough hours that their average monthly earnings fell. When the Congressional Budget Office analyzed a proposal in 2021 to raise the federal minimum to $15 an hour, it estimated the bill would raise pay for millions of workers and lift about 900,000 people out of poverty, and that it would also cost about 1.4 million workers their jobs.


So both things are true at once. Some workers gain a raise. Others lose the job entirely. The law takes from the weakest to give to those slightly stronger. It does not ask the employer, the customer, or the public to pay for the raise. It asks the man who can no longer find work.


Even if the job losses were smaller than CBO estimated, my argument would not change. The question is not only how many men are hurt. It is who has the right to decide what a man’s work is worth.


There is also a part of this history most people have never heard. When minimum wage laws were first promoted in America, in the early twentieth century, some of their leading advocates openly said the purpose was to keep the “unemployable” out of work: immigrants, women, and men they judged inferior. The economist Thomas Leonard documented this in *Illiberal Reformers* (2016). I do not say this to accuse anyone who supports a minimum wage today of that motive. Most supporters sincerely want to help the poor. I say it because those early advocates understood what the law does more clearly than its modern friends do. They knew it would shut the least productive men out of work. They wanted that result. We should not want it.


## No central authority knows what labor is worth


Ludwig von Mises, Friedrich Hayek, and Milton Friedman each saw a different side of this problem.


Hayek’s insight was about knowledge. The information needed to price anything correctly is scattered among millions of people, each knowing his own small corner: this shop’s margins, this town’s rents, this worker’s reliability, this season’s demand. No central office can gather it all. Prices are how that scattered knowledge gets communicated. A wage is the price of labor, and when a central authority fixes it, it overrides the knowledge of everyone who actually knows the situation. One federal number is supposed to fit a diner in Manhattan and a hardware store in a small Montana town, a seasoned welder and a sixteen-year-old stocking shelves.


Mises’s insight was about what happens next. When government fixes a price and the result is a shortage, in this case a shortage of jobs for the least skilled, the government rarely repeals the price. It adds another intervention to fix the damage of the first: subsidies, training programs, public employment, wage supplements. Each one requires another. The economy moves step by step toward central management, not because anyone planned it but because each fix creates the next problem.


Friedman’s insight was about who pays. He argued for decades that the minimum wage is one of the laws that most harms the people it claims to help, especially the young and the poor, because it takes away their chance to be hired at the wage their present skills can earn.


In Chapter 2 of *Transforming America*, I described the Soviet planner who never knew how many shoelaces to make, because he had abolished the owners whose yes and no would have told him. A minimum wage is not communism. Most American wages are set freely and far above the legal floor. But it uses the same instrument communism uses for everything: a central authority declaring by fiat what a thing is worth, regardless of what the people involved know and agree to. It is a step in that direction, and steps in that direction tend to be followed by more.


## Honor that is compelled is no longer honor


Now the second question: is the minimum wage loving?


Its supporters believe it is. They want to honor the worker. But consider what honor is. When an employer pays a man well because he values that man, because he has watched him work, trusts him, and wants him to stay and prosper, that wage is an act of respect. The worker knows it. It says, *I see what you do for me, and I honor it.* That is how a man’s heart is built up by his work, and how an employer’s heart is trained in gratitude.


When the government compels the same wage, the respect disappears from the transaction. The employer pays because he must. The worker receives what the law says he is owed, not what his employer chose to give him. The relationship that God meant to train both men’s hearts, the master who knows he has a Master in heaven and the servant who serves *“as to the Lord”*, has been replaced by a statute. We have legislated respect, and in doing so we have taken away the one thing that made it respect: that it came freely from the heart.


I made the same argument about Social Security in Chapter 5. When the government takes from one man to give to another, the giver loses the character-building that comes from sacrificing for the poor, and the receiver loses the knowledge that someone cared for him. The same happens here. A law can force the outward act. It cannot force the honor, and without the honor, the act has lost most of its worth.


## What about the employer who won’t pay fairly?


The strongest objection is this: what about the employer who exploits his workers? Won’t some men pay as little as they can get away with?


Yes, some will. That is the sin Scripture condemns, and the condemnation stands whether or not a law punishes it. But the remedy that actually works is the one the market supplies when it is healthy: other employers. A worker who is underpaid can leave and take his skills to someone who will pay him more. Employers who want good workers must compete for them. That competition, not a federal floor, is why the great majority of American workers earn well above the minimum.


Where that remedy fails, it fails for a reason I discussed in Chapter 3: concentration. The company town, where one employer owns every job, can hold wages down because the worker has nowhere else to go. The answer to that problem is not a central price-setter. It is breaking the concentration, keeping many employers in every market, removing the licensing and regulatory barriers that keep small businesses from starting, and refusing the government favors that let one large employer crowd out the rest.


And beyond the market, there is the accountability Christ intended his people to exercise. A Christian employer should hear from his brothers if he is grinding his workers down. His pastor should tell him what James 5 says. His customers can take their business elsewhere. His own conscience, if it has been trained, will tell him first. These are the distributed defenses of a healthy society, the immune system I described in Chapter 3. They are slower and less tidy than a law. But they change hearts, and a law does not.


## What I propose


I advocate the following, and I hold it open to a better argument.


First, the federal government should get out of setting wages. I would end the federal minimum wage mandate and return the question to the states and, ultimately, to employers and workers. States that keep their own minimums will become a visible test of which approach serves the poor better.


Second, the government should prosecute wage fraud vigorously. Withholding agreed wages, falsifying hours, misclassifying workers to avoid paying what was promised: these are theft, and theft is the state’s business.


Third, I call on employers, and especially Christian employers, to pay as men who will give an account. Pay what is just and equal. Pay more when you can, because your worker is your neighbor and you know what he needs. Make room at the bottom rung for the man nobody else will hire, the way Israel’s farmers left the corners of their fields for the poor to glean with their own hands. That is how a man is honored: not by a statute, but by another man who chose to see him.


Fourth, I call on the churches to teach what the Bible says about wages, plainly and often, and to hold their members to it. If we want a nation where workers are paid fairly, we need a nation of employers whose hearts are just. No law can build that. Only the Gospel can.


The laborer is worthy of his hire. On that, everyone agrees. The question is whether we will honor him from the heart, or let the government pretend to do it for us while it quietly closes the door on the men who need work most.
