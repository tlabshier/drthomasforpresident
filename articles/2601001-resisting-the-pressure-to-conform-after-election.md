# Staying Unbought: How a Pioneer Keeps His Positions After He Gains Power

*An analysis prompted by Robert W. Malone and Jill Glasspool Malone, "The Kennedy Exception" (Malone News, September 21, 2026)*

---

## Summary

Every reform movement eventually faces the same test: its leader wins a position of power, and the people who sent him there must decide whether they will hold him to the standards he once applied to others. The Malones' essay argues that the medical freedom movement is failing that test with Robert F. Kennedy Jr., Secretary of Health and Human Services. This paper does not try to settle the vaccine questions behind the dispute. It uses the case to ask a broader question: how does a man who arrives in Washington as a reformer avoid being captured by its personalities, its money, and its culture, and how do his supporters keep from being captured by him?

The argument here is that capture rarely comes as a bribe. It comes as a series of reasonable-sounding accommodations, each defended as patience, realism, or loyalty, while the man's surroundings slowly change what he hears and whom he wants to please. The defenses against it must therefore be set in place *before* the pressure arrives: written commitments, a public ledger of what was promised and what was done, an honest distinction between "I cannot" and "I will not," a resignation line drawn in advance, protection for the people who actually carry out the mandate, a financial firewall, and the deliberate cultivation of friends who will tell him he is wrong. The movement needs a matching discipline: judge the leader by the same evidence it would demand of an opponent.

---

## 1. The Question

The pioneer is defined by positions he took when holding them cost him something. He was outside the gates. His credibility came from saying what powerful people did not want said. Then he is handed the keys. Now the same positions cost him differently: they cost him standing with the president who appointed him, goodwill with colleagues, the cooperation of the bureaucracy, and the approval of allies who would rather celebrate than question.

So the question is not whether a man in power should ever change his mind. A man who learns something new and changes his view for stated reasons is doing exactly what honest inquiry requires. The question is how to tell *that* kind of change from capture, and how a man can arrange his life and office so that only the first kind happens.

---

## 2. The Case as the Malones Present It

The essay's charge can be summarized in four parts.

**The promises.** Before taking office, Kennedy's organization, Children's Health Defense (CHD), petitioned the FDA in 2021 to revoke the emergency authorizations for the COVID vaccines, and Kennedy called the COVID vaccine the "deadliest vaccine ever made." In September 2024, he endorsed Rep. Paul Gosar's bill to end the vaccine manufacturers' liability shield.

**The record in office.** Nineteen months in, the Malones note, the Pfizer and Moderna COVID mRNA vaccines remain licensed; NIH continues to fund mRNA research; the COVID PREP Act liability declaration remains in force through 2029, although the Secretary can amend such a declaration prospectively and HHS used that very authority in May 2026 for an Andes virus countermeasure; and in August 2026 the FDA approved Moderna's mFLUSIVA, the first mRNA influenza vaccine licensed in the United States. The widely cited $500 million BARDA mRNA cancellation of August 2025, they argue, was a narrow research pivot, and HHS's own announcement said other mRNA uses were unaffected.

**The personnel.** The officials most willing to restrict these products are gone. Vinay Prasad refused to accept Moderna's flu vaccine application, was reversed, and left FDA; Tracy Beth Høeg was dismissed; Commissioner Marty Makary resigned after, according to Washington Post reporting the essay cites, the White House had approved a plan to replace him. Meanwhile, Kennedy has praised nominees with conventional pro-vaccine records.

**The silence and the adulation.** Robert Malone, who served as vice-chair of ACIP's COVID vaccine workgroup, reports that a completed report on post-vaccination injury was to be presented in February 2026, that HHS failed to publish the required meeting notice, and that a senior HHS aide told him the White House did not want the issue aired before the midterms. Then, on September 17, Kennedy keynoted the CHD conference and told the audience they had a strong and steadfast friend in the White House, and was received, the Malones say, with praise rather than questions, including from Tony Lyons, whose publishing house publishes Kennedy's books and who co-chaired the super PAC that backed his campaign.

Their conclusion: "Accountability that stops at Bobby Kennedy's feet is not accountability. It is allegiance."
### What is documented and what rests on one account

Some of these facts are independently confirmed in public reporting. The FDA did approve mFLUSIVA for adults 50 and older in August 2026, after the agency first refused to file the application in February, reversed itself days later when Moderna publicly challenged the decision, and received a unanimous advisory committee recommendation in June. Associated Press reporting confirms Kennedy's September 17 keynote at CHD, his first appearance there as Secretary, and his "steadfast friend" line.

Other claims rest on the Malones' own account, chiefly what HHS aide Stefanie Spear is reported to have said about White House wishes regarding ACIP. The essay itself is careful to say no written directive has surfaced. These claims deserve to be taken seriously and investigated, but a fair reader holds them as testimony, not as established fact.

---

## 3. The Fairest Defense of Kennedy

An analysis that asks for equal standards owes Kennedy the best version of his defense. It runs roughly as follows.

- **He is not the final authority.** A Cabinet secretary serves at the President's pleasure. If the President does not want a fight over licensed vaccines before a midterm election, the Secretary can comply, persuade, or leave. Staying and winning smaller victories is a defensible choice.

- **Revoking a license is a legal process, not a decree.** Withdrawing an approved biologic requires evidentiary grounds and procedure that can be challenged in court. The courts have already blocked his revised childhood schedule and his reconstituted ACIP. A reformer who acts carelessly can lose the war in the courtroom.

- **He has done things no predecessor would have done.** He replaced the entire ACIP, narrowed routine COVID vaccine approvals, canceled the BARDA mRNA portfolio, and proposed changes to the vaccine injury compensation process. AP's coverage of the CHD speech actually read it as a *renewed* turn toward vaccine issues after a period focused on food policy.

- **Personnel turnover has many causes.** The essay itself concedes Makary had other disputes with the administration.

- **People may legitimately change their minds.** Kennedy's later praise of Operation Warp Speed may reflect a real change of assessment rather than surrender.

These points carry weight. They do not, however, answer the essay's central demand, which is not "do everything at once" but "explain." Several of the matters the Malones raise, such as the PREP Act declaration, are within the Secretary's own authority. Where a man has the power and has not used it, the honest answer is a reason, stated publicly, not an appeal to patience.
### The same standard applies to the critics

The essay's own principle cuts both ways. Robert Malone is a former ACIP member who resigned in frustration, an author whose workgroup report was shelved, and the publisher of a paid newsletter whose business model rewards controversy. None of that makes him wrong. It means his account should be checked the same way he asks readers to check Tony Lyons's praise: not dismissed because of the interest, but weighed with it in view. A movement that adopts the essay's test should apply it to the essay too.

---

## 4. How Capture Actually Works

The word "captured" suggests a single moment of surrender. In practice, capture is a gradual change in what a man sees, whom he answers to, and what he wants. Six mechanisms do most of the work.

**1. The reference group changes.** Before office, the pioneer's peers were fellow dissenters and the people he served. In office, his daily company is the President's staff, agency heads, lawyers, lobbyists, and reporters. Without anyone intending it, *their* approval becomes the approval he notices. What once felt like obvious duty starts to feel like a provocation that would embarrass people he now sees every day.

**2. Dependence on a principal.** An appointed officer holds power on loan. The President's priorities, including electoral timing, become constraints he must work within. This is legitimate, but it creates a constant temptation to present the principal's choice as his own conviction, or to stay silent about the difference. The Malones' account of the midterm calendar, if accurate, is a textbook case.

**3. Money and patronage.** Capture by money is rarely a payment for a decision. It is a web of relationships in which the people who praise a leader also profit from his success: book contracts, speaking circuits, PACs, consulting. Scripture states the mechanism exactly: "thou shalt take no gift: for the gift blindeth the wise, and perverteth the words of the righteous" (Exodus 23:8). The gift does not purchase the verdict; it blinds the judge.

**4. Flattery and the courtier problem.** *"A man that flattereth his neighbour spreadeth a net for his feet"* **(Proverbs 29:5)**. Adulation is pleasant, and the man who is surrounded by it gradually loses access to the information he most needs. Nobody wants to spoil the standing ovation. This is capture *by one's own side*, and it is the essay's deepest point: the leader can be captured by the establishment and by his admirers at the same time, and the second makes the first invisible.

**5. Filtering by personnel.** Who is hired, kept, and let go determines what reaches the leader's desk. When the people who press hardest for the mandate leave and the people comfortable with the status quo stay, the information environment shifts, whether anyone planned it or not. A leader's protection of his own most committed subordinates is one of the most reliable measures of whether he is still pursuing his original aim.

**6. The rationalization of the long game.** Every compromise can be defended as preserving capital for a larger victory later. Sometimes that is true. But "later" has no natural end point, and the larger victory rarely comes on its own. Without a stated deadline, patience becomes indistinguishable from abandonment.

The oldest account of this in the biblical record is Saul's. When confronted for disobeying a clear command, he gave the reason plainly: "I feared the people, and obeyed their voice" (1 Samuel 15:24). Capture by the crowd is as real as capture by the palace.

## 5. Disciplines for the Man in Office

Because capture works by slow accommodation, the defenses must be structural and set in place before the pressure arrives. Willpower in the moment is not enough. The following are offered as practices any reformer could adopt; they are proposals held open to better ones.
### 5.1 Write the commitments down before taking office

A pioneer should enter office with a short, specific, public list: what he will do, by roughly when, and what would count as success. "Make vaccines safer" cannot be measured. "Amend the PREP Act declaration for product X within twelve months, or explain publicly why not" can be.
### 5.2 Keep a public ledger

At regular intervals, publish the list with a status beside each item: done, in progress, blocked, or abandoned, with the reason. The ledger converts vague loyalty into an evidence question that supporters and critics alike can answer. It also protects the officeholder: a reasonable explanation, offered early, is far more persuasive than a defense assembled after an essay like the Malones' appears.
### 5.3 Distinguish "I cannot" from "I will not"

Many promises do require Congress, the courts, or the President. A leader who says so plainly, naming the constraint, keeps his integrity. The damage comes when a choice ("I will not, because the President prefers otherwise" or "I have changed my mind") is presented as an impossibility. Honest speech requires saying which is which. If his view has changed, he should say what evidence changed it.
### 5.4 Draw the resignation line in advance

Every appointed reformer should decide, privately and before taking office, which commitments are so central that being forbidden to pursue them would require him to leave. When the principal forbids the core work, there are three honest options: persuade, comply and disclose the disagreement, or resign. Silence is the fourth option, and it is the one that leads to capture. Resignation is not failure; it is sometimes the only way to keep the public's trust in what one said.
### 5.5 Protect the people who carry out the mandate

Reformers seldom fall alone. They fall when the subordinates who push their agenda are removed one at a time. A leader who means what he said will spend political capital defending those people, or will explain publicly why he did not. Their survival is a fair outward measure of his intent.
### 5.6 Build a financial firewall

No business relationships with those whose praise matters to his standing. No book contracts, paid speeches, or ventures with allies while in office. Full disclosure of existing ties. Supporters should ask the same question of a friendly publisher that they would ask of a pharmaceutical sponsor.
### 5.7 Keep a Nathan

When David had done great wrong, the prophet Nathan told him, *"Thou art the man"* **(2 Samuel 12:7)**. Every man in power needs at least one person whose role is to say what no one else will, and who loses nothing by saying it. *"Faithful are the wounds of a friend; but the kisses of an enemy are deceitful"* **(Proverbs 27:6)**. In practice, this means appointing a few advisers explicitly chosen for independence, meeting with critics on a schedule, and treating dissent from allies as information rather than disloyalty.
### 5.8 Take questions in friendly rooms

Every appearance before one's own supporters should include open questions on the hardest issues. A leader who returns to friendly audiences only for applause is training himself, and them, to stop asking. The CHD conference would have been the ideal place for him to explain the PREP Act decision in his own words.
### 5.9 Stay rooted outside the capital

The culture of Washington shapes a man through daily rhythm: who he eats with, where he spends weekends, whose opinion of him he checks first. Deliberate habits push back: returning home, keeping old friendships with people who have nothing to gain from him, rest and worship, and a self-imposed limit on years in office. The American model is Washington himself, who laid down power twice when he could have kept it.
### 5.10 Make the process public

Meeting notices published on time, postponements explained, reports released. Procedure is where quiet capture most often hides. Treat a postponed meeting with no stated reason as a decision that requires one.

## 6. Disciplines for the Movement

Followers can be captured too. A movement that grew up demanding evidence can, once its hero holds power, begin crediting every success to him and every failure to his enemies. The Malones are right that this is the moment a movement either keeps its principles or trades them for a person.

**The name-swap test.** The essay's most useful tool is a simple substitution: describe the record without the name, attach an opponent's name, and ask whether you would accept the explanation. If the answer changes with the name, the standard was never the evidence.

**Judge by outcomes against stated promises.** Applause for intentions is easy. The question is what was promised, what authority existed, and what was done.

**Apply the money question to friends.** Weigh praise from someone with financial ties to the leader with those ties in view, exactly as the movement taught people to weigh praise from industry.

**Separate support for a man from support for a cause.** A person may admire Kennedy and still insist on answers. Asking him is not betrayal. Refusing to ask him is how a movement loses its ability to correct itself, and how it fails him: the leader who hears only reassurance is the leader most likely to fail.

---

## 7. The Moral Foundation

The test for any public act is whether a neighbor would feel loved by it. Accountability, rightly understood, passes that test in both directions.

For the *public*, a leader who keeps his word, or explains honestly why he could not, treats citizens as adults entitled to the truth about the power they lent him. A leader who lets a small achievement be celebrated as a large one is telling a lie by proportion, even if every individual statement is accurate.

For the *leader*, honest questioning is a form of care. Flattery isolates him, and isolation is where good men go wrong. The friend who asks the hard question is the one who loves him.

For the *movement*, holding its own leader to its own standard is what makes its earlier criticism of others credible. Principles that apply only to opponents were never principles.

None of this requires deciding whether Kennedy's critics or his defenders are right about the vaccines. It requires only that the questions be asked of him in public, and answered.

---

## 8. Application: A Pledge for Any Reformer Seeking Office

If a candidate wishes to be trusted with power on the strength of positions he has taken, he could make commitments like these before the first vote is cast:

- I will publish a specific list of my commitments, and a public ledger of their status, at regular intervals.

- When I do not keep a commitment, I will say whether I could not or chose not to, and why.

- If I change my mind, I will say what evidence changed it.

- I have decided in advance which commitments I would resign rather than abandon.

- I will defend, or publicly explain my failure to defend, the people who carry out my mandate.

- I will take no gifts, contracts, or business from those whose praise affects my standing, and I will disclose existing ties.

- I will keep advisers whose job is to tell me I am wrong, and I will meet with my critics.

- I will take open questions whenever I speak to my own supporters.

- I will limit my own time in office.

- I invite every supporter to apply to me the same standard of evidence he would apply to my opponent.

These are offered as a starting point. Better versions are welcome.

---

## 9. Conclusion

The pioneer's danger in power is not usually that he will sell out. It is that he will be slowly persuaded, by people he likes and circumstances that seem reasonable, that the positions which defined him can wait. The defense is to make waiting visible: to write down what he promised, report what he has done, name the reason for every gap, and keep around him people who love him enough to ask. The movement's defense is the same in reverse: keep asking, and treat a friend in power exactly as you would a stranger.

As the Malones put it, the real test of speaking truth to power comes when the power is held by your friends.
