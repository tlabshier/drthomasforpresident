# 261111 - Trust in Voting Results and Immigration Statistics

# How Would We Know?


### Truth, Trust, and the Count

**By Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | 11 November 2026 **

*Source: Developed from a conversation with my friend and business partner Charlie Gutierrez, 11 November 2026.
Edited by Claude for clarity from the call transcript, with research notes added.*


---


## The Question Under the Question

A week after the 2026 midterms, Charlie told me how the election looked to him from Bakersfield: it stinks. He considers himself a normal person with ordinary common sense. He is frustrated by the price of gas, but to him that matters less than stopping the Islamic regime in Iran from getting a weapon and oppressing its own people. He thinks the economy is doing well. So when the country handed both houses of Congress to the Democrats, and California elected Xavier Becerra governor by a wide margin after Steve Hilton beat him in the debates, Charlie could not make it add up. His gut says there was widespread cheating, the same way he believes 2020 was stolen.

Many Americans feel exactly what Charlie feels. Beneath the particular charges (machines, illegal ballots, Venezuela) is a deeper question, and I think it is the one that matters: **how would we know?** If the count were honest, how could an ordinary citizen be sure? If it were not, how could he prove it?

Here is where I stand. **I stand with elections that can prove themselves.** I have not found evidence that the 2026 results were stolen, and I will not claim what I cannot show. But a system that asks citizens to trust it, rather than letting them verify it, has a weakness that honest people are right to worry about. Below I lay out what I found, where the uncertainty lies, and what I think should change.

## How I Try to Find Out What Is True

I want to be candid about my method, because it shapes everything I conclude.

Every day, articles and claims come across my desk: a Facebook post, a Substack essay, a podcast, a story my wife Margo heard. I do not have an inside source or an advisory panel. I have the AIs. When I get a story about a stolen election, I run it through Claude and ask directly: is this true? I also tell it, in so many words: you do not have access to all the conservative blogs and testimony; is it possible you are missing something? Look for the blind spots. And I ask it to list every source it used, so I can see where its picture comes from.

Every time, the answer comes back the same: I am trying to find it, but I don't see the blind spot; these claims don't hold up.

I take that seriously, but I do not take it as final. When you talk to an AI, you are talking to something like the group mind. It sees far more of the world's information than I do, but it is a curated slice: the mainstream record plus some alternative media. That is not God's position. Bias is not quite the right word for this; it is a limitation, and both Claude and I acknowledge it up front. The best I can do is to grade the evidence by its kind, look deliberately for the strongest dissenting case, and say plainly where the gaps are.

Truth is not a partisan project. It is simply what is. The question is how to determine it.

## What the Record Shows

**The margins.** Nationally, Democrats took control of both the House and the Senate. In California, Becerra won by roughly 64% to 36%. Charlie is right that one good thing happened: the 5% one-time tax on billionaires' net worth (Proposition 40) was losing by about ten points as the count continued, while a competing measure to bar such wealth taxes was leading. Voters could see billionaires packing up to leave in anticipation.

Margins that large matter for the fraud question. A stolen election needs enough illegitimate votes to cover the gap. Covering a twenty-eight-point gap in California, or a national sweep, would take an operation of enormous size, and large operations leave large traces.

**Noncitizen voting.** It exists. That is not a rumor; it is a matter of criminal charges. On 19 October, the Justice Department charged 28 green-card holders in California, 21 of them accused of actually casting ballots, and federal prosecutors said about 113 noncitizens had been charged in 20 states over the previous two months. Every one of those votes cancels a citizen's vote, and each is a real wrong.

The administration also flagged more than 20,000 California registrants as likely noncitizens and asked the courts to set their mail ballots aside. A district judge, the Ninth Circuit, and then the Supreme Court all refused, and the state said the list was full of errors. The Supreme Court gave no reasoning. I note the limit of what this proves. The courts decided that the ballots should not be blocked on that list's evidence; they did not certify that all 20,000 were citizens. Still, 28 prosecuted cases out of 20,000 flagged names, against margins in the millions, does not describe a stolen election.
> **Note on Charlie's figures.** Charlie remembered the number as 50,000 or 20,000 ballots. It was about 20,000.

**The "Chavez machines."** Charlie believes Venezuela's dictator started an electronic vote-rigging scheme in the early 2000s that was imported into California, and that California has not had a Republican majority since. There is a factual seed here. Smartmatic did get early financing and its first major election contract from Hugo Chávez's government in 2004. But in the 2020 election, Smartmatic's only U.S. client was Los Angeles County, where it built ballot-marking devices that print a paper ballot; its systems there did not count the votes. Fox News paid $787.5 million to settle a defamation suit over false claims about voting machines, in that case Dominion's. And California's one-party rule has a simpler explanation in party registration: the state last elected a Republican governor, Arnold Schwarzenegger, in 2006 and has had a Democratic trifecta since 2011. Voter registration in California is heavily Democratic. That is a moral and educational problem, but it is not evidence of rigged machines.

**The Stalin line.** We both quoted the line about how it doesn't matter who votes, only who counts the votes. The attribution comes from the memoir of Stalin's former secretary, Boris Bazhanov, about voting inside the Communist Party in 1923, and most historians doubt it. The principle is much older than Stalin; American newspapers were printing versions of it in 1890. The principle is still true, whoever first said it.

## Why the Concern Remains Plausible

If the record on outcomes is that clear, why do so many sensible people, Charlie among them, still believe the count cannot be trusted? I do not think the answer is simply that they have been misled. Several real features of our system invite the suspicion.

**The mail-ballot chain of custody is weak.** If you go in person and show your ID, it is hard to cheat. When millions of ballots are mailed out and we simply trust that the person who returns each one is the person entitled to vote, the chain of custody has a gap. It is like a teacher letting a student take the test outside, ask his friends for the answers, and come back in, and then asking him, "Did you answer all of these yourself?" "Yes, I did, teacher." Ballot harvesting, where someone collects a batch of ballots and turns them in, widens that gap. I am not saying the gap was exploited in 2026 on a scale that changed anything. I am saying a system with such a gap cannot prove it wasn't exploited.

**California forbids its cities from asking for ID.** Charlie told me it is illegal in California even to ask for ID, and that is close to the truth. In 2024, the state passed SB 1174, which bars local governments from requiring identification at the polls. It was written in direct response to Huntington Beach, whose voters had approved a local voter-ID requirement. An appeals court struck down the Huntington Beach measure in 2025, and the California Supreme Court declined to review it in January 2026. Officials say California's elections are already fair, safe, and secure. That may be so. But a state that goes to court to stop a city from checking ID has chosen to make verification harder, and it should not be surprised when citizens draw conclusions from that choice. It may be virtue signaling about the sacred right to vote; it is not how you would design a system if integrity were your first concern.

**Popular safeguards keep failing.** The SAVE America Act, requiring proof of citizenship to register and photo ID to vote, passed the House in February 2026 and was blocked in the Senate. Charlie remembered it as having 86% approval from both sides. The real picture is more mixed. Gallup in 2024 found 83% support for proof of citizenship at registration. A Harvard CAPS/Harris poll in March 2026 found 74% support, including 61% of Democrats. But an Economist/YouGov poll the same month found 91% of Republicans in favor and Democrats split, 35% for and 53% against. In some polls, 86% of Republicans appear in favor. What is solid is that photo ID and proof of citizenship are popular with majorities, and that Congress still did not enact them. When the people want a safeguard, and their representatives refuse it, suspicion is a reasonable response.

**Official denials are interested testimony.** When election officials tell us the system is secure, they are vouching for their own work. That does not make them wrong, but it does make their word one witness among several, not the final judge. Opponents of the SAVE Act raise a real point: some eligible citizens would struggle to produce documents, and in one poll 9% of Democrats said so, against 3% of Republicans. Notice that this cuts both ways. Each party can see how a rule would help or hurt it, which is exactly why neither party's assurances should settle the matter. Verification should.

It's also worth noting that suspicion runs in both directions. In one poll before the midterms, 66% of Republicans said Democrats were trying to steal the 2026 election, and 77% of Democrats said the same of Republicans. A country in which most voters on both sides think the other side cheats has a problem that more reassurance will not fix.

Scripture puts the principle simply: *"A false balance is abomination to the LORD: but a just weight is his delight"* **(Proverbs 11:1)**. The count is the nation's scale. It is not enough for the scale to be honest; the merchant must let the customer see the weights.

## Both Things Can Be True

Charlie and I came at the election from different angles, and I think both are partly right.

My first instinct was that the people who decide elections are not people like Charlie, who think hard and hold firm views. Elections are decided by independents: the people who honestly do not know whether conservative or liberal is right, whether there is a God, whether boys belong in girls' bathrooms, whether a woman may end her child's life. Many of them vote their stomach or their pocketbook. They are moved by the price of gas, by which yard signs they see more of, by television ads telling them one candidate is a crook and the other a saint. I do not say this from superiority. When I sit down with a voter guide and see the long write-ups on every candidate, I am overwhelmed too. I have a life to live, so I vote the party line because I trust the platform, and I hope the people on the ticket really support it. And this year the Democrats and their allies in the media laid down a heavy smoke screen, painting Trump and the Republicans as a dumpster fire.

Charlie asked me how someone as young as I am could be so cynical. It does not feel like cynicism to me. It feels like reality. And it is the reason for my candidacy: if the deciding voter is uninformed and unphilosophical, then someone has to put the moral questions in front of him, plainly and with reasons.

Charlie's angle is that my analysis may be beside the point if the people counting the ballots have already settled the outcome. Suppose most people think clearly and see how the state is being run into the ground, and suppose the independents agree, and still it makes no difference because of who counts the votes. Charlie says the elections in California have made no sense for decades, so it is impossible to tell how many people are voting sensibly.

These two views are not rivals. The swing voter may decide elections, and the count may still need to be made verifiable. The first calls for teaching. The second calls for reform.

## What Would Let Us Know

So I return to the question: what would it take for a citizen to know that the result reflects the will of the people? This is my current thinking, held open to a better argument.


 	- **A paper ballot of record for every vote.** If we are going to use machines, and it looks as if we are, then every machine count should be checked against the paper. Run the two in parallel and audit a public random sample by hand. If the machine and the paper agree, the machine has proved itself.

 	- **Inspectable code.** Voting-machine software should be open to qualified independent examination. With what AI can now do in reading and testing code, it should be possible to establish that the software is clean.

 	- **In-person voting with ID as the norm.** Mail ballots should be kept for those who need them: the military, the disabled, the ill, and those away from home. A ballot cast in person, by an identified citizen, has an unbroken chain of custody.

 	- **Proof of citizenship at registration**, applied in a way that does not shut out eligible citizens who have trouble finding documents.

 	- **No ballot harvesting.** A ballot should go from the voter to the election office, not through a third party collecting them in batches.


Charlie brought up a striking example from Switzerland. In two cantons, Glarus and Appenzell Innerrhoden, citizens still gather once a year in the town square for the *Landsgemeinde*, an open-air assembly dating to the 14th century, and vote on laws and officials by a show of hands. When a show of hands is not clear, officials make an exact count. Neighbors vote in front of neighbors. Charlie's point is that cheating in such a system is nearly impossible, and that a civil society ought to be made of people willing to be open about their opinions; if everyone is open, no one can be singled out and persecuted.

I had never heard of it, and I find the principle compelling: transparency is the enemy of fraud. But I would weigh two things against it. First, it would only work at our scale with a national holiday, so that everyone could go; the infrastructure would shut down. Second, the secret ballot was not adopted on a whim. American states adopted it in the late 1880s and 1890s largely to stop vote buying and employer intimidation of workers. A man whose vote is public can be paid for it or punished for it. So I would take the Swiss lesson as a principle (make the count visible to the people) rather than as a blueprint for national elections. As Charlie said, honest elections would have to be held sacred for something like that to happen. Today they are not, and that is what has to change first.

## A Second Case: The Immigration Stories

The same problem of knowing what is true came up when we talked about immigration.

My impression was that the administration was going soft. Margo, who listens to podcasts and testimonies all day, told me a story: Greg Bovino, the hard-line Border Patrol commander who meant to remove everyone who came in illegally, had been fired by Trump, and Markwayne Mullin, whom she saw as soft, had been put in charge. I have seen the meme more than once that ICE is abusive, and I had heard that Trump said it was just too hard to get them all out and was talking about amnesty. I told Charlie I could not verify any of it.

Charlie had heard the opposite. Someone he knows works at Bakersfield's Meadows Field airport, and he says big jets come in nearly every day, sometimes twice, to pick up or drop off detainees from a nearby detention center. She has been working three or four extra hours a day to handle the ICE flights. Charlie said deportations are moving forward quietly, without much press, and that they definitely haven't slowed down. He also pointed to Stephen Miller's elevation to White House chief of staff as a public sign that the President is still committed to deportations.

When Charlie looked up the Bovino story during our call, it came back as inaccurate compared with how it had been told to me. Here is what I found in the record afterward:


 	- Bovino was removed from his commander-at-large role in January 2026 after agents in operations he oversaw in Minneapolis fatally shot two U.S. citizens, and he returned to his post as chief of the El Centro sector. He retired in March. He was not replaced by Mullin. Mullin became Secretary of Homeland Security in March, replacing Kristi Noem.

 	- Since retiring, Bovino has become a loud public critic of Mullin, accusing him of undermining the mass-deportation agenda, and Mullin has faced a backlash from immigration hardliners who think parts of his approach are too lenient. Margo's story, then, had a real basis: the movement is fighting over how far enforcement should go.

 	- On *60 Minutes* on 8 November, Trump said that many illegal immigrants who hold stable jobs and pay taxes should be allowed to stay. Immigration hawks called that amnesty. Mullin has said amnesty will never happen.

 	- Stephen Miller was in fact named chief of staff after the midterms.

 	- Meanwhile, removal flights have run at record levels. An independent flight-tracking project counted 245 deportation flights in April 2026 alone, a monthly record, and nearly 900,000 people had been deported under Trump by June. Charlie's firsthand report fits that pattern. I haven't independently confirmed the Meadows Field details, so I treat them as a lead, but they align with the national numbers.


So both stories were partly true. Enforcement has been happening at a record pace, quietly. And the political commitment to finishing the job is wavering after the midterm losses. If the enforcement is real and the public believes it has stopped, then at least part of what happened was a failure to communicate. As Charlie said, there is so much manipulation and spinning of events that it is very hard to discover what is true.

On ICE itself, I will state my position. The officers who carry out this work are putting their lives on the line to remove people we let walk across the border by the millions, and removing even one of them takes enormous resources. Agents have reported vehicles being driven at them. Every use of force should be judged on its own facts, and where an officer did wrong he should answer for it. But I reject the picture of these men as a criminal organization. I consider them heroes, and I think we owe every one of them our thanks.

## If I Were Actually Elected

This conversation brought me to the thought that frightens me most about this whole endeavor. Suppose the one-in-81-million lottery actually happened and I were elected. How would I govern?

Everything I have worked out so far rests on the back of the AIs. I depend on their eyes, ears, and analysis. Perhaps sworn into office, I would have better resources. But from what I can see, people in that position end up surrounded by yes-men, partisans, people sold out to industry, and experts who are all over the map about what is true. Would I sit at my desk typing prompts, saying Claude recommends this, OpenAI says that, and Perplexity says something else? A man writing blogs can afford to be wrong. A president makes decisions. That is why I am trying to think through every situation I can imagine now, gathering every perspective I can find (this person's blog, that person's website, what the Bible says) and asking what a righteous leader would actually do.

Charlie has thought about this too, and his answer is the best one I have heard. First, he would pray constantly and fast for God's wisdom. Second, he would have a different American family at every lunch and every dinner, so he would hear about the real world from people who live in it rather than from people whose whole lives revolve around power. He does not see how anyone could keep a clear view of the world surrounded only by Washington insiders. A continual dialogue with ordinary people from all over the country would keep a president grounded. I intend to take both pieces of that advice.

## The Law

Near the start of our talk about the campaign, Charlie urged me to return to Frédéric Bastiat's *The Law*, which I read years ago. It is a short book, written in 1850, and Charlie calls it beautiful and basic. Bastiat asks what government is and what its proper role is, and he warns against what he called legal plunder: using the law to take from one person and give to another, which is theft made legal. The government does that quite a lot.

Charlie's summary is worth recording. Government is the force of a gun. Aimed properly, always at the wrongdoer and never at the innocent, it makes life predictable. People can form proper expectations, and they do not feel they must hide their money from both the criminals on the street and the government. That, Charlie says, is where Californians are now: stuck between thieves and the state. He registers his car in South Dakota for that reason; thieves steal catalytic converters, and he cannot keep up with the state's demands for replacing them. Everyone who can afford to leave California is leaving. If people understood Bastiat's principles, Charlie says, they would see the world differently, and they would see America with much more optimism.

He is right that this belongs among my major essays: a condensed, easily digested statement of what law is for. It also answers the question this whole essay has circled. A government that confines its force to defending life, liberty, and property has nothing to hide, and it has no reason to resist letting citizens verify the count. I will write that essay next.

## Sources and Their Limits

My sourcing for this essay leans on the mainstream record: wire services, major newspapers, court rulings, official announcements, and nonpartisan election groups, along with a few conservative outlets. Curation could have hidden evidence. If primary evidence of outcome-changing fraud in 2026 never reached these sources, I would not have seen it, and I would welcome it. Official statements (from election officials, the Justice Department, and the White House alike) are interested-party testimony and are treated that way here. Polling on voter ID and proof of citizenship varies widely with wording and sponsor. The Meadows Field report is firsthand but secondhand to me, and I treat it as a lead. I call a claim inaccurate because primary records contradict it, not because an outlet called it false.
