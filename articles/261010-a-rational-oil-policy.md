# 261010 - A Rational Oil Policy

# Neither Addiction Nor Idol: Oil, Gratitude, and Counting the Cost


**A Position Paper on Energy Policy**

*Dr Thomas for President 2028*; 10 *October 2026*


*Responding to [“The 6,000 Reasons the World Can’t Simply Quit Oil”](https://newswithviews.com/the-6000-reasons-the-world-cant-simply-quit-oil/) by Ronald Stein, PE, and Yoshihiro Muronaka, P.E. Jp (September 2026)*


---


## The Question Stein Asks


Ronald Stein and Yoshihiro Muronaka ask a fair question. People now say they’re “addicted” to smartphones and social media, and some say the same about oil. Is our use of petroleum an addiction, or merely a lifestyle choice we could set aside if we cared enough?


They answer: neither. They call it *functional necessity*. Crude oil, coal, and natural gas are useless and even dangerous in their raw state. Refineries and chemical plants turn them into transportation fuels and thousands of products: plastics, synthetic rubber, fertilizer, pharmaceuticals, lubricants, asphalt, and the materials that make wind turbines and solar panels. Wind and solar generate electricity, but electricity alone does not fly a jet, pave a road, or make a syringe. Remove the refinery before a substitute exists, they argue, and people die of disease, hunger, and exposure.


On the central point, I agree with them, with qualifications, and add the moral frame. A question about necessity is ultimately a question about how we treat our neighbor and how we receive what God has given.


## Why the Word “Addiction” Matters


Words carry moral judgments. To call a thing an addiction is to say it is a pathology: something that harms the user, that he would be better off without, and that he should be helped or pressured to quit. Activists did not coin that word alone. In 2006, a Republican President told the nation in his State of the Union address that America was “addicted to oil.” The word has bipartisan history, and it has shaped policy.


Stein is right that the word is wrong. An addiction is a craving that serves itself at the expense of the person’s good. Fertilizer that feeds half the world is not that. Neither is the diesel that runs an ambulance, the plastic that keeps a blood bag sterile, or the asphalt under a school bus. These serve life.


But I would go beyond “functional necessity,” because necessity is a cold word and it leaves out the heart. Scripture gives us a better word: **gratitude**. Moses warned Israel that when they had eaten and were full, and their houses and herds had multiplied, they would be tempted to say, *“My power and the might of my hand have gotten me this wealth.”* Instead, he told them to remember that the Lord gives the power to get wealth **(Deuteronomy 8:17–18)**. The hydrocarbons in the ground were laid down long before any man existed. His gift also includes the minds that learned to refine them. The right posture toward that gift is neither shame (addiction) nor indifference (lifestyle), but thankfulness joined to responsibility.


Gratitude also guards against the opposite error — the error of making oil an idol. A thing received with thanks can be held with an open hand. You cannot question, improve, or replace a thing you worship when something better comes. The Christian position is not “oil forever.” It is “use what God has given wisely, and do not throw it away before you have something better to put in its place.”


## Where Stein’s Case Is Strong


**Electricity is not all of energy.** This is the article’s heart, and it is correct. Roughly speaking, electricity is about a fifth of the world’s final energy use. The rest is heat, motive fuel, and chemical feedstock. A policy that measures “clean energy progress” only by the share of electricity from wind and solar measures only a small part of the problem.


**Fertilizer is the clearest example.** Synthetic nitrogen fertilizer is made from natural gas through the Haber-Bosch process. Energy scholar Vaclav Smil estimates that about half the world’s people are alive because of it. Whatever one believes about climate, a policy that sharply raised fertilizer prices, with no replacement, would fall first and hardest on the poorest farmers and the hungriest children.


**Their own death statistic is the strongest argument they make — though they do not draw the conclusion.** They note that burning raw solid fuels indoors for cooking and heat kills millions of people a year through household air pollution. The World Health Organization puts the figure at roughly three million deaths annually, mostly women and children in poor countries who cook over wood, dung, charcoal, or coal. What saves these lives? Clean cooking fuel — and the most widely available clean cooking fuel in the world is liquefied petroleum gas, a refinery and gas-processing product. Here the refinery is not a necessary evil. It is a direct instrument of mercy. I will return to this in the policy section.


## Where the Case Is Weaker


I hold position papers on this site to a rule: persuade by reason first, and keep every claim in proportion. Exaggeration is a kind of lying — not by stating a falsehood, but by stating a truth at the wrong size. *“Ye shall do no unrighteousness in judgment, in meteyard, in weight, or in measure. Just balances, just weights… shall ye have”* **(Leviticus 19:35–36)**. That standard applies as much to arguments I agree with as to arguments I oppose. By that standard, several things in the article should be tightened.


**The opponent is drawn too small.** The article warns that removing refineries with no substitute “could be the greatest threat” facing humanity. But very few serious policymakers propose removing refineries with no substitute. Even the International Energy Agency’s most aggressive net-zero pathway still has the world using a substantial amount of oil in 2050, much of it as chemical feedstock rather than fuel. The real debate is about pace, cost, mandates, and who bears the risk of a transition that outpaces its replacements. That debate is serious enough on its own terms. Arguing against a position almost no one holds lets the actual policy errors — premature refinery closures, mandates written before the substitute is ready, permitting systems that block both old and new energy — escape scrutiny.


**Some numbers are high.** The world’s vehicle fleet is usually estimated at about 1.4 to 1.5 billion, not 1.7 billion. The UN commonly estimates the share of world trade carried by sea at roughly 80 percent by volume, not 90 percent. The global commercial aircraft fleet in active service is generally counted in the high twenty-thousands; the article’s 35,550 likely includes parked or stored aircraft. None of these corrections weakens the argument — 1.4 billion vehicles still run on refined fuel — which is exactly why the higher figures are unnecessary.


**Life expectancy is stated too generously.** Global life expectancy did roughly double from about 32 years in 1900 to over 70 today. But it does not yet “exceed 75” worldwide; the global figure is in the low seventies. Some nations exceed 75, and the United States does. Again, the true number is impressive enough.


**“Wind and solar cannot make products” is true of the panel, not of the electron.** In principle, electricity can make hydrogen, ammonia, and even synthetic hydrocarbons from captured carbon and water. Today those processes are expensive and small. That is the honest point: not that they are impossible, but that they are not yet ready at scale or price. The distinction matters because it points to the answer.


**The authors’ interests should be stated.** Ronald Stein founded a firm that supplies staffing to oil refineries and serves as a policy advisor to the Heartland Institute. That does not make him wrong. Many people who know an industry best work in it. But readers deserve to know, and good arguments survive disclosure.


## The Moral Frame: Count the Cost, Love the Neighbor


Every law is a moral act. The test I apply to any energy policy is simple: **would my neighbor feel loved by this act in the public square?**


Consider the poor family in a cold climate whose heating bill doubles because a mandate closed the supply before a replacement arrived. Consider the farmer in Africa whose fertilizer becomes unaffordable. Consider the mother in South Asia breathing smoke over a dung fire because development lenders will no longer finance gas infrastructure. None of them feels loved. A policy that imposes these costs on the weakest, in the name of protecting them from a future harm, has its priorities backward.


But the same test cuts the other way. The neighbor downwind of a refinery that cuts corners on emissions does not feel loved either. Nor does the child whose asthma worsens in a city choked with exhaust. God placed man in the garden* “to dress it and to keep it”* **(Genesis 2:15)**. Stewardship means using the earth’s gifts fully *and* keeping the earth clean for the next generation. Gratitude for oil does not license carelessness.


Jesus gave the principle that governs both sides:* “For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost, whether he have sufficient to finish it?”* **(Luke 14:28)**. A transition that tears down the old structure before the new one is built is the half-finished tower. So is a refusal to build anything new at all. Both are failures of foresight.


## What I Would Do


These are my positions. I hold them with conviction, but I also keep them open: if someone brings a better argument, I want to hear it.


**1. No mandate without a working substitute.** No federal rule should phase out a fuel, feedstock, or product until a replacement exists at scale, at a cost ordinary families can bear, with a demonstrated supply chain. Mandates should follow capability, not precede it. This is “count the cost” written into administrative law.


**2. Build the substitute — abundant energy first.** My platform rests on the conviction that cheap, abundant energy underpins a prosperous, free society. I support aggressive development of thorium molten-salt reactors and other advanced nuclear power. Abundant, cheap, carbon-free heat and electricity are what would make synthetic fuels, hydrogen, and ammonia affordable. That is the realistic path to reducing oil use: not by forbidding it, but by making something better and cheaper. When the replacement wins on price, no mandate is needed.


**3. Fix permitting for every kind of energy.** The same regulatory apparatus that blocks a new nuclear plant also blocks refinery upgrades, pipelines, and the import terminals that keep fuel flowing when a refinery closes. Federal permitting should have firm deadlines and fixed decision points. A yes or no in two years is more honest than a maybe that lasts ten.


**4. Respect the States, and let them bear their own choices.** Fuel specifications and refinery regulation are largely matters of State law, and under the Tenth Amendment they should stay there. A State may choose a faster transition than I would. But it should not be shielded from the cost of that choice by federal bailouts, and it should not export the cost to neighboring States through supply shocks it could have foreseen.


**5. Let American gas and LPG save lives abroad.** The quickest way to prevent millions of deaths from smoky indoor cooking is clean fuel, and LPG is the proven option. American foreign assistance and development lending should not refuse to finance gas and LPG infrastructure in poor countries on climate grounds. Denying a mother clean cooking fuel to protect her from a long-term risk, while she faces an immediate one every day, fails the neighbor test.


**6. Honest numbers from every side.** “Energy literacy,” which Stein rightly promotes, must include the whole picture: the products oil makes possible *and* the pollution it causes, the cost of mandates *and* the finite nature of the resource. Federal agencies should publish their energy forecasts with stated assumptions and ranges, so citizens can see which predictions come true. Proportion in public speech is a moral duty, not a matter of style.


## Conclusion


Stein and Muronaka are right that the world cannot simply quit oil, and right that “addiction” is the wrong word for it. I would put it this way: oil is a gift, received over two centuries of human ingenuity, that lifted billions of people out of hunger and cold. The right response to a gift is gratitude and wise use — not shame, and not worship. We should use it well, keep the earth clean while we do, and work hard to build the better thing that will someday make it less necessary. Until we build and prove that better thing, we should not tear down what feeds and warms our neighbors.


That is counting the cost. That is loving the neighbor. And that is the energy policy I would bring to the office.
