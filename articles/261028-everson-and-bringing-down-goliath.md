# 261028 - Everson and Bringing Down Goliath

# Five Smooth Stones:


### On Scott Lively, Everson, and the Two Olive Trees


Oct 28, 2026 · @Thomas Abshier


## A giant worth naming


I stand with Scott Lively on the central claim of his October 28 newsletter: *Everson v. Board of Education* (1947) replaced the founders’ settlement between church and state with a judicial doctrine of separation, and America cannot recover its moral footing while that doctrine governs. Lively casts *Everson* as Goliath, the giant who defied the armies of the living God. The image is apt. A single ruling, built on a phrase lifted from a private letter of Thomas Jefferson, has shaped nearly eighty years of law on prayer, schooling, and the public acknowledgment of God.


Lively also gives the lost right name. He calls it *coadjuvancy*: two independent authorities, church and civil government, each sovereign in its own sphere, cooperating to govern the same people under God. Neither absorbs the other. Neither pretends the other does not exist. That is a better word than “separation,” and a far better one than “wall.”


My purpose here is to affirm his thesis and to sharpen it. When David went out to meet Goliath, he* “chose him five smooth stones out of the brook”* **(1 Samuel 17:40)**. He did not pick up whatever lay at hand. A stone that misses only rouses the giant. In a fight won or lost before judges and in the minds of fellow citizens, the accuracy of our history and our law is the stone’s smoothness. Several of Lively’s stones are rough at the edges, and in nearly every case the precise version cuts deeper than the loose one. What follows takes his argument point by point, corrects what needs correcting, adds what he left out, and ends where he ends: the giant can fall.


## What Everson actually did


*Everson* did its damage through its reasoning, not its result. The case arose when a New Jersey taxpayer challenged a township program that reimbursed parents for bus fare, including parents whose children rode to Catholic schools. The Court ruled 5–4 that the program was constitutional. The Catholic parents won.


Yet Justice Hugo Black’s majority opinion did two things that outlasted the bus fares. First, it applied the Establishment Clause to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment’s Due Process Clause. Before 1947, the clause bound only Congress, as its own words say: “Congress shall make no law.” (Lively attributes this extension to the Supremacy Clause; the Court’s actual vehicle was the Fourteenth Amendment, and the distinction matters, because it is the incorporation doctrine that Justice Clarence Thomas has challenged.) Second, Black wrote that the First Amendment “has erected a wall between church and state,” and that the wall “must be kept high and impregnable.”


So the giant came onto the field dressed as a friend. The religious party won the case and lost the principle. That pattern deserves attention because it has repeated itself ever since: small accommodations granted within a framework that treats public acknowledgment of God as constitutionally suspect.


Lively notes that the Court was composed “almost entirely” of Democratic appointees. In fact, Franklin Roosevelt or Harry Truman appointed all nine justices. That fact proves less than it seems, though. The four dissenters, also Roosevelt and Truman appointees, would have built the wall higher still and struck down the bus fares. The doctrine was not a party program. It reflected the prevailing legal culture of the time, and that is harder to overturn than a partisan majority.


## The cases that followed


Lively’s list of *Everson*‘s descendants is substantially right. Stated precisely, each one strengthens his case.


**Torcaso v. Watkins (1961).** Maryland required a notary public to declare his belief in God. Roy Torcaso, an atheist, refused, and the Court ruled for him. Lively says the Court declared atheism a religion entitled to equal protection. That overstates the holding. The holding was that no state may force a man to profess a belief or disbelief in any religion, and on that point the Court was right; I will return to it below. But in footnote 11, Justice Black listed “Secular Humanism” among the religions in America that do not teach belief in God. That footnote is the sharper stone. If secular humanism is a religion, then a government that teaches it in its schools and writes it into its law is not neutral. It has chosen a religion. The Court conceded the premise that undoes its own neutrality.


**Engel v. Vitale (1962) and Abington School District v. Schempp (1963).** *Engel* struck down a state-composed prayer in New York schools. *Schempp, *the next year, struck down daily Bible reading and the Lord’s Prayer. Within sixteen years of *Everson*, practices that had opened the school day across much of the country for generations were gone.


**“Benevolent neutrality.”** Lively attributes this phrase to Black in *Everson*. It actually comes from Chief Justice Warren Burger in *Walz v. Tax Commission* (1970), which upheld property tax exemptions for churches. Burger used it to soften Black’s wall, not to build it. The correction strengthens Lively’s point rather than weakening it. Even the kindly version of neutrality is still neutrality, and Jesus did not leave neutrality open: “He that is not with me is against me” (Matthew 12:30). A government cannot be neutral about whether God exists. It can only be honest or dishonest about which answer it has chosen.


**Employment Division v. Smith (1990).** Lively says *Smith* forced Justice Scalia into a half-measure. In fact, Scalia wrote the majority opinion, and he wrote it by conviction: a believer must obey a neutral law of general application, even one that burdens his worship. Congress later introduced the half-measure in the Religious Freedom Restoration Act of 1993. Lively’s larger point stands. The whole “religious liberty” strategy since then has been a search for exemptions inside a framework that treats God as a private matter. Exemptions are carve-outs. They concede the ground.


**The Johnson Amendment (1954).** The connection Lively draws to Lyndon Johnson and Justice Black is real history. In 1948, Black, sitting as circuit justice, stayed the lower-court order that would have kept Johnson’s name off the ballot after his disputed Senate primary. Six years later, Senator Johnson added to the tax code the ban on campaign intervention by tax-exempt organizations, churches included. The present state of that ban, and what it means for churches, needs its own section below.


## The ground has already shifted


Lively writes that *Everson* has never been squarely challenged. It has not been overruled, and Justice Thomas’s view, that the Establishment Clause protected state arrangements from federal interference and should never have been applied against the states, remains a minority view. But Lively leaves out the most important development of the last decade.


In *Kennedy v. Bremerton School District* (2022), the Court upheld a high-school football coach’s right to pray at midfield after games. In doing so, it declared that it had long ago abandoned the *Lemon* test, the three-part formula that had governed Establishment Clause cases since 1971. Instead, the Court said the clause must be interpreted by reference to historical practices and understandings.


That changes the battlefield. For fifty years the question in these cases was whether a practice had a secular purpose, whether it advanced religion, and whether it entangled government with the church. Those questions were designed to be answered against the public acknowledgment of God. The question now is what the founding generation did and understood. That is the ground on which the founders’ coadjuvancy is strongest: chaplains in Congress, days of prayer and thanksgiving proclaimed by presidents, state constitutions that named God, the Constitution itself dated “in the Year of our Lord.”


The Court has not yet followed its own method to the end. It has not said that *Everson*‘s wall was ahistorical. But it has handed us the sling. The work now is to put the history before the Court in case after case, carefully and accurately, until the contradiction between the method and the wall can no longer be held. This is a reason for hope, not complacency.


## Coadjuvancy in Scripture


Lively grounds coadjuvancy in the tribal order of Israel: civil leadership in the tribes, sanctuary service in Levi, and the forty-eight Levitical cities, six of them cities of refuge (Numbers 35; Joshua 20–21). The pattern is real, and Scripture gives us texts that state it even more plainly.


The clearest is the reform of King Jehoshaphat. When he set judges over Judah, he gave them this charge: “Amariah the chief priest is over you in all matters of the LORD; and Zebadiah the son of Ishmael, the ruler of the house of Judah, for all the king’s matters” (2 Chronicles 19:11). Two officers, two jurisdictions, one people, one God. That is coadjuvancy in a single verse.


Scripture also shows what happens when the line is crossed. King Saul, impatient for Samuel, offered the burnt offering himself and lost his dynasty for it (1 Samuel 13:8–14). King Uzziah entered the temple to burn incense, was withstood by eighty priests, and was struck with leprosy until the day of his death (2 Chronicles 26:16–21). The civil power may not take up the priest’s office. Yet the cooperation runs both ways. When Athaliah seized the throne and murdered the royal heirs, it was Jehoiada the priest who hid the child Joash and restored the rightful king (2 Kings 11). The sanctuary did not rule, but it preserved the throne.


The cities of refuge show the same interlocking. A man who killed another unintentionally fled to the city, but he was not thereby beyond the law. “The congregation shall judge between the slayer and the revenger of blood” (Numbers 35:24), and if they found the killing unintended, he lived in the city of refuge until the death of the high priest (Numbers 35:25). Civil judgment and sanctuary protection worked together. Neither alone could do justice.


Zechariah’s vision of the two olive trees, which Lively rightly makes central, has a companion passage two chapters later. Joshua the high priest is crowned as a sign of the coming Branch, who “shall sit and rule upon his throne; and he shall be a priest upon his throne: and the counsel of peace shall be between them both” (Zechariah 6:13). This points beyond Zerubbabel and Joshua to Christ, who alone holds both offices, a king and “a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek” (Psalm 110:4; Hebrews 7). Here is the deepest reason the two offices must stay distinct in the hands of men. Only One is fit to unite them. Until He reigns visibly, any man or institution that claims both is usurping what belongs to Him.


## God in every conscience, and the neighbor of another faith


There is a further reason the two spheres must stay distinct, and it goes to the center of what this ministry teaches. God is not only above the world as its Lawgiver. He dwells in each person and experiences each life from the inside. Every man’s conscience is the place where God meets him directly. The civil magistrate cannot enter that place, and the church has no sword to force its way in.


This is why coadjuvancy is not theocracy, and why *Torcaso*‘s actual holding was right. No government may compel a man to profess what he does not believe. A coerced confession is a lie spoken to God, and a state that demands it trains its people in hypocrisy. The founders’ settlement held two things together: the public law acknowledged God openly, and it did not police the inner life. Acknowledgment without compulsion.


That principle also answers a question Lively raises and then leaves at the level of grievance. He writes that adversaries have mobilized immigrant populations, Muslims especially, to turn each recovered inch of free exercise into a foothold for competing claims. The problem he points to is real: under a doctrine of neutrality, every faith’s claim on public life must be weighed as equal, and the founders’ Christian settlement becomes one claim among many. But the answer is not to withdraw liberty of conscience from our neighbors. The answer is to recover the confidence to say which God the public law of this nation acknowledges.


I test public policy with three questions. First, is it righteous: does it accord with what God has established and judges to be right? Second, if so, is it loving toward the neighbor? Third, would I want it done to me, if I stood where he stands? Apply them here. Suppose I were a Christian living in a Muslim nation. I would expect that nation to acknowledge its own faith in its public life; I would not ask it to pretend otherwise. What I would ask, and what I would rightly resent being denied, is freedom to worship and to keep my conscience. The golden rule therefore points directly to coadjuvancy: the Muslim, the Jew, and the unbeliever among us are free in worship and conscience, and protected in person and property, while the nation’s public law honestly acknowledges the God of Scripture. What none of us can have is a public law that pretends to stand nowhere.


## Andorra, examined closely


Lively’s illustration from Andorra is apt, and its present circumstances teach more than he shows. The co-principality dates from a 1278 agreement between the Bishop of Urgell and the Count of Foix, whose rights passed in time to the kings and then the presidents of France. Since its 1993 constitution, Andorra has been a parliamentary democracy that declares itself non-denominational. The two co-princes remain heads of state, but executive power lies with the head of government, and the co-princes’ powers are limited. Lively’s statement that neither is a figurehead is half right: neither is merely ceremonial, but neither governs.


What the co-princes do hold is the power to sign. And this year that power has been tested. Andorra is one of the few countries in Europe where abortion remains illegal. Its government has spent years negotiating with the Holy See to decriminalize it, President Macron pressed the matter on his visit this April, and the head of government has set a red line: no change that would permit abortions to be performed inside Andorra, because he fears the bishop would abdicate and the co-principality would collapse. The government’s workaround is to have the law signed by the French co-prince alone, as was done for earlier laws the bishop would not sign, including civil marriage.


Two lessons follow. First, the church’s real power in a coadjuvant order is the power of refusal. It cannot command the legislature, but it can decline to lend its authority to what God forbids, and that refusal still shapes what an avowedly secular government dares to do. That is the cities-of-refuge power: not a sword, but a place the sword cannot simply override.


Second, the refusal must have consequence or it decays into ornament. Where a single civil signature is enough, the state can route around the church, and the bishop’s dissent becomes a private scruple. A coadjuvancy that does not require mutual consent drifts into a civil state with an ecclesiastical decoration. For America, where the church holds no seat in government, the consequence must come through the people: through voters, through magistrates who fear God, and through juries who refuse to convict a neighbor under an unjust law. The church forms the conscience; the people carry it into the public square.


## The 508 path and the Johnson Amendment, stated accurately


Lively urges churches to leave the 501(c)(3) corporate form and points to section 508(c)(1)(A) of the tax code as one path. Renaissance Ministries took that path, so I can speak to it from experience, and I want to state plainly what it does and what it does not do.


What it does: section 508(c)(1)(A) exempts churches from the requirement to apply to the IRS for recognition of tax-exempt status. A church organized under it does not ask the state’s permission to exist, and it does not hold its standing by a government letter. That is a jurisdictional statement of real weight. It says the church is a pre-existing body whose rights the law recognizes rather than creates, which is exactly Lively’s point and mine.


What it does not do: it does not, by itself, place the church outside the political-campaign restriction. The tax code still treats a church as an organization described in section 501(c)(3), exempt automatically rather than by application, and the 1954 restriction is a condition attached to that exemption. As the statute reads, a church that never applied is bound by the same condition as one that did. Churches should not be told otherwise. A stone that misses only rouses the giant, and a pastor who endorses a candidate believing his 508 status protects him may learn the difference the hard way.


The live question is in the courts right now. In July 2025 the IRS agreed, in a proposed consent judgment with the National Religious Broadcasters and two Texas churches, that a church speaking to its own congregation about candidates during worship does not violate the restriction. On March 31, 2026, Judge J. Campbell Barker of the Eastern District of Texas refused to enter that judgment, holding that the court lacked authority because no church had yet been penalized. He observed that the plaintiffs could avoid the harm entirely by giving up their 501(c)(3) status. The Justice Department has asked the Fifth Circuit to reverse him. Meanwhile the restriction remains in force for next week’s election.


Judge Barker’s observation is the honest shape of the problem. Under the present statute a church has two clean options: win the constitutional argument that the government may not condition a church’s exemption on the content of its preaching, or decline the exemption and accept the tax consequences of speaking freely. I stand with the first. A church is not a subsidized charity that owes its voice to the Treasury. It is a sovereign in its own sphere, and the First Amendment forbids Congress to abridge its speech or prohibit its exercise. Until that argument is won, churches should know exactly where the line lies.


## Why the concern is well founded


Some will answer that the wall has protected religion as much as it has restrained it, and that the corrections above show the case against *Everson* is weaker than Lively claims. They show the opposite. The case is strong; it only needs to be made accurately. Five considerations keep it strong.


- **The record of the founding is not secular.** The Constitution is dated “in the Year of our Lord.” Congress opened with chaplains from its first session. As late as 1960, about four in ten school districts still had Bible reading, and half had some form of daily devotional. A nation that lived this way for more than 150 years did not understand its First Amendment to forbid it. The intuitive sense that something unnatural happened after 1947 is correct.

- **Neutrality is not neutral.** The Court itself, in *Torcaso*‘s footnote 11, counted secular humanism among the religions. A public order that removes God from its schools and its law has not stepped off the field. It has crowned another occupant of the throne while calling the throne empty.

- **The change came by decree, not consent.** No amendment was proposed, no legislature voted, no people ratified. A settlement that had governed the nation since its founding was replaced by the reasoning of a single opinion, in a case the religious party won. That is not how a self-governing people changes its organic law.

- **The incentives run one way.** The 1954 restriction trades the church’s prophetic voice for a tax benefit, and most churches have accepted the trade without examining it. A pulpit that may not name the moral stakes of an election when it matters most has been domesticated, whatever its doctrine says.

- **The results are visible.** Within sixteen years of *Everson* the schools were cleared of prayer and Scripture. The decades since have taught generations of children, by omission, that God has no bearing on public life. Doctrine shapes law, law shapes institutions, and institutions shape the people who pass through them. It would be strange if a nation taught for three generations that God is a private hobby did not come to live as though He were.


## The path back


Lively’s practical program is sound, and I would add to it.


- **Measure judicial nominees by *Everson*.** Lively is right that a nominee’s willingness to confront the wall matters more than his views on the later cases the wall produced. After *Kennedy*, the question to ask is simple: does he follow the history-and-tradition method all the way to the founding, or does he stop at 1947?

- **Teach *Holy Trinity* with its author’s own explanation.** In *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States* (1892), Justice David Brewer wrote for a unanimous Court that “this is a Christian nation.” Thirteen years later Brewer explained what he meant in a short book, *The United States a Christian Nation* (1905): not that Christianity was legally established or that any man was compelled to belong to a church, but that the nation’s life, laws, and institutions were Christian in character. That is coadjuvancy in Brewer’s own words, and quoting him accurately disarms the charge that the phrase means compulsion.

- **Put the history before the courts.** The method the Court now professes is the method on which *Everson* will fall. Briefs, scholarship, and public teaching should lay out the founding practice case by case.

- **Return establishment questions to the states.** Justice Thomas’s reading, that the Establishment Clause restrained Congress and protected the states’ own arrangements, is the federalist path. Several states kept official church support well into the nineteenth century; Massachusetts ended its last in 1833, by its own people’s vote. That is how such questions were meant to be settled: locally, by consent.

- **Recover the church’s independence and its voice, honestly.** Churches should know what 508(c)(1)(A) does and does not do, support the constitutional challenge to the 1954 restriction, and preach the moral stakes of public life without waiting for a tax ruling to grant permission.

- **Form consciences that will act.** The church’s power is refusal, and refusal reaches the public square through citizens: voters, officeholders, and jurors who will not lend their hands to injustice.


I hold this position with conviction, and I hold it open to a better argument. If someone can show that the founders meant the wall Justice Black described, I will listen. I do not expect to be shown it, because the record says otherwise.


David did not win because his stone was larger than Goliath’s spear. He won because, as he told the giant, “the battle is the LORD’s” (1 Samuel 17:47), and because the stone he chose flew true. Lively has named the giant correctly. Our task is to choose smooth stones: accurate history, sound law, the plain words of Scripture, and a public order that acknowledges God without compelling any man’s conscience. The two olive trees can again supply the oil, and the lamp can burn.
