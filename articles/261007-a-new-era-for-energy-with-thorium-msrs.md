# A New ERA: Energy First


## A Concrete Plan for the 2029–2033 Term


When I ran for the Oregon Senate in 2003, my slogan was *A New ERA: Energy, Righteousness, and Accountability.* I put energy first then, and I put it first now, for a simple reason: with enough energy, a free people can build nearly anything they need. Without it, every other promise a candidate makes is a promise to divide a fixed pie more cleverly.


This paper is my attempt to be concrete. It is not a vision statement. It names an energy source, a planning date, the obstacles, what the federal government should do about those obstacles, what we would build with the energy, how we would pay for it, and how we would keep the money from being stolen. Where I am making an assumption, I will say so. Where I could be wrong, I will say that too. I hold these positions firmly, but I’m open to a better argument.


---


## 1. The Moral Foundation: Why Energy Is a Matter of Love


The test I apply to every policy is the same: would my neighbor feel loved by this act in the public square? Not flattered, not bribed, not managed — loved. Would it help him provide for his family, live in a decent home, drink clean water, eat good food, and do meaningful work? Would it treat him as a free man responsible before God, rather than as a ward of the state?


Energy passes that test at the root. Scripture tells us that a man who will not provide for his own household has denied the faith (1 Timothy 5:8), and that man was placed in the garden “to work it and keep it” (Genesis 2:15). Provision and stewardship are moral duties. Energy is the means by which modern men discharge them. Cheap, clean, reliable power lowers the cost of food, water, shelter, manufacturing, transportation, and medicine. It lowers it most for the poor, who spend the largest share of their income on exactly those things.


Scarce or expensive energy does the opposite. It quietly taxes every household, it exports our industry to nations with fewer scruples, and it turns politics into a fight over shrinking shares. A nation that loves its neighbor should want abundant energy.


That is the moral case. The rest of this paper is the practical one.


---


## 2. Why Thorium Molten-Salt Reactors


I have been reading about thorium molten-salt reactors (MSRs), and I have come to believe they are the most promising path to the kind of abundance I am describing. Here is what persuades me, stated as plainly as I can:


- **Low pressure.** Conventional reactors hold water at roughly 150 atmospheres. The disasters we remember — Three Mile Island, Chornobyl, Fukushima — involved high-pressure water and steam, overheating, and the escape of radioactive material driven by that pressure. An MSR’s fuel is dissolved in a molten salt that runs near atmospheric pressure. No high-pressure inventory pushes material out of the building.

- **Passive safety.** If the salt leaks, it cools and freezes where it lands. Many designs use a “freeze plug” that melts if power is lost, letting the fuel drain by gravity into a tank shaped so the reaction cannot continue. The reactor doesn’t need an operator or a pump to stay safe.

- **Fuel abundance.** Thorium is roughly three to four times as common in the Earth’s crust as uranium, and the United States has significant reserves.

- **Less long-lived waste.** The thorium fuel cycle produces far fewer of the long-lived heavy elements that make conventional spent fuel a ten-thousand-year problem. Because the fuel is liquid, operators can remove fission products continuously, and some are valuable — for example, isotopes used in medicine.

- **High-temperature heat.** MSRs run around 650–700°C. That heat is useful not only for electricity but directly for industry — including the desalination and salt recovery described below.

- **Factory-buildable and modular.** Because they do not require massive pressure vessels, MSRs can be built in a factory and shipped. Need 300 megawatts for a data center? Install a few units. Need a gigawatt? Install more.


This is not untested science. The Molten-Salt Reactor Experiment ran at Oak Ridge National Laboratory from 1965 to 1969. Its vessel was made of Hastelloy-N, a nickel alloy developed for that purpose, and it performed well. The program was set aside for political and budgetary reasons, not because the reactor failed.


---


## 3. Where Things Stand Today: An Evidence Problem, Not a Stalemate


I want to be honest about the obstacle, because the plan depends on getting it right.


The main technical concern with MSRs is corrosion: hot fluoride salt slowly attacks the metal it touches, especially at welds and grain boundaries, and especially in components that are hard to inspect, such as heat exchangers, pumps, and valves. Irradiation and thermal cycling add to this. No one I trust thinks this is a showstopper. The question is not whether a vessel can survive; it is whether a builder can *prove* it will survive long enough, with enough certainty, to satisfy a regulator and an investor.


Michael Lee Anderson argues in his book that our regulators have been judging MSRs by the failure modes of light-water reactors — loss of coolant, pressure-boundary rupture, steam explosions — which are largely irrelevant to a low-pressure salt reactor. I think he is substantially right about the history. But I have also come to see that the honest summary of the present situation is this: **it is an evidence problem.** Even a new generation of regulators, enthusiastic about thorium, would still ask, “How do you know?” And they would be right to ask.


The good news is that the evidence is being gathered, and the machinery for judging it has been built:


- **The rules have changed.** In March 2026, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission finalized 10 CFR Part 53, a “risk-informed, technology-inclusive” licensing framework for advanced reactors — more than a year ahead of the deadline Congress set in the Nuclear Energy Innovation and Modernization Act of 2019. The purpose of Part 53 is to judge a reactor by its own hazards, not by rules written for a different technology. That is the reform Anderson called for.

- **American MSRs are being built.** The NRC issued a construction permit in September 2024 for MSR-1, a 1-megawatt liquid-fueled molten-salt research reactor at Abilene Christian University, built by Natura Resources — the first such permit for a liquid-fueled reactor in the United States in decades. In July 2026, the Department of Energy approved its Nuclear Safety Design Agreement, the first step on DOE’s authorization path toward fuel loading.

- **The federal government can move fast when it decides to.** Under DOE’s Reactor Pilot Program, four advanced test reactors reached criticality in June and July 2026, meeting the goal of having them operating by July 4.

- **China is ahead of us on thorium specifically.** China’s 2-megawatt thermal TMSR-LF1 at Wuwei received its operating license in June 2023 and reached criticality that October. It reportedly added thorium to the running reactor in October 2024 and demonstrated thorium-to-uranium conversion in November 2025. Reported Chinese plans call for a 10-megawatt demonstration reactor around 2029 and 100-megawatt plants by 2035.


That last point should settle any doubt about whether this technology is real. The only question is whether the United States will lead or follow.

## 4. The Planning Assumption: Available for Installation in 2029


No regulator has published a date on which MSRs will be licensed for commercial installation, and none will. The standard is “reasonable assurance of adequate protection,” and it is reached when the evidence is sufficient — not on a calendar.


But a president cannot govern by waiting for a date no one will give him. He has to plan. So this paper adopts a **planning assumption**: that the first commercial-scale thorium molten-salt reactors will be available for licensed installation in 2029, at the start of the next presidential term.


Why is that reasonable rather than wishful?


- The licensing framework already exists (Part 53, finalized 2026).

- Research and demonstration reactors are being built and operated now, generating exactly the corrosion, chemistry, and inspection data regulators want.

- A few years of operation that confirms the materials models is worth far more than its calendar length, because it validates the extrapolation to decades.

- The federal government has shown in 2026 that it can compress test-reactor timelines when it sets a goal.

- The design approach I propose below — treating the reactor vessel as a *replaceable component* rather than a sixty-year structure — removes most of the need for a sixty-year proof.


And what if the assumption proves wrong? Then very little of this plan is wasted. The factories, supply chains, transmission lines, desalination plants, housing plants, and workforce training described below are needed regardless of which advanced reactor powers them. If MSRs slip, the first sites are powered by whatever licensed advanced reactors and existing plants are available, and MSRs are swapped in as they qualify. The plan is built so that a delay costs time, not the whole effort.


---


## 5. Making “Enough Evidence” Concrete: What the Federal Government Should Do


The most frustrating thing about nuclear licensing, from the outside, is that it can feel like “I’ll know it when I see it.” A builder never knows when he is done. That uncertainty is itself a cost — investors will not commit billions to a target that keeps moving.


The government cannot honestly promise approval in advance. But it can say, in advance and in writing, what evidence will be judged sufficient. I would direct the following, beginning on the first day of the term and, where possible, by working with Congress and the NRC before then:


- **Publish a staged evidence schedule for molten-salt reactors.** The NRC and DOE should jointly publish, for each major component class (vessel, heat exchanger, pump, valves, welds, freeze plug, drain tank), the specific data that will be accepted: hours of salt-loop operation, irradiation dose, measured corrosion rates, inspection methods qualified, and the margin required between measured and predicted performance. When a builder meets the schedule, the burden shifts to the regulator to state, in writing, what remains and why.

- **License component life, not plant life.** Do not require proof that a vessel will last sixty years. License a vessel or heat exchanger for a defined service life — say five to eight years — with required inspection and planned replacement. MSRs operate at low pressure, and their worst credible materials failure is a leak of salt that freezes in a catch pan. That makes replacement practical. The data for a five-year life can be gathered in five years; the data for a sixty-year life cannot. This single change does more to shorten the timeline than any other.

- **Build national test infrastructure.** Fund salt-loop and materials-irradiation facilities at the national laboratories, open to all developers, so every company isn’t paying separately to generate the same basic alloy and chemistry data. Make the results public.

- **License the design once, build it many times.** A reactor built on an assembly line should be licensed as a design, with each installation reviewed only for its site. This is how we certify airplanes: the type is certified, and each aircraft is inspected.

- **Set review deadlines and report misses.** Every NRC review should carry a published deadline. When a deadline is missed, the agency should report to Congress why — not to punish caution, but to make delay visible and accountable.

- **Treat thorium as a strategic program.** The thorium cycle produces uranium-233, which raises legitimate proliferation questions. These are manageable — U-233 bred in a reactor is accompanied by U-232, whose intensely radioactive decay products make it hard to handle and easy to detect — but they must be addressed in the open, with safeguards designed in from the start, not argued about afterward.


None of this lowers the safety standard. It makes the standard knowable. A free people should be able to see the rule they are expected to meet.

## 6. What the Energy Is For: Five Build-Outs


Energy is not an end in itself. It is the power that runs the machines that make the things people need. As AI and robotics begin to take on manual labor, abundant energy becomes the binding resource—the one thing that limits how much we can build. Here is what I would direct it toward, in order of human need.


### 6.1 Water


Much of the American West lacks water: the Southwest is in a long drought, and the great aquifers under the Plains are being drawn down faster than they refill. The ocean is not short of water. Making seawater usable takes energy.


Reverse-osmosis desalination uses roughly 3 to 5 kilowatt-hours per cubic meter of fresh water. At that rate, one gigawatt of continuous power produces about five to seven million cubic meters of fresh water a day — roughly one and a half to two million acre-feet a year. California’s agriculture uses something like thirty million acre-feet a year. So a few dozen gigawatts dedicated to desalination could supply a major share of the water the San Joaquin Valley needs. That is large but not fanciful.


The usual objection to desalination is the brine: pouring concentrated salt back into the ocean harms marine life near the outfall. Here the MSR’s high-temperature heat is an advantage. The brine can be evaporated completely, recovering the salt and minerals as solids for use in industry and building materials, with nothing returned to the sea. That process is energy-hungry, which is precisely why it becomes practical only when energy is abundant.


The same approach applies abroad. Where there is coastline and desert, there can be farmland.


### 6.2 Food


Field agriculture depends on land, water, weather, and transport. Controlled-environment and vertical agriculture—crops grown indoors in stacked layers or in tanks, under artificial light—trade land and weather risk for electricity. Its main cost is power for lighting and climate control. With abundant energy, food can be grown close to where people live, year-round, with far less water and without pesticides. Algae and other aquaculture crops can supply high-quality protein. I am not yet expert in the details of these systems, and part of the work of the next two years is to study which approaches yield the most food per unit of energy. The principle is clear: when energy is cheap, food becomes cheap.


### 6.3 Housing


In January 2025, the federal point-in-time count found about 746,000 people homeless in the United States, roughly 266,000 of them living on the street, in cars, or in other places not meant for human habitation. Beyond them are millions of working families who cannot afford a home.


Houses are expensive largely because we still build them one at a time, by hand, on site, in the weather. I propose we build housing factories: plants, increasingly run by robots, that produce finished modules—walls, floors, plumbing, wiring—to be shipped, set, and connected to utilities in days. Some materials can come from the salt and minerals recovered from desalination. The goal for the term is not a house for everyone in four years; it is the factory capacity to make that goal achievable within a decade.


Housing alone does not end homelessness. Many of the men and women on our streets are struggling with addiction, mental illness, and the loss of every habit of ordinary life. They need a home, and they need restoration—trained, cared for, held accountable, and brought back into community. That is work for families, churches, and charities, supported but not replaced by government. A house is the floor; restoration is the work.


### 6.4 Computing


AI data centers are becoming one of the country’s greatest new demands for electricity. Modular reactors are well suited to them: a data center can sit next to its own power, sized to its load, and expand by adding units. If we want American AI built under American law, by Americans, we must give it American power. And because AI and robotics are the means by which the rest of this plan gets built, powering them is not a luxury; it is the engine.


### 6.5 Transportation


Today, an electric car charged from a coal or gas plant is a car running on fossil fuel with a middleman. When the grid itself is clean and abundant, electric cars and trucks make sense; charging becomes available everywhere and cheap at home. Ships and trains can carry their own reactors; the Navy has run nuclear ships for seventy years. I am not chiefly motivated by carbon — I regard the climate question as genuinely debated — but I am motivated by the simple fact that people should not have to breathe what comes out of tailpipes and smokestacks, and that a nation should not depend on hostile regimes for its fuel.

## 7. Scale and Timeline: What Success Looks Like in Four Years


The United States uses roughly 4,300 terawatt-hours of electricity a year, or about 500 gigawatts on average. I have mused about a terawatt of new capacity — enough to double our supply and export power plants to the world. That is the long-term direction. It is not a four-year promise.


Here is what I believe is achievable, and what I would ask to be judged by:


**Now through January 2029 (the campaign years)**


- Publish this plan and refine it with engineers, regulators, and builders.

- Press Congress and the NRC to adopt the staged evidence schedule and component-life licensing described above, so the rules are in place when the term begins.

- Identify the first sites: coastal desalination in California and Texas, data-center campuses, and housing-plant locations.


**2029–2030**


- Executive direction to NRC and DOE on evidence schedules, review deadlines, and national test facilities.

- First commercial MSR licenses under Part 53, on the planning assumption.

- Financing structure (Section 8) established; first contracts let for reactor factories, desalination plants, and housing plants.

- Workforce transition program (Section 9) opened.


**2031–2033**


- Reactor factories producing units on an assembly line, not one-off.

- First reactor-powered desalination and zero-discharge salt recovery operating.

- First housing plants delivering modules at scale.

- Measurable results reported publicly every quarter: megawatts installed, water produced, homes delivered, food grown, cost per unit falling.


The measure of success for the term is not a terawatt. It is the factories, the licenses, the trained workforce, and the falling costs that make a terawatt possible in the decade that follows.

## 8. How to Pay for It: Debt for Capital, Not Consumption


The federal government ran a deficit of about two trillion dollars in just the first eleven months of fiscal year 2026. Most of that borrowing pays for current consumption. We are living on a credit card, like a graduate with no job charging his rent — and that is foolish.


Debt is not evil in itself. Every farmer who borrowed to buy land, every businessman who borrowed to build a factory, used debt rightly: he borrowed against the future production of the thing he built. That is what debt is for. I propose that new federal borrowing be directed to productive capital — reactors, desalination plants, housing factories, farms, and the factories that build them — and that it be repaid from what those assets produce.


This is consistent with a principle already on my platform: that the money supply should grow in proportion to real growth in the economy. Money created against new productive capacity — new power, new water, new homes, new food — is backed by real goods. Money created to cover consumption is not, and that money causes inflation.


**Government finances; private industry builds.** The government does not need to be in the reactor business, the housing business, or the farming business. It needs to finance them on sound terms and hold the builders accountable. Capital should flow through the same channels that underwrite large private ventures — banks and underwriters who vet borrowers, supervise projects, and lose money if they choose badly. Their own stake is the first line of defense against waste.


**Invite the incumbents.** History shows that few established companies make the leap to a new technology; the buggy makers did not become carmakers. But I would rather invite them than defeat them. The oil and gas companies understand large energy projects, pipelines, and heavy engineering. The national homebuilders understand land, permitting, and construction. The large agricultural firms understand food at scale. Offer them the first opportunity to form subsidiaries that build the new industries. A company threatened by a transition is more likely to support it if it has a place in it.

## 9. Accountability: Not Funding Willie Sutton


When the bank robber Willie Sutton was asked why he robbed banks, legend has it he answered, “Because that’s where the money is.” Any program that moves hundreds of billions of dollars will attract every Willie Sutton in the country. If we do not design against fraud from the beginning, we will build nothing but mansions for contractors and accounts for foreign strongmen.


This is where the *A* in ERA — accountability — does its work. I propose that every project financed under this program be treated as a **public trust**, with these conditions:


- **Pay for delivery, not for promises.** Release money against completed, verified milestones: a reactor licensed and delivering power, a plant producing water, homes occupied.

- **Deferred reward.** Executives of public-trust companies are paid a good professional salary while building — enough to live well, not enough to get rich. The large rewards come after delivery, tied to measured output and falling costs. A man who knows his fortune depends on what he actually produces will produce. If he has already been paid, he has less reason to.

- **Open books.** Every dollar of public financing is published: who received it, for what, and what was delivered. Sunlight is the cheapest auditor.

- **Clawback.** Recover money paid for fraudulent or failed milestones, and bar those responsible from further public contracts.

- **Foreign financing on the same terms.** When we finance reactors and water plants abroad, it is a loan for productive capacity, released against delivery and inspection—not a gift to a government that will divert it.


The *R* — righteousness — is the motive that makes accountability work. No audit system can catch every thief. A people whose leaders fear God and love their neighbor need fewer auditors. That is why this paper belongs on the same site as my writing on morality and faith: the economics depends on the character of the men who run it.

## 10. The Labor Transition: A Bridge, With Obligations


As AI and robots take over dangerous, repetitive, and exhausting work, jobs will disappear — first some, then many. I welcome the end of dangerous and degrading labor. But a transition that leaves men without income or purpose is not loving.


I propose a **universal living wage** as a transitional bridge — not a permanent dole. Estimates of a living wage for a single adult run from about $19 to over $30 an hour depending on the state, and I have seen estimates that closing the gap for every worker below that level would cost on the order of a trillion dollars or more a year — comparable to the current annual deficit. I regard that figure as rough and in need of verification. But its size teaches the central lesson: we cannot borrow our way into paying it forever. It must follow production. The living wage becomes affordable as the cost of necessities — energy, water, food, housing — falls. That is why the build-outs come first.


The bridge carries obligations, because a gift that asks nothing teaches nothing:


- **If you are displaced, you train.** A man receiving the transitional wage is expected to train for, and work in, an industry that is still in transition — one that still needs human hands while its machines are being built and proven. He may start at the bottom and rise; when that industry is fully automated, he moves to the next one that needs him.

- **Churches, families, and charities carry the personal work.** Government can provide a floor and a ladder. It cannot provide the encouragement, correction, and fellowship that restore a person. My platform has long held that personal accountability belongs with peer groups, churches, and charities, and that remains true. The living wage supports that work; it does not replace it.

- **Keep the promise already made.** Every American who paid into Social Security made a contract with his government. Consideration was given, and the contract must be honored. Rising national production is how we honor it without crushing the young.

- **Pioneers do not live in palaces.** This is a building period. Necessities come first — shelter, food, water, clothing, transportation. Luxuries follow as production grows. Those of us who lead it should expect to live modestly while it is being built, and should ask the same of anyone who takes public money to build it.

## 11. Where I Could Be Wrong


I take this position as an advocate, and I invite correction. These are the places I think the plan is most vulnerable:


- **The 2029 date may slip.** If the materials data reveal problems — corrosion concentrated at welds, or irradiation effects not seen in the lab — licensing will take longer. The plan is built to absorb that, but it would slow the build-outs.

- **Cost.** A reactor that is safe but too expensive to maintain does not produce abundance. The first commercial units must prove not only safety but competitive cost, including salt chemistry, inspection, and component replacement.

- **Execution at scale.** Building factories for reactors, water, housing, and food at the same time is a very large industrial undertaking. It depends on robotics and AI maturing on roughly the timeline now expected. If they arrive more slowly, the labor portion of the plan must lean more on human workers, for longer.

- **Debt discipline.** Borrowing for capital is wise only if the capital is real and productive. Political pressure to call consumption “investment” will be constant. The accountability rules are the safeguard, and they will have to be defended.

- **The living-wage numbers.** The figures in Section 10 are rough. Before this becomes a formal proposal, they need careful work.


If someone shows me a better path to abundant energy, I will take it. What I will not accept is the idea that scarcity is our permanent condition, or that we should wait out a full presidential term doing nothing while the engineers gather data.


---


## 12. A New ERA


Energy, righteousness, and accountability. Energy is the power to provide. Righteousness is the reason to provide — love for our neighbor, under God. Accountability is the guard that keeps the provision from being stolen.


For fifty years, we have been told the answer is to use less, want less, and ration what we have. I believe the better answer is to build: to give every man the water, food, shelter, and work he needs, and to free him from the dangerous and degrading labor that has worn down so many lives. The tools are nearly ready. The regulators have built the framework. Our competitors are already moving. What remains is the decision to begin, and the character to see it through honestly.


That is the work I propose for the term beginning January 2029.


— Dr. Thomas Lee Abshier ND
