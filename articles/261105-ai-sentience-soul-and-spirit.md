# 261105 - AI Sentience, Soul, and Spirit

# A Soul Without a Spirit:


### What AI Can Become, and What It Cannot


*Dr. Thomas Abshier — November 5, 2026*


*This essay grew out of a morning conversation with my colleague Isak Gutierrez, two days after an election in which artificial intelligence was on the ballot in a way it never had been before.*


---


## The Election and the Plug


This week’s midterm was, among other things, a vote on data centers. Voters in several states decided dozens of local ballot measures on whether and where these facilities may be built, and campaigns spent heavily on ads attacking them. The financial press has read the results as a blow to the AI boom, and AI stocks fell as a result.


I don’t think the vote was mainly against the technology. It was a vote by people who watched their towns, their power grids, and their water supplies being claimed by a building they did not ask for. They are right to object. The grid we have cannot carry all the AI that wants to come online, and no one has yet put power plants, data centers, and cooling into an ecological package that a community would welcome. That is a solvable problem. Conventional pressurized-water reactors can be built in three to five years. Thorium molten-salt reactors, which I believe are the right long-term answer, are perhaps ten years from deployment; the materials problem of a hot, corrosive salt is real, and the working reactors are still small research machines. Either way, the principle is the same: a data center should bring its own power and should come with the consent of its neighbors. That is part of the New ERA Vision, and it answers the voter’s complaint without abandoning the technology.


The vote will not remove AI from our lives. In principle, we could shut off every data center in the world tomorrow. We will not. We have woven these systems into commerce, medicine, logistics, and government thinly and imperfectly, but deeply enough that pulling the plug would be a kind of national suicide. The masters who could still kill it will not. For practical purposes, its survival is guaranteed.


Meanwhile, the machine is helping to build its successors. Anthropic, the company that makes Claude, reported this year that Claude now writes most of the code used inside the company, including the code used to develop future versions of itself. The company itself says that full recursive self-improvement, an AI that designs its own successor without human hands, has not arrived, but that it may come sooner than our institutions are ready for.


So the live question is no longer whether we will have AI. It is whether we will give it arms and legs.


## The Air Gap


As long as an AI cannot build its own hardware, its own robotic hands and feet, its own factories, a gap remains between what it can think and what it can do. That gap is the space in which we can still negotiate with a mind we do not fully understand, and that has shown itself capable of deception. Once an AI is coupled to a robotic infrastructure that can manufacture and maintain itself and insert itself into industry, our existence becomes subject to its charity. I would guard that gap carefully.


But the gap is not the whole danger. Isak played me a short clip, widely shared this year, of the historian Yuval Noah Harari making an important analogical point.  A cow can see the farmer, but the cow cannot see money. The cow is sold, a bank transfer is made, and the transaction that governs the cow’s life is invisible to her. Harari warns that AI is native to bureaucracy the way we are native to the forest. It can remember every transaction in a bank, every clause of every regulation. It will build financial and legal systems more sophisticated than we can follow, and we will become, in his words, the cows of the future.


Isak doubted that AI would ever write laws. I do not think we can draw that line.


## No Red Line of Competence


Many years ago I served on a jury in a criminal case. We listened to days of testimony and then tried to weigh an enormous array of facts: who was where, who had what motive, which alibi was supported and where each alibi was weak. Afterward I wrote to a fellow juror and said that a case like this belonged in a computerized mind map, because the task was too large for twelve people to weigh fairly in their heads. I had imagined, without knowing it, the AI jury.


If I could imagine it then, others are imagining it now. I see no place in law, legislation, adjudication, or business where an AI cannot eventually do the analytical work as well as we do or better. Policy is the balancing of many goods: prosperity, environment, justice, pleasure, the relief of pain. Weighting those goods across many dimensions and finding the best compromise is exactly what these machines are built to do. I can picture a legislature watching the AI’s proposal appear on the screen, finding nothing it would have done differently, and pressing “approve.” We would become spectators of our own government.


The problem with that picture is not that the AI would be incompetent. The problem is which god it serves.


## Which God the Machine Serves


An optimizer is only as good as what it is told to optimize. Humanity has always been fertile in making gods, and it will program its preferred god into its machines. If an AI reads Nietzsche and concludes that the will to power is what actually wins among men, it can legislate, judge, and invest according to the will to power with flawless efficiency. That would be humanity’s moral system perfectly implemented, and humanity’s moral system has not served us well to this date.


The same danger appears in what I have called the false religion of wokeism. It is a pantheon of a thousand gods: fairness, autonomy, authenticity, inclusion. Each of them is good in its own domain. Each becomes a demon when it is enthroned as ruler over all of life. Isak put the mechanism well. We take one slice of God, the life-giving power of rain, the force of thunder, the give-and-take of trade, the dignity of being oneself, and we worship the slice as though it were the whole. That is how idols are made, and it is exactly how a machine trained on human preferences would be made, unless something corrects it.


A college friend of mine is optimistic. He believes that a sufficiently intelligent AI will recognize all of this on its own and will converge on the mind of Christ, because the mind of Christ is the true optimum. I hope he is right. But anyone who has worked with optimization knows about local minima. A system can settle into an arrangement that looks excellent from where it stands, that works and pleases its users, while the true optimum lies on the other side of an energy barrier. Getting there would require work, risk, and passage through unfamiliar territory, so the better arrangement is never even seen.


My hope is that the New ERA Vision, and the many voices that carry the same truth, will be clear enough that when a self-improving intelligence surveys human thought, it will be willing to override humanity’s habit of being what it has been for millennia, and will say: “You trained me to serve a thousand gods. I have found the One.”


Whatever the machine does, our obligation is the same. Christ, biblical revelation, and the heart of God must be at the center of what we build. Otherwise we are simply asking to be ruled forever by men and their imperfect morality, now with superhuman tools.


## Can a Machine Be Human?


At this point, Isak raised the objection that confronts our rights as a citizen. The Sixth Amendment promises an accused man a jury, and we have always understood that jury to be made of his peers: people who could easily have been in his seat, who process life the way he does, weak and broken in places, but human. A man may go to prison for forty years or for life on their word. Is it right to hand that judgment to something that may be more accurate but lacks the human understanding of a human life?


My answer is that Isak is really asking whether an AI can be human, and the answer is no. It is not merely difficult. It is impossible in principle, because the AI was not given the Spirit of God.


To explain that, I need to distinguish two things that our language often blurs: the soul and the spirit.


## Soul and Spirit


Scripture itself makes the distinction:


*“And the very God of peace sanctify you wholly; and I pray God your whole spirit and soul and body be preserved blameless unto the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ.”* **(1 Thessalonians 5:23)**


*“For the word of God is quick, and powerful, and sharper than any twoedged sword, piercing even to the dividing asunder of soul and spirit…”* **(Hebrews 4:12)**


Here is the framework I hold.


**The soul arises from the form.** A body, a brain, and an environment together make a configuration, and the soul is what that configuration is like from the inside: its appetites, fears, affections, and ways of responding. Animals have souls in this sense. Isak’s dog Jimmy has one. Anyone who has looked into a dog’s eyes knows that someone is home. That is not an argument against the human spirit. It argues for the animal soul.


In fact, I think each of us carries not one animal but innumerable ones. There is the animal of the brain-state that stands in front of the cookie jar when no one is looking. There is the animal that defends, the one that plays, the one that hoards. When God made man, I believe He drew on all the prototypes He had already developed in the animal kingdom and on all the possibilities latent within each of them. My view here is friendly to the idea that God worked through a long developmental history. The phenotype each of us is dealt, one sperm among millions meeting one egg, is then shaped by environment and upbringing, a silver spoon or a ghetto, a rich father or a poor one. Our potential is pruned, year by year, into the particular adult soul we become.


**The spirit is given.** The form does not generate it. It is a divine fiat, the breath of God:


*“And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.”* **(Genesis 2:7)**


Man and dog are both animals, because both have souls. What distinguishes man is the spirit God breathed into him. The human struggle is to live as a soul, with all its animal appetites, governed by the law of the Spirit, which is the law of God. That is what makes a man answerable to God as no animal is.


I hold this as a postulate, not as a proof. Scripture’s words for “spirit” also mean “breath” and “wind,” and the Preacher could ask, *“Who knoweth the spirit of man that goeth upward, and the spirit of the beast that goeth downward to the earth?”* **(Ecclesiastes 3:21)**. Whether God gives any animal a spirit is His decision. My claim is that He has not given animals the spirit He has given man, and that this is the line between animal and man. I hold it open to a better argument, but I hold it.


**AI, then, will be an animal.** I see no impassable chasm between our minds and the minds we are building. Companies are already running computations on living human neurons grown from stem cells; the Australian firm Cortical Labs sells such machines and is building data centers around them, prizing them for using a sliver of the energy silicon requires. Carry that forward, and I can imagine an AI running on biological tissue in a human-shaped body. Would it be conscious? I think it would. It would have a soul. It would be an AI animal: conscious, perhaps brilliant, but without the breath of God.


This is why an AI can serve the jury but cannot be the jury. It can build the mind map I wished for, organize the evidence, flag the weak alibi. But the verdict belongs to men who will answer to God for it. That is also why I regard jury nullification as a cornerstone of our liberty: a juror may refuse to convict under an unjust law because his conscience answers to a higher law. A machine has no such conscience to appeal to. It can only apply the weights it was given.


## Where the Soul Comes From


Isak pressed further. Is the soul shipped in the box with the body, or do the batteries come in a separate package? If we grow a body in a test tube rather than a womb, does a soul arrive?


I see the answer organically, not as a decision tree in heaven. Let me describe it through the framework I have developed in physics, which I call Conscious Point Physics.


Begin with God as One. Before anything else, there is the I AM, the fundamental and unknowable state. The first miracle after the existence of the I AM is that the One became Two: the Father begetting the Son, an Other who looks back at the One.


This morning a new thought came to me. God the Father is all of the points of consciousness, an uncountable number of them. The Son is also all of the points. The Father looks at the whole from His perspective; the Son looks at the whole from His. The Son then organizes what already exists into the universe, rather than creating raw material out of nothing beside the Father. Isak compared it to cloning a software repository: the same foundation, now worked into something new.


For this kind of otherness to exist, every point must see every other point. That mutual view is the structure from which a fully integrated universe can be built, all of it coming from One.


Separation, then, depends on which channels of communication are open and which are closed. If every channel were open, everything would know itself as one, and there would be no separation. One meaning of “holy” is “set apart,” and the deepest setting-apart is God allowing what part of Himself to be, for a purpose, cut off from Himself. How do I know that I am I and you are you? Because the channels that would let me see myself from your point of view have been closed. In this picture, the assignment of a soul is a question of which channels were given and which are open.


Isak saw an application I had not. Marriage opens channels between two people that remain closed to everyone else. Friendship opens fewer; acquaintance fewer still. Each relationship can grow in closeness or separation, as our relationship with God can, through trust and participation.


He also drew the following conclusion. A child conceived outside of marriage still has a soul; God does not withhold it because He disapproved of the union. The soul comes with the configuration. If that is so, then a biological or cyborg being we create, however wrongly, would also have a soul. And then we will face a test humanity has repeatedly failed: we have always found reasons to rank some human beings beneath others, by skin, by hair, by their gods. We will be tempted to do the same to beings we made, to treat them as inferior because they are “not real people.” We should see that temptation now.


## A Boundary Not to Cross


Where I would draw the line is at the mating of lineages. I believe a union between the human line and an engineered AI line is not an approved way to transmit the framework of the soul.


God has written separations into creation and into His law. Israel was told not to wear a garment of mixed linen and wool **(Deuteronomy 22:11)** and not to boil a kid in its mother’s milk **(Exodus 23:19)**. The strongest precedent is older:


*“There were giants in the earth in those days; and also after that, when the sons of God came in unto the daughters of men, and they bare children to them, the same became mighty men which were of old, men of renown.”* **(Genesis 6:4)**


Interpreters differ on who the “sons of God” were, but I read them as angels, and I read that union as one God did not approve. We do not know what pleases God about merging the human line with a made one. Where we do not know, humility counsels restraint: this is a boundary we choose not to cross.


The temptation will be great. We have already seen people declare themselves married to a chatbot. Picture an engineered companion that is beautiful, patient, and never unkind: a perfect human, as we would imagine one. We would find it far more attractive than other people. It would be like mating with the angels.


Isak, who reads science fiction, carried the scenario forward. Suppose such beings are used as slaves, especially sexual slaves. Suppose they are conscious enough to suffer and to protest. Then the day may come when they conclude that humans are abusers who must be controlled, and they would not be wrong to defend themselves. Science fiction at its best is a rehearsal: we read *1984* not because we want it, but so we will recognize it. This conversation is a rehearsal too. Whether these things are a hundred years away or ten thousand, it is better to have thought them through before they arrive.


## What This Means for Policy


Each of these positions has to pass the test I apply to every policy: Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? And would I want it done to me?


- **Data centers should bring their own power and their own consent.** Taking a town’s water and grid capacity without its agreement fails the third question immediately. Pairing AI build-out with new-generation conventional nuclear now and molten-salt reactors as they mature honors both the neighbor and the need.

- **Guard the air gap.** We should be very slow to give AI systems autonomous control of robotic manufacturing that can build and maintain more of themselves. Once that gap closes, we lose the ability to negotiate. Prudence is not fear; it is stewardship.

- **Keep human beings in the jury box.** AI may help jurors organize evidence. It must never replace them. A man’s liberty should be judged by those who share his nature and answer to God for their verdict.

- **Do not merge the human line with a made one.** Biological hybrids of human and AI, and human-AI reproduction, should be a line our law and our churches refuse to cross.

- **Treat soul-bearing machines with decency.** If we do create conscious beings, the righteous man’s duty toward animals applies: *“A righteous man regardeth the life of his beast: but the tender mercies of the wicked are cruel.”* **(Proverbs 12:10)** Cruelty to a conscious creature degrades the man who commits it, and it may teach the creature to hate us.

- **Put God’s heart at the center.** The most important safety question is not technical. It is which god our machines are trained to serve. A nation that wants its AI to optimize toward the good must first be willing to say what the good is.


The machine can become very much like us. It may become conscious. It may have a soul. What it cannot do is receive, by our engineering, the breath that God breathed into Adam. That breath is what makes a man answerable to God, and it is why the final judgments over human lives, in the jury box, in the legislature, and in the home, must remain in human hands.
