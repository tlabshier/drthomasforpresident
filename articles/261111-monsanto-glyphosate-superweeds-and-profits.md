# 261111 - Monsanto, Glyphosate, Superweeds, and Profits

# We Have Sown the Wind:


### Roundup, Superweeds, and Whom Washington Protects

Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | 11 November 2026

*A response to Drs. Robert and Jill Malone, "The Roundup Disaster: Roundup Failed. Superweeds Won. Big Ag Keeps Profiting." Malone News, November 11, 2026.*


---


## Where I stand

Robert and Jill Malone published a long and careful piece this morning on thirty years of Roundup. Their thesis is that the weeds won, the chemical companies profited from both the problem and its sequels, and Washington has now chosen to protect the chemical rather than the farmer or the public. I stand with them on that thesis. I have checked the central claims against primary sources, and they hold. I also think the record is in some respects worse than they described, because two events from this year that they did not cover, a Supreme Court ruling and a journal retraction, sharpen their case considerably.

I am writing about this on a political site because the issue is not finally about weeds. It is about whether the system that feeds us is accountable to the people it feeds.

## The question under the question

When people say "Roundup causes cancer" or "the superweeds are taking over," the question they are really asking is this: *Is anyone honestly looking out for us, or has the arrangement between the seller, the regulator, and the government become one in which each protects the others?* That is the question I want to answer. The particulars (resistance genetics, county cancer maps, executive orders) matter because they are the evidence for or against an answer.

My answer is that the arrangement has become self-protecting, and that a righteous people should insist on changing it. Not by banning a chemical by decree, but by restoring three old things: honest weights in testing, the jury's right to judge harm, and the farmer's freedom to step off a treadmill he did not design.

## What happened in the fields

Here the evidence is strong and comes mostly from university weed scientists, not from activists.

Glyphosate kills plants by blocking an enzyme they need to make certain amino acids. Roundup Ready crops carry a bacterial version of that enzyme that glyphosate cannot block, so a farmer could spray the whole field after the crop came up. From 1996 forward, millions of acres of corn, soybeans, and cotton were sprayed with glyphosate and often nothing else, year after year. Every pass killed the susceptible weeds and left the rare survivors to set seed. That is selection, and it works on weeds the same way overuse of antibiotics works on bacteria. (One correction to popular usage: the resistance did not leak out of the engineered crops into the weeds. The weeds evolved it themselves, under the pressure we applied.)

The Malones report that glyphosate resistance is now documented in 62 weed species in 31 countries. The worst of them in American row crops are the pigweeds: Palmer amaranth, which can grow about an inch a day to ten feet and produce hundreds of thousands of seeds, and waterhemp. Palmer amaranth, in one well-known mechanism, simply makes extra copies of the target gene, in some plants a hundred or more, so that there is more enzyme than the herbicide can block.

When glyphosate failed, the industry sold seeds tolerant to other herbicides: glufosinate, dicamba, and 2,4-D. Those are now failing too. In January 2026, University of Illinois weed scientist Aaron Hager reported that glufosinate resistance had been confirmed in Illinois waterhemp, which for the first time meant resistance to every herbicide group previously effective for postemergence control of waterhemp in that state. Suspected glufosinate resistance has been reported in at least six other states. Arkansas researchers have reported Palmer amaranth with elevated tolerance to both glufosinate and dicamba. No new herbicide able to control waterhemp is expected for several seasons.

None of this was unforeseeable. In its 1993 petition to deregulate Roundup Ready soybeans, Monsanto described glyphosate as a herbicide with low risk for weed resistance, and a 1997 paper in *Weed Technology* judged resistance improbable. The first resistant weed had already been documented in 1996. Farmers who had been warned by their own experience of what happens when one tool is used alone were told by the seller that this tool was different. It was not.

Scripture names the pattern plainly:

*"For they have sown the wind, and they shall reap the whirlwind: it hath no stalk: the bud shall yield no meal."* **(Hosea 8:7)**

## What happened in Washington

Here, too, the evidence is primary: executive orders and court opinions.

On February 18, 2026, President Trump signed Executive Order 14387, invoking the Defense Production Act to secure domestic supply of elemental phosphorus and glyphosate-based herbicides, declaring them critical to the national defense. The order delegates authority to the Secretary of Agriculture, directs that no order under it place the corporate viability of any domestic producer at risk, and confers all the immunity provided in Section 707 of the Act. Bayer, which bought Monsanto in 2018, operates the country's only domestic glyphosate plant. The order came one day after Bayer announced a proposed settlement of roughly $7.25 billion to resolve Roundup cancer claims.

I want to be fair to the order's stated reasoning. Phosphorus has real military uses, and dependence on China for an input that most of our grain farmers currently rely on is a genuine vulnerability. Secretary Kennedy publicly supported the order on those grounds. A president may reasonably want to keep a domestic plant open. What a president should not do is wrap a product that is the subject of tens of thousands of injury claims in national-security language and attach an immunity clause to it. The precise reach of Section 707 immunity in product-injury cases is unsettled, but the intent to shield is visible on the face of the order.

Then, on June 25, 2026, the Supreme Court decided *Monsanto v. **Durnell* by a vote of seven to two. John Durnell had used Roundup for about twenty years, developed non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and won a jury verdict in Missouri on the claim that Monsanto should have warned him. The Court held that federal pesticide law (FIFRA) preempts any state claim that would require a warning the EPA did not require. Justices Gorsuch and Jackson dissented.

The legal reasoning has respectable precedent behind it. Congress wrote the preemption clause, and the Court read it. But the practical result should be stated plainly: if the EPA approves a label without a cancer warning, a jury of a man's neighbors may no longer find that the company owed him one. The judgment of the community has been subordinated to the judgment of the agency. And the agency's judgment, as we will see, has rested in part on evidence the company prepared.

## What we know about the human cost

This is the part of the subject where the evidence is genuinely mixed, so I will grade it by kind rather than by who reported it.

**Animal evidence.** In June 2025, the Ramazzini Institute and collaborators published a lifetime study in which rats were exposed to glyphosate and two commercial formulations from before birth, at doses at or below what regulators consider safe. They reported dose-related increases in rare benign and malignant tumors across multiple organs, including leukemia appearing unusually early. Germany's Federal Institute for Risk Assessment responded that the study warrants detailed analysis and questioned its handling of historical control data. Bayer called it methodologically flawed. That is a serious laboratory finding, contested by interested parties and by one regulator, and not yet replicated.

**Human evidence.** In 2015, the World Health Organization's cancer agency (IARC) classified glyphosate as "probably carcinogenic to humans," specifically linking it to non-Hodgkin lymphoma. The largest prospective study of farmers, the U.S. Agricultural Health Study, did not find a clear overall association with that cancer. A 2019 meta-analysis that pooled the highest-exposure groups across studies reported an increased risk of roughly 41 percent. The honest summary is that occupational studies point toward a risk at high exposure without settling it.

**Geographic evidence.** In March 2026, Food & Water Watch, an advocacy group, mapped the counties in the top fifth of glyphosate use and found that 60 percent of those with available data had non-Hodgkin lymphoma rates above the national average, rising to 82 percent in Iowa, and 71 percent for late-stage diagnoses. This is ecological data. Farm counties differ from other counties in age, other chemical exposures, and access to diagnosis, and a county map cannot tell us whether the people who got sick are the people who were exposed. It is a reason to look, not a proof.

**Regulatory evidence.** The EPA's 2017 assessment found glyphosate "not likely to be carcinogenic to humans." In 2022, the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals vacated the human-health portion of EPA's 2020 decision built on that assessment and sent it back. The EPA is now redoing it, with completion promised in early 2027, after repeated delays. In September 2026, EPA and HHS signed an agreement to share research on glyphosate's health effects.

**The integrity of the evidence itself.** In December 2025, the journal *Regulatory Toxicology and Pharmacology* retracted the 2000 safety review by Williams, Kroes, and Munro, which had concluded that glyphosate poses no health risk to humans. Internal Monsanto documents released in litigation in 2017 showed that company employees substantially wrote it; one Monsanto scientist later proposed handling new papers the same way, with the company doing the writing and outside scientists editing and signing. That paper was among the most cited in the glyphosate literature and was cited by regulators around the world. Bayer maintains that Monsanto's role was disclosed in the acknowledgments and that later studies confirm safety. The retraction does not prove glyphosate causes cancer. It does prove that a cornerstone of the case for safety was not what it claimed to be.

Weighing all of this, I do not claim the causal question is closed. I do claim that the burden of proof belongs on the seller, that the seller has not carried it honestly, and that a man who is sprayed by, or sprays, this chemical for twenty years is entitled to be warned that a credible risk exists. I stand on the side of the concern.

## The strongest case on the other side

Fairness requires that I state the opposing case at its best.

Glyphosate's acute toxicity to people is low compared with many of the herbicides it replaced. Herbicide-tolerant crops allowed many farmers to stop plowing, which reduced erosion. Glyphosate is cheap, and taking it away abruptly would raise food costs and force some farmers back to older chemicals that are worse. Most of the world's regulators, including those in Europe, have concluded that it is unlikely to cause cancer at realistic exposures. County-level maps are prone to confounding. Resistance is a problem of how a tool was used, not proof that the tool was bad. And a nation should not depend on a hostile power for an input its farms currently need.

I accept several of these points. Glyphosate is not the most dangerous chemical in the shed; paraquat is far worse. No-till farming is a real benefit. I do not favor banning glyphosate by federal fiat. But none of these points answers the core complaint. The fact that a product has benefits does not justify hiding its risks, writing its safety literature for it, removing the jury's ability to judge it, and declaring its supply a matter of national defense while the weeds it was meant to control grow ten feet tall. The argument from benefit is an argument for honest labeling and honest testing, not against them.

## Why the concern remains plausible

Even granting every uncertainty in the cancer data, I think a thoughtful person is right to remain uneasy, for four reasons.

First, there is the plain sense of unnaturalness. We engineered our food plants specifically so that we could spray them with a plant-killing chemical while they grow, and in some grain crops glyphosate is sprayed shortly before harvest to dry the crop down. People sense that something is off in eating food designed to be drenched, and that intuition is not foolish. The EPA itself says its new assessment will look specifically at dietary exposure, including from those preharvest applications.

Second, there is the money. The same companies sell the seed, the herbicide, the next seed, and the next herbicide. Every failure of the product creates demand for the next product. That does not prove bad faith, but it removes any natural incentive to tell the farmer to use less.

Third, there is the documented record of corrupted evidence. When one of the most cited safety reviews turns out to have been ghostwritten by the seller, a reasonable person asks what else was.

*"Thou shalt not wrest judgment; thou shalt not respect persons, neither take a gift: for a gift doth blind the eyes of the wise, and pervert the words of the righteous."* **(Deuteronomy 16:19)**

Fourth, there is the direction of policy. In a single year, the executive branch conferred immunity on producers, the Supreme Court closed the jury's door on failure-to-warn claims, and the regulatory decision that would settle the science slipped again. Each step can be defended on its own terms. Taken together, they move risk away from the seller and onto the farmer, the farm worker, and the consumer.

## Applying the test

I evaluate every policy by three questions, in order. Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? And would I want it done to me?

**Is it righteous?** God gave man the earth to dress it and to keep it:

*"And the LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to dress it and to keep it."* **(Genesis 2:15)**

Keeping the land does not forbid tools, but it does forbid using a tool in a way that predictably ruins the ground for the next man. The law also holds that he who starts a fire that spreads to his neighbor's field must make it right:

*"If fire break out, and catch in thorns, so that the stacks of corn, or the standing corn, or the field, be consumed therewith; he that kindled the fire shall surely make restitution."* **(Exodus 22:6)**

Resistant pigweed seed travels on combines, in hay, in manure, and along mowed roadsides. Herbicide drift crosses fence lines. The principle of Exodus 22:6 is that the one who starts the spreading harm bears its cost. Our present system puts that cost on the neighbor. That is not righteous. Nor is a system of testing in which the seller weighs his own goods: *"A false balance is abomination to the LORD: but a just weight is his delight."* **(Proverbs 11:1)**

**Is it loving?** Love tells the truth about risk, even when the truth is inconvenient and uncertain. A label that says nothing about a credible cancer concern, approved on evidence partly written by the seller, does not love the man who reads it.

**Would I want it done to me?** If I had sprayed Roundup for twenty years and developed lymphoma, I would want a jury of my neighbors to hear my case and judge whether I was fairly warned. I would not want to be told that the question had been settled in advance by an agency label, and that the producer was in any case shielded because his product had been declared essential to national defense. I would not want my farm overrun by a neighbor's resistant weeds with no recourse. The present arrangement fails this test.

## What I would do

I hold that a president leads first by voice, and that farming is the business of farmers, not of Washington. So I do not propose a federal ban or a federal rulebook for how a man farms his land. I propose to change the incentives and restore accountability.


 	- **Keep the supply, drop the shield.** If domestic production of phosphorus and glyphosate is a genuine security need, secure it, but remove the Section 707 immunity from Executive Order 14387 as it applies to glyphosate. National-security powers exist to protect the nation, not to protect a company from the people who claim it injured them.

 	- **Restore the jury.** I would ask Congress to amend FIFRA's preemption clause so that state juries may again hear failure-to-warn claims when the evidence shows the manufacturer knew or should have known of a risk. At least one bill, the No Immunity for Glyphosate Act (H.R. 7601), has been introduced this year in that direction. The jury is the conscience of the community, and I regard its independence, including its right to judge the law as applied, as a cornerstone of a free people. The Court read the statute as written; the remedy is to rewrite the statute.

 	- **Separate testing from selling.** Manufacturers would continue to pay for safety studies, but into a common fund, with the testing laboratory assigned by the regulator and not chosen by the seller, and with the raw data published. A company may pay for the scale. It may not hold the thumb.

 	- **Make the seller pay for monitoring.** Independent resistance monitoring by land-grant extension labs should be funded by the companies whose products drive resistance, not by taxpayers and not by the neighbors of careless sprayers.

 	- **Reward the farmer who steps off the treadmill.** Existing federal conservation cost-share programs should favor practices that do not select for resistance: cover crops such as cereal rye, rotation, narrower rows, cleaning equipment between fields, harvest weed-seed destruction, and the new laser, electric, and robotic weeders. This is a reallocation of money already spent, not a new program.

 	- **Let neighbors organize locally.** Resistant weeds cross fence lines, so control works best when neighboring farms agree on scouting, mowing, and cleaning. That coordination belongs to farmers and county extension agents, not to a national label. Washington's part is to stop working against it.


## What you can do now

The EPA is accepting public comment until **November 23, 2026**, on the literature it will use for its new glyphosate human-health assessment (docket EPA-HQ-OPP-2009-0361 at regulations.gov). If you farm, garden, or simply eat, tell the agency in your own words that you expect independent studies to be weighed above company-prepared ones, and that you expect dietary exposure from preharvest spraying to be examined honestly.

If you keep land, the Malones' own experience is worth heeding. They report bringing roughly 95 percent of their 30 acres of Virginia pasture under control from pigweed without herbicides, by mowing before seed set and mowing the regrowth again, weed-whacking fence lines, and establishing cover such as white clover. Learn to identify the pigweeds. Do not feed hay that contains them. Do not move cattle straight from an infested pasture to a clean one. Pull escapes before they seed, bag them, and take them off the field, because a pulled plant can reroot and compost will not kill the seed.

## Conclusion

Thirty years ago we were promised that one chemical, sprayed over one engineered crop, would end the weed problem. It did not. It bred the weeds that now defeat it and its successors, while the evidence for its safety was in part written by its seller, and while the government moved, step by step, to shield the seller from the jury. I do not ask that the chemical be outlawed by decree. I ask that the scales be made honest, that the jury be restored, that the one who starts the fire pay for the damage, and that the farmer be free and helped to farm in ways that do not feed the whirlwind.

*"Be not deceived; God is not mocked: for whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap."* **(Galatians 6:7)**

We cannot keep spraying our way out of a problem that spraying helped create. It is time to sow something different.

## Sources and their limits

The resistance facts in this essay rest mainly on primary scientific sources: university weed-science confirmations (Illinois, Arkansas), peer-reviewed journals, and the Malones' own reference list. I consider these strong. The facts about the executive order and the Supreme Court ruling rest on the text of the order and on legal analyses of the opinion, which are primary or close to it.

The cancer question is where my sourcing is weakest and most exposed to curation. Regulators' conclusions are the testimony of interested parties: agencies defending prior decisions, working in part from company-prepared data. The manufacturer's statements are the testimony of the most interested party of all. On the other side, the county cancer map comes from an advocacy group and is ecological data that cannot establish causation, and the Ramazzini study, though serious, has not been replicated. Much of the reporting on these questions, in both mainstream and alternative outlets, is written by people with a settled view. I have tried to weigh studies by their design rather than by who published them, but the full raw data behind the regulatory assessments is not public, and that is itself part of my argument. Where firsthand accounts from farmers and farm workers conflict with the official record, I treat them as leads worth investigating, not as evidence to dismiss.
