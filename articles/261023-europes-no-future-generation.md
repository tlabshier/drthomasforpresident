# Europe’s “No-Future Generation”: What America Should Learn and Do


Oct 23, 2026 · Thomas Lee Abshier, ND


## Plain language summary


Young people in at least eight European countries are in the streets. They are protesting high rents, crowded schools, low pay, corruption, and a future that looks smaller than their parents’ past. Their governments, left, right, and center alike, have no good answer because they’ve already spent the money. Pensions for the old, interest on the debt, and now rearmament come first, and the young get what is left.


America is on the same road, perhaps ten years behind. The typical first-time homebuyer here is now forty. Social Security’s trust funds run dry in about eight years. Interest on the federal debt now costs more than national defense.


My position: America should respond to Europe’s crisis as a friend, not a master or a scold, and should prevent the same crisis here by doing three things. First, make energy abundant and cheap, because expensive energy makes everything expensive. Second, stop borrowing from our children; it is theft, and Scripture calls it so. Third, restore the family, the local school, and the local community as the first places a young man looks for his future, rather than a government that can no longer keep its promises.


*Provenance: Claude drafted this essay in my voice at my request on 23 October 2026, responding to the New York Times article listed under Resources. Figures were checked against current reporting; items still to be verified are listed in the editorial notes.*


## What is happening in Europe


Since September, high school students in France have been marching over school conditions. Their protests spread. Young Spaniards and Portuguese have pitched tent cities in Madrid and Lisbon over rents. Italian and French students have marched for better schools, and some of those marches turned into looting and riots. Greeks have protested university funding. Young Albanians joined an anti-corruption movement against their prime minister. The Times counts at least eight countries.


The demands differ, but the reporter found one common thread: the young fear for their future. A twenty-seven-year-old stagehand in Madrid called his peers “the no-future generation.” A psychologist in Lisbon asked what the point of working is if she cannot afford a house, pay her bills, or have a family. A fifteen-year-old French girl said her dream is a job she loves and not having to check her expenses every month.


The movement is not yet as large as the protests that followed the 2008 financial crisis. The grievances are not passing moods. They are the predictable outcome of choices European nations made over two generations, and the same choices are being made here.


## Why it happened: the bill has come due


The Times names the causes plainly. Europe’s population is aging. Its debts are rising. Governments are struggling to pay pensions for the old, schools for the young, and housing, transportation, and health care for everyone, and they are reluctant to raise taxes. Rising military spending, as the United States pulls back, adds to the strain. Fuel prices are soaring, and inflation and bond yields are rising.


Underneath the economics is a moral story.


**The state promised what only families and neighbors can provide.** For seventy years the European welfare state promised security from cradle to grave. A promise that size can be maintained only if each generation is larger and more productive than the last. But the promise itself undermined the conditions needed to keep it. When the state cares for you in old age, children become a cost rather than a necessity and a blessing. Birth rates fell below replacement across the continent. Now there are too few young workers to support the old, and the young can see that the benefits they pay for will not be there when they arrive.


**The old voted themselves the young’s inheritance.** **Proverbs 13:22** says a good man leaves an inheritance to his children’s children. Modern democracies have done the reverse. They borrowed against their grandchildren’s income to pay for their own comfort, and they called the borrowing compassion. The young protesters in Madrid feel this even when they cannot name it: “Things keep changing in a way that doesn’t benefit us.”


**Energy was made expensive on purpose.** Europe chose to shut down nuclear plants and price fossil fuels upward for climate policy, and it ended up depending on Russian gas. Expensive energy is a hidden tax on everything: food, transportation, manufacturing, and housing. When fuel prices spike, the poor and the young pay first, and the French protests turned explicitly to the cost of living and fuel.


**Housing became an investment rather than a home.** In Lisbon, the protest is against real-estate speculation. When land use is tightly restricted, and money is cheap for those who already have assets, housing becomes a store of wealth for the old and a barrier to marriage and family for the young.


**Corruption robs the young of hope that effort will be rewarded.** The Albanian students joined a movement against what they see as a government that sells the country’s assets to the connected. Corruption teaches the young that the game is rigged, and a young man who believes it is stops playing by its rules.


I do not say these things to mock Europe. These are our own errors, just at a more advanced stage.


## The test I apply


Before taking a position, I ask three questions:


- **Is it righteous?** Does it conform to what God has established as right, including His law against theft, false weights, and covetousness?

- **If so, is it loving?** Does it actually serve my neighbor’s good, rather than humor his appetite of the moment?

- **Would I want it done to me?** If I were a twenty-five-year-old renting a room in Madrid or Missoula, would I see this policy as justice and care, judged from the perspective of a soul committed to righteousness?


A government that borrows from the unborn fails the first question; it is theft from those who cannot consent. A government that promises benefits it knows it cannot pay fails the second; it is the love of a flatterer. And no young person, asked honestly, would want the bill for his grandparents’ comfort handed to him along with a smaller house and a later marriage. These policies fail all three questions.


## How America should respond to Europe


**As a friend, not a master and not a scold.** Europe’s young are our neighbors in the sense Christ meant. Their pain is real. Americans should not gloat over a continent in trouble, and we should not use their disorder as a talking point in our own partisan fights. The proper posture is the one we would want if the roles were reversed: honest counsel, fair dealing, and help where help is asked for.


**By not adding to their burden.** Much of Europe’s new military spending follows from American policy decisions. Whatever one thinks of those decisions, a friend tells his allies plainly, early, and consistently what he will and will not do, so they can plan. Sudden reversals and threats against allies’ territory teach them America cannot be trusted, pushing frightened nations to borrow more.


**By selling them energy, and later teaching them to make it.** The single most helpful thing America can offer Europe is energy: natural gas now, and, in time, thorium molten-salt reactor technology that I believe should become the foundation of our own economy. Cheap energy lowers the cost of everything. This is trade between free peoples, not charity, and it serves both sides.


**By keeping our own house clean abroad.** The Albanian movement is named for a bird habitat where a development backed by the family of an American president’s son-in-law won government approval. I make no judgment on the merits of that particular deal. My point is general and applies to every party: when relatives of those who hold American power do business with foreign governments during that power, the appearance alone damages America’s name and the cause of honest government everywhere. As president, I would ask Congress to bar the president’s immediate family and his cabinet from accepting concessions or contracts from foreign governments during the term of office, and I would hold my own family to that standard without waiting for a law.


**By distinguishing protest from riot.** Peaceable assembly to petition government for redress of grievances is a right, and it is righteous. Looting and burning are crimes against neighbors, often against small shopkeepers no richer than the protesters. The answer to riot is impartial justice: arrest, charge, and trial before a jury of the accused’s peers, applied equally regardless of the rioters’ politics. I am wary of leaders on any side who use disorder as a pretext for soldiers in the streets and blanket blame of a disfavored group. “Restored authority” without righteousness is only a different master.


## How to prevent the same crisis here


America is not Europe. We have a younger population, more energy in the ground, and a stronger tradition of private enterprise and church charity. But the trend lines are the same.


- The National Association of Realtors reported in November 2025 that the median age of first-time homebuyers reached **40**, a record. In 1981, it was 29. The share of first-time buyers fell to a record low of 21 percent.

- The 2026 Social Security Trustees project the combined trust funds to be depleted in **2034**, after which incoming taxes would pay only about 83 percent of scheduled benefits.

- Federal interest costs passed **$1.2 trillion** in the first eleven months of fiscal 2026, more than the nation spent on defense.

- Gallup reported in May 2026 that the gap between young and older Americans’ views of the job market is wider than in any other of the 141 countries it surveys.


These are the same symptoms that put Europe’s young in tents. Here is what I would do.


### 1. Energy: make it abundant and cheap


This is the foundation of my platform, the E in A New ERA. Abundant energy is the closest thing to a free lunch that God placed in creation. It lowers the cost of food, housing, transportation, and manufacturing at once, and it lets a young man build something with his hands and earn a living wage doing it.


- Remove the regulatory barriers that make new nuclear power in America take decades and cost billions before a single watt is delivered, and build thorium molten-salt reactors at scale.

- Stop using regulation to raise the price of existing energy sources while we make the transition.

- Treat cheap, reliable power as a moral good, because it is a gift to the poor.


### 2. Righteousness: stop stealing from the young


- **No new borrowing for current consumption.** Debt for a war of survival or a true emergency can be righteous. Debt to pay this year’s bills is theft from those not yet born. I would veto it and ask Congress to propose a constitutional amendment banning it.

- **Tell the truth about Social Security.** I have written for twenty years that the trust fund is an IOU the young will be taxed to pay. Those who paid in under a promise must be honored; that is a debt of justice. But the young must be told plainly what they will and will not receive, and allowed to build real, owned savings that the government cannot spend.

- **Honest money.** Inflation transfers wealth from wage earners and savers to asset owners. A young family saving for a home is the first casualty. Sound money depends on honest weights and measures **(Proverbs 11:1)**.


### 3. Restore the family and the household economy


The deepest reason Europe’s young have no future is that the society stopped planning for them to have children. A nation that doesn’t raise children has no future to promise anyone.


- **Housing for families.** Most housing rules are local and should stay local. But Washington should stop rewarding supply restrictions, end federal programs that inflate prices while claiming to help buyers, and favor family ownership over large-scale investor purchases of starter homes.

- **Let parents keep what they earn.** Substantially raise the tax exemption for each child. Raising a child is a nation’s most important investment, and the tax code should treat it as such.

- **Let marriage pay.** Remove the marriage penalties hidden in the tax code and in welfare programs.


### 4. Education that leads to work and to a home


The French students are right that their schools are failing them. Ours are failing many of ours, too, at great expense.


- Return education to parents and local communities. Fund the student, not the system, and let families choose, including home education.

- Honor the trades. Apprenticeships should be as respected a path as a four-year degree, and federal student loan policy should stop pushing young people into debt for degrees that don’t lead to work.

- Prepare the young for a world changed by artificial intelligence and robotics. That world may bring abundance or a new monopoly of the machines, and the difference will depend on whether ordinary people own a share of what the machines produce.


### 5. Accountability: give the young a real voice


Young Americans distrust both parties, and they have reason. Accountability means returning power to people who can see what is happening.


- Return decisions to the level of government closest to the problem: family, community, and state before Washington.

- Restore the jury as a check on unjust law and prosecution, including the jury’s ancient right to refuse to convict under a law it judges unrighteous.

- Make every federal program expire unless Congress renews it, so one generation’s old commitments don’t become the next generation’s permanent chains.


## Why the concern is real, and why it applies to us


Some will say Europe’s protests are mostly organized by the hard left, or are simply the restlessness of youth, and that America is different. Parts of that are true. Some of these movements carry ideologies I oppose; some protesters want more of the same government spending that caused the problem, and the numbers are still modest.


But I side with the young people’s complaint, even as I reject their proposed cure. They are right that they have been handed the bill. They are right that they cannot afford a home or a family on honest work. They are right that their governments are telling them less than the truth. Their intuition that “things keep changing in a way that doesn’t benefit us” is accurate, and it is accurate here as well.


The concern is plausible for three reasons that do not depend on any single statistic.


First, the money motive is plain. Every interest group that benefits from current spending has a reason to keep the young from seeing the full bill, and those groups are older, richer, and more likely to vote.


Second, the system rewards the behavior. A welfare state that replaces the family as provider will, over time, produce fewer families. It has done so everywhere it has been tried, and no amount of policy fine-tuning has reversed it.


Third, there is something unnatural about a society in which the old consume the inheritance of the young. Every healthy culture in history has understood that parents sacrifice for children. When that order reverses, the young sense the wrongness before they can articulate it, and eventually take to the streets.


America has a short window to choose a different road. We do not need to wait for tent cities in our own capitals to act. The answer is neither more government nor no government, but righteous government: honest about money, generous with energy, and humble enough to return the care of persons to families, churches, and communities, where God placed it.


I offer this position as an argument, not a decree. If someone has a better way to give our young people a future, I want to hear it.


## Open questions and editorial notes


### Questions still open


- **Social Security transition.** This essay states the principle (honor the promise to those who paid in; give the young owned savings) but not the transition mechanics. That belongs in a future installment of the Social Security series.

- **Family-size tax measure.** The per-child exemption amount is not specified. Should it be pegged to the actual average cost of raising a child?

- **Foreign-business bar for officeholders’ families.** Scope needs definition: immediate family only, or extended family; foreign governments only, or foreign state-linked companies as well?


### Editorial notes


- **NAR figures.** Median first-time buyer age of 40 and the 21 percent share are from NAR’s 2025 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers (transactions July 2024–June 2025, released November 2025). The 1981 median of 29 is from the same coverage. Check whether a 2026 Profile has been released before publication; it would normally appear in early November.

- **Social Security.** 2034 combined OASDI depletion and roughly 83 percent payable are from the CRS summary of the 2026 Trustees Report. OASI alone is projected to be depleted in late 2032.

- **Interest costs.** The $1.27 trillion figure for the first eleven months of FY2026 and the comparison with defense are from secondary reporting; verify against the Treasury Monthly Statement before publication.

- **Gallup.** The 141-country comparison is from Gallup World Poll coverage in May 2026 (Atlanta Journal-Constitution).

- **Albania.** The description of the Flamingo movement and the Kushner-backed development is taken from the Times article. The essay deliberately makes no judgment on the deal itself.

- **Defense spending.** The paragraph on American policy and allies’ rearmament is written as principle, not as criticism of any named decision.
