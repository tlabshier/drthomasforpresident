# 260819 - Spent Nuclear Fuel as Resource

# Buried Talent: Spent Nuclear Fuel, a Broken Promise, and the Proper Work of Government


**A Position Paper by Thomas Lee Abshier, ND** | 19* September 2026*


---


## Framing Note and Disclosure


This paper responds to [“Unlocking America’s Nuclear Gold Mine: The Promise of Spent Fuel,”](https://newswithviews.com/unlocking-americas-nuclear-gold-mine-the-promise-of-spent-fuel/) by Ronald Stein, Steve Curtis, and Olivia Vaughan (NewsWithViews, September 17, 2026). I summarize their argument in my own words and do not reproduce it. The reader who wants their case in full should read it at the link.


The reader should weigh one fact. By their own biographies, Mr. Curtis is Chief Technical Officer of a company formed to advance fast-reactor recycling, and Ms. Vaughan is CEO of a nuclear innovation company. That does not make them wrong. Men who know a field are usually the ones who build in it. But when an author would be paid under the policy he recommends, the reader should check the argument with extra care.


---


## Part I: What the Authors Argue, and How It Holds Up


### The argument


The authors welcome the friendlier national tone toward nuclear power but say the tone has been mistaken for progress. They point to the Department of Energy’s Nuclear Lifecycle Innovation Campus program, where five states have signed only non-binding memoranda and none has agreed to accept spent fuel. They argue that federal “consent-based siting” is a process that measures itself by meetings and reports. They then propose a reframe: used fuel (which they call “slightly used nuclear fuel”) still holds most of its energy, so it is an asset. Their remedy is to let private enterprise lead, seeded with money already collected in the Nuclear Waste Fund, in states that volunteer to host.


### What checks out


- **The program is as they describe it.** DOE named Utah, Tennessee, Oklahoma, Louisiana, and Idaho as initial contenders, and the Secretary of Energy signed non-binding memoranda with them on July 24, 2026. Any campus that advances would need a separate, legally binding agreement. The request for information to the states went out on January 28, 2026.

- **The backlog is real.** More than 95,000 metric tons of spent fuel sit at dozens of temporary storage sites, and the Government Accountability Office has reported that the inventory grows by roughly 2,000 metric tons a year.

- **The federal failure is real and has a price.** The 1982 law charged nuclear utilities a tenth of a cent per kilowatt-hour, and in return the Energy Department was to begin disposing of spent fuel by the end of January 1998. When it did not, the utilities went to court. Taxpayers now pay utilities up to $800 million a year in damages. The total has reached about $11 billion since 1998 and is projected to reach as much as $44.5 billion.

- **The money is tied up.** The Nuclear Waste Fund holds roughly $51 billion collected from ratepayers, and current law restricts its use to the dormant Yucca Mountain project unless Congress acts.

- **Resistance is real.** Nevada has long resisted Yucca Mountain, and Texas and New Mexico have pushed back more recently against private interim storage.


### What needs proportion


I have called hype “lying by proportion,” so I must apply that test to a conclusion I find congenial.


**The 95 percent figure.** It is true in principle. Used fuel from our present reactors is roughly 95 percent uranium, nearly all of it uranium-238, plus about one percent plutonium and a few percent fission products. Our light-water reactors cannot burn that uranium-238, but a fast reactor can. So the energy is there, but it can be reached only with machines the United States does not operate commercially today. We ran an experimental fast reactor in Idaho for thirty years. Russia runs two at power-station scale. France has reprocessed fuel for decades but recycles it into ordinary reactors, which recovers only a modest share. Reprocessing has struggled commercially because fresh uranium is cheap, not because the physics is doubtful.


**The “gold mine.”** Gold can be sold at today’s price, and used fuel cannot. A fairer comparison is an ore body that needs a mill nobody has built yet. The ore is still worth a great deal. But a paper that calls it a gold mine invites the reader to expect revenue next year, and what he will actually get is a construction program that runs for decades.


**“Beyond government grants.”** This is the argument’s weak joint. The authors criticize grant-driven policy, and then they propose to start private companies with the Nuclear Waste Fund. Unless the money is tied to delivered results, that is simply another grant. I think the proposal can be repaired, and Position 4 below is my repair.


**An omission.** The piece does not mention why America stopped reprocessing. Separating pure plutonium is a weapons-proliferation risk, and that concern drove the 1977 decision to defer commercial reprocessing. The ban was lifted in 1981, but the concern did not go away. Any honest recycling policy has to address it.

## Part II: Commentary


Underneath the engineering there are three moral questions.


### A promise was made and not kept


The government took money from ratepayers for a service with a contractual deadline, and it has not delivered the service for twenty-eight years. The courts have been plain about it. In *Indiana Michigan Power Co. v. Department of Energy* (D.C. Cir. 1996) the obligation was held to be unconditional. In 2013 the same court ordered the fee reduced to zero, because the government could not justify charging for a program it was not running.


The damages are paid from general tax revenue, so the citizen has paid twice: once on his electric bill for the service, and again on his tax return for its absence. The fund’s balance is held as Treasury obligations, which means the cash was spent long ago and the fund is a promise backing a promise.


I do not raise this to inflame anyone. A nation that wants its citizens to keep their word must keep its own, and this is the longest-running broken commercial promise on the federal books.


### Someone must live next to it


The authors are right that consent has so far produced no host. I draw a different lesson from that than impatience with consent. The standard I apply to every public act is whether the neighbor affected by it would feel loved. For the people of a county asked to receive the nation’s used fuel, that test requires four things:


- the truth about the risk, stated without minimizing;

- binding terms rather than memoranda;

- a real share of the benefit;

- the power to say no before the first cask arrives.


Consent has been slow because trust is low, and trust is low because of the broken promise I just described. Overriding consent would not fix that. Becoming a government whose word is worth accepting would.


### What do we owe our grandchildren?


We have two ways to fail them. We can leave them our waste to guard, or we can bury beyond recovery a resource they would have used. Dry-cask storage is safe for many decades, which gives us time to do this properly. But having time does not excuse sitting idle for a third generation.


### Why government-only has failed


I agree with the authors that a government-only approach has failed, and the reason deserves stating. Government is a competent umpire and a poor player. An agency loses nothing when a deadline slips, while an investor loses his capital. That difference in incentive is a moral matter as well as an economic one, because it determines who bears the cost of delay. For half a century the citizen has borne it.

## Positions


I hold these as advocacy with reasons, and I am open to a better argument.


### 1. Keep the promise, and say plainly that it was broken.


I would ask Congress to amend the Nuclear Waste Policy Act so the fund can be used for the purpose ratepayers paid for, at sites other than Yucca Mountain alone. I would also have the government publish each year, in one place, what has been collected, what has been paid in damages, and what is still owed. Only Congress can appropriate (Art. I, §9). A President’s part is to propose, to press, and to direct the Department to perform.


*Moral ground:* “He that sweareth to his own hurt, and changeth not” (Psalm 15:4). “Better is it that thou shouldest not vow, than that thou shouldest vow and not pay” (Ecclesiastes 5:5).


### 2. Consent is a requirement and not merely a tactic.


No state or county should receive used fuel without a binding agreement ratified by its own elected bodies. The agreement should include enforceable federal commitments, a share of the revenue, independent monitoring that the community chooses, and a right to withdraw before delivery begins.


*Moral ground:* “All things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them” (Matthew 7:12). If I would not accept the arrangement for my own county on these terms, I have no business offering it to another.


### 3. Store retrievably, and treat the material as a trust.


Until recycling proves itself or fails, used fuel should be kept where it can be recovered. We should neither bury the resource irretrievably nor pretend it is already wealth.


*Moral ground:* The servant condemned in the parable of the talents had not lost his master’s money. He had buried it (Matthew 25:25). After the feeding of the five thousand the instruction was, “Gather up the fragments that remain, that nothing be lost” (John 6:12).


### 4. Pay for performance and not for promises.


If Waste Fund money goes to private enterprise, it should be released per ton of fuel actually received, secured, and processed under license. It should not be paid as up-front grants. Private capital should go in first and be lost first. Operators should post bonds for decommissioning and carry their liability, so that the profits and the risks stay with the same party. This is the change that makes the authors’ proposal consistent with their own criticism of grants.


*Moral ground:* “The labourer is worthy of his hire” (Luke 10:7), which means the wage is owed for work done. “A false balance is abomination to the LORD: but a just weight is his delight” (Proverbs 11:1). An arrangement in which investors keep the gains and citizens absorb the losses is a false balance.


### 5. Government should set the rules and stay out of the game.


That means:


- clear licensing rules with definite timelines;

- rigorous safety and transport standards;

- a firm preference for recycling methods that never isolate weapons-usable plutonium.


Within those rules, builders should be free to build, to fail, and to succeed at their own expense.


*Moral ground:* “For rulers are not a terror to good works, but to the evil” (Romans 13:3). The ruler’s office is to restrain harm and to keep the scales honest. It is not to run the mill.


### 6. Speak in proportion.


Used fuel is neither a gold mine nor a curse that lasts forever. It is a dangerous and valuable material that a competent nation can manage, and ours has chosen not to for forty years. Citizens can handle that fact and should be told it plainly.


*Moral ground:* “Lying lips are abomination to the LORD: but they that deal truly are his delight” (Proverbs 12:22).


---


## Closing


The authors end with a choice between talking and building. I would put the order differently. A nation must repent before it can repair. It must admit that it took payment for a duty it did not perform, and then it must perform that duty by means that treat the host community as a neighbor and the investor as a man who should bear his own risk. If we do that, the material now stored in seventy-odd cask yards may well light our grandchildren’s homes. If we skip it, we will have found a new way to spend the same money without solving the problem.
