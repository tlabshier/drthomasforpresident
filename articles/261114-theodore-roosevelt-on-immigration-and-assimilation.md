# 261114 - Theodore Roosevelt on Immigration and Assimilation

# One Flag, One Language, One Loyalty:


### What Roosevelt Actually Wrote, and Why It Still Holds

*By Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | 14 November 2026*

A friend's post on Facebook carried a newspaper clipping titled *"Teddy had it so right! Not Kennedy, but Roosevelt!"* Under a portrait of Theodore Roosevelt it printed a passage on immigrants and loyalty, labeled "Theodore Roosevelt's ideas on Immigrants and being an AMERICAN in 1907." The clipping has been passed around by email and social media for twenty years. (The headline plays on the nickname "Teddy," which Roosevelt shared with Senator Edward Kennedy, who was pressing an immigration bill when the chain email began circulating in 2006.)

Before I build anything on a quotation, I want to know whether the man actually said it. So I checked. The short answer is that Roosevelt did write these words, but not in 1907, and not exactly as the clipping prints them. The correct version is better than the meme, and the circumstances in which he wrote it give it more weight, not less.

## What Roosevelt actually wrote

The passage comes from a typed letter dated January 3, 1919, from Roosevelt to Richard M. Hurd, president of the American Defense Society. The letter is held in the Theodore Roosevelt Papers at the Library of Congress, and the Theodore Roosevelt Center at Dickinson State University has digitized it. It was read aloud at a Society meeting on January 5, 1919. Roosevelt died in his sleep the next morning, January 6. It is among the last public statements he made.

Here is the passage in his own words:
> "Our principle in this matter should be absolutely simple. In the first place, we should insist that if the immigrant who comes here in good faith becomes an American and assimilates himself to us, he shall be treated on an exact equality with everyone else, for it is an outrage to discriminate against any such man because of creed, or birthplace, or origin.
>
> "But this is predicated upon the man's becoming in very fact an American, and nothing but an American. If he tries to keep segregated with men of his own origin and separated from the rest of America, then he isn't doing his part as an American.
>
> "There can be no divided allegiance here. Any man who says he is an American, but something else also, isn't an American at all.
>
> "We have room for but one flag, the American flag, and this excludes the red flag, which symbolizes all wars against liberty and civilization, just as much as it excludes any foreign flag of a nation to which we are hostile.
>
> "We have room for but one language here, and that is the English language, for we intend to see that the crucible turns our people out as Americans, of American nationality, and not as dwellers in a polyglot boarding-house; and we have room for but one sole loyalty, and that is loyalty to the American people."


## What the clipping changed

The clipping is substantially genuine, but it differs from the original in several ways:


 	- **The date.** It says 1907, when Roosevelt was President. The letter was written in January 1919, ten years after he left office and three days before his death.

 	- **One phrase.** "The man's becoming in very fact an American" became "the person's becoming in every facet an American." The meaning survives, but the words are not his.

 	- **Omissions.** The clipping uses ellipses to drop the sentence about immigrants who keep "segregated with men of his own origin," the explanation that "one flag" excluded both the revolutionary red flag and the flag of any hostile nation, and the image of America as a "polyglot boarding-house."


None of these changes reverses Roosevelt's meaning. But they matter. If I quote a man, I owe him his own words, and I owe my readers the truth about when and why he said them. When it is passed along with the wrong date and altered words, a true statement becomes easy to dismiss. Anyone who wants to wave away the principle has only to point out that the citation is wrong. The correct citation is:

**Theodore Roosevelt, letter to Richard M. Hurd, American Defense Society, January 3, 1919. Theodore Roosevelt Papers, Library of Congress Manuscript Division.**

## The setting

Roosevelt wrote this letter seven weeks after the Armistice ended the First World War. Between 1900 and 1915 more than fifteen million immigrants had come to America. That was about as many as had arrived in the previous forty years combined, and most of them came from countries where English was not spoken. The war had pitted the homelands of many of these immigrants against one another and against the United States. The Bolshevik revolution in Russia was a little over a year old, and its red flag was being carried in American streets. Roosevelt's "red flag" is not a figure of speech. He meant the international revolutionary movement that asked its members for a loyalty above any nation.

He had been making the same argument for years. In a Fourth of July speech in 1917 he said that we must have in this country "but one flag, and for the speech of the people but one language, the English language." At Nebraska's statehood semicentennial in June 1917, he left his prepared text to tell the crowd that he never would have become president if he had learned and spoken only Dutch, the language of his own ancestors. Roosevelt was not attacking anyone else's heritage. He was speaking as a descendant of immigrants who had made the same passage himself.

## Where I stand

I stand with Roosevelt. His principle is right, and it is right for reasons deeper than national convenience.

Notice the structure of what he said. His first sentence is not a demand on the immigrant. It is a demand on us. If a man comes in good faith and becomes an American, he is to be treated on an exact equality with everyone else, and to discriminate against him because of his creed, birthplace, or origin is "an outrage." Only after that promise does Roosevelt state the condition: that the man actually become an American, and nothing else alongside it. This is a covenant with two sides. The nation pledges full equality and the newcomer pledges undivided allegiance. Each side has something to give and something to receive.

That is the pattern of the most famous immigrant in Scripture. Ruth the Moabitess did not come to Israel asking to remain a Moabite with an Israelite address. She said: *"Intreat me not to leave thee, or to return from following after thee: for whither thou goest, I will go; and where thou lodgest, I will lodge: thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God"* **(Ruth 1:16)**. Israel received her so completely that she became the great-grandmother of King David and an ancestor of Christ. Her full assimilation was met with full acceptance.

The law given to Israel made the same two-sided promise. On one side: *"But the stranger that dwelleth with you shall be unto you as one born among you, and thou shalt love him as thyself; for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt"* **(Leviticus 19:34)**. On the other: *"One law shall be to him that is homeborn, and unto the stranger that sojourneth among you"* **(Exodus 12:49)**. The stranger is loved as one born among the people, and he lives under the same law as everyone else. He does not get a separate law, and he is not left outside the community.

The principle of undivided allegiance is older than Roosevelt and older than America: *"No man can serve two masters: for either he will hate the one, and love the other; or else he will hold to the one, and despise the other"* **(Matthew 6:24)**. Christ was speaking of God and money, but He was stating a law of the human heart. In the end, loyalty is a single thing. A man may love his mother's cooking, his grandfather's songs, and the hills his family left behind. But when the interests of two nations conflict, he can stand with only one of them.

## Testing the principle

I put every policy to three questions. Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? And would I want it done to me?

**Is it righteous?** I believe it is. A nation is a people bound together by a shared law, a shared story, and shared obligations. God established nations: He *"hath determined the times before appointed, and the bounds of their habitation"* **(Acts 17:26)**. Asking those who join a nation to join it fully is not arrogance. It is honesty about what a nation is. The naturalization oath already asks this, and it says it plainly. The new citizen swears that he will *"absolutely and entirely renounce and abjure all allegiance and fidelity to any foreign prince, potentate, state, or sovereignty."* Roosevelt was not inventing a new standard. He was asking us to mean the oath we already administer.

**Is it loving?** Here I think Roosevelt's principle is more loving than the alternatives that sound kinder. An immigrant who never learns English is confined to a narrow world. He depends on intermediaries to understand his own government, his doctor, his landlord, and the courts. His children translate for him, and the natural order of the family is turned upside down. He is open to exploitation by employers who prefer a worker who cannot read his own contract. Treating a newcomer as a permanent guest who need not learn the household's language is not a welcome. It is a polite form of exclusion. Love wants the stranger to become a full member of the family, sitting at the table, not a tenant renting a room in what Roosevelt called the "polyglot boarding-house."

**Would I want it done to me?** If I moved to Japan intending to live and die there and raise my grandchildren as Japanese citizens, I would want the Japanese to expect me to learn their language, honor their law, and give my loyalty to their nation. I would also want them to promise that once I had done so, they would never treat me as a second-class citizen. That is exactly Roosevelt's bargain. I would accept it gladly, and I would think less of a nation that asked less of me.

## Where the principle has been abused

I have to be honest about the history, because the strongest objection to Roosevelt's letter comes from the organization he wrote it to.

The American Defense Society, with Roosevelt on its advisory board, did not stop at urging immigrants to learn English. In 1918 it called for a strict ban on teaching the German language, and at the end of that year it launched a national campaign to eliminate German instruction altogether. Across the country, German teachers lost their jobs, towns and streets with German names were renamed, and families changed their surnames out of fear. By the summer of 1918 about half the states had restricted or banned German. In some places, Society officials encouraged local "vigilance corps" to watch their neighbors' speech and report anyone they considered disloyal. Nebraska passed a law forbidding the teaching of any foreign language to young children, even in private and church schools. In *Meyer v. Nebraska* (1923), the Supreme Court struck that law down, holding that parents have the right to have their children taught a language other than English.

The Court was right, and I will say so plainly. A Lutheran congregation teaching its children to read Luther's Bible in German was not a threat to the Republic. A grandmother singing to her grandchildren in Spanish or Tagalog is not an act of disloyalty. When the state goes into the home, the church, and the private school to police which words a family may speak, it has confused unity with uniformity and loyalty with fear. Such laws fail all three of my questions. They are not righteous, because they punish the innocent along with the guilty. They are not loving, because they shame people instead of drawing them in. And no one would want them done to himself.

But this qualification must itself be qualified. The abuse of a principle does not refute it. The German-language panic of 1918 shows what happens when a sound demand on the heart is turned into a heavy hand of government. It does not show that the demand was wrong. Roosevelt's letter asks the immigrant to *become* something: to adopt English as the common speech of public life and America as the single object of his political loyalty. That is a matter of character and choice. The proper tools for it are invitation, expectation, teaching, and the example of the citizens already here. Fines, informants, and book burnings are not. My own principle of leadership is that the President leads chiefly by his voice. He persuades the nation of what is right and calls on families, churches, and local school boards to act on it. On this issue Roosevelt's voice was his strongest tool. The Society's coercion was its weakest.

## What I would do

These are the positions I take, held with my reasons and open to a better argument:


 	- **Make English the official language by law.** In March 2025 an executive order designated English as the official language of the United States. That is a good start, but a future president can undo an executive order with a stroke of the pen. Congress has tried for decades and never passed such a law. It should. The law should cover the business of the federal government: its laws, ballots, forms, and proceedings. It should not regulate what any citizen speaks at home, at church, or in his own shop.

 	- **Pair the expectation with real help.** If we ask newcomers to learn English, we owe them a real way to do it. I would rather see churches, libraries, and local volunteers teaching English to their immigrant neighbors than see one more federal program. The expectation and the help belong together, just as Roosevelt put the promise of equality and the demand of allegiance in the same sentence.

 	- **Mean the oath.** The naturalization oath renounces every foreign allegiance. I believe that anyone who holds high federal office, sits in Congress, or holds a security clearance should hold one citizenship only, and that is ours. For ordinary citizens I would not strip anyone of a second passport by force of law. But I would say plainly, as Roosevelt did, that loyalty to America cannot be shared with another nation.

 	- **Teach the story.** A people stays one people by knowing its own history: the Declaration, the Constitution, the Revolution, the Civil War, and the faith that shaped the founders. Education is local, and I will not ask for federal money or federal control here. I will use the President's voice to urge every school board to teach American history and civic duty without apology, and to make sure every child, whether native-born or newly arrived, learns why this nation is worth his loyalty.

 	- **Hold the other side of the bargain.** Roosevelt called discrimination against the assimilated immigrant "an outrage." I agree. A citizen who took the oath in good faith is as American as I am, and any law, policy, or habit of speech that treats him otherwise breaks our side of the covenant.


## Why the concern remains plausible

Some will say Roosevelt's anxiety belonged to 1919 and that the melting pot has always worked out in the end. I am not persuaded, for several reasons.

First, there is the simple, intuitive sense that a country whose people cannot speak to one another is not one country. Self-government is government by conversation: town meetings, newspapers, campaigns, and above all juries. Federal law already requires a juror to read, write, and understand English, and for good reason. I hold that the jury's power to judge the law as well as the facts is a cornerstone of American liberty. A juror cannot judge a law by his conscience if he cannot understand the words of the law. A common language is not a cultural preference. It is a working part of a free republic.

Second, there are money motives that work against assimilation. A worker who cannot read English cannot easily organize, complain, find a better job, or read his own contract. Some employers find that convenient. There are also political motives. A voting bloc that stays separate, speaks its own language, and gets its news through its own intermediaries is easier to deliver on election day than a body of independent citizens. When powerful interests profit from keeping newcomers apart, we should not expect assimilation to happen by itself.

Third, the means of separation have multiplied since Roosevelt's day. In 1919 an immigrant who wanted news from home waited weeks for a letter. Today he can live in his native language entirely: satellite television, social media, and video calls with family abroad, all day long. Some foreign governments now actively court their diasporas abroad as instruments of influence. The pull toward a divided allegiance is stronger now than it was when Roosevelt warned against it.

Finally, there is the question under the question. The people who keep sharing this clipping, twenty years after it first circulated, are not mainly interested in the date of a letter. They are reaching for something they sense has been lost: the conviction that America asks something of those who join it, and that what it asks is good for them. That conviction rested on a confidence that our nation, with all its sins, is worth joining wholeheartedly. When we stop asking newcomers to become Americans, we are quietly saying that there is nothing in particular to become. I do not believe that. I believe America is a nation founded on the truth that our rights come from God, and that becoming fully part of it is a privilege worth asking of anyone and worth offering to everyone.

## The strongest case on the other side

The strongest objection is this: human beings naturally hold layered loyalties. A man can love his family, his town, his state, his church, and the country of his grandparents without betraying America. The Irish-American who marches on St. Patrick's Day and the Italian-American who keeps his grandmother's recipes are no less loyal for it, and immigrants who served in our wars proved that a hyphen did not make them less American. Speaking a second language is an asset to the nation, not a threat. And the record of 1918 shows how easily "100 percent Americanism" turns into persecution of loyal citizens.

I accept most of that, and I believe Roosevelt's actual words accept it too. He did not object to memory, affection, or heritage. He objected to *allegiance*: to the man who says he is an American "but something else also," to the immigrant who keeps himself "segregated" and "separated from the rest of America," and to the flag of a hostile nation or of a revolution against liberty. Loving one's grandmother's songs is affection. Placing a foreign government's interests above America's is allegiance. The first is human and good. The second cannot be shared. I would add only this: a second language is an asset when it is spoken alongside English, and a burden on its speaker when it is spoken in place of English.

## Conclusion

Three days before he died, Theodore Roosevelt wrote one more letter on the subject that had occupied him through the war. He asked for very little that a good host and a good guest should not already want: equality for the newcomer, and in return the newcomer's whole heart. One flag, one language for our public life, and one loyalty.

The clipping got the year wrong. It did not get the principle wrong. I stand with Roosevelt, and with Ruth before him: *thy people shall be my people.* That is the invitation America should extend, and the answer it should hope to receive.

**Limits.** The wording of Roosevelt's letter is drawn from published transcriptions that match the digitized original. My account of the 1918 German-language campaign relies mostly on mainstream historical and encyclopedic sources.
