# 231026 - Surveillance, Liberty and Security

# Watching the Wicked Without Watching Everyone


## A Position Paper on Surveillance, Privacy, and the Fourth Amendment


*Dr. Thomas — October 2026*


---


## My Position in Brief


I stand for this: government should watch the wicked, and it should need a judge’s warrant before it reads the private words of any American.


Gathering intelligence on foreign enemies abroad is a righteous duty of government. Rummaging through the private communications of Americans without a warrant is not — whether those communications were swept up “incidentally,” bought from a data broker, or opened through a weakness deliberately engineered into the encryption that protects them.


The ideal answer to the surveillance problem would be a truly righteous society, in which those who watch the bad men never turn what they learn against the innocent, never use it for political advantage, and never use it for blackmail. I hope and work for that society. But we do not live in it yet, and the men who hold the keys are men, subject to the same temptations as the rest of us. So the law must be built for the men we have, not the angels we wish we had. That means warrants, audits, real penalties, and no master keys.


---


## The Dilemma


The national debate has split into two camps, and neither is satisfactory.


The first says: *no surveillance.* Government is not to be trusted, so it should be blind. But a government that refuses to watch men plotting mass murder has failed in its first duty. Scripture describes the civil ruler as* “the minister of God, a revenger to execute wrath upon him that doeth evil”*** (Romans 13:4)**. A ruler who will not look for the evildoer cannot execute that office.


The second says: *watch everything, and trust the watchers.* Collect it all, store it all, and rely on the good character of the officials who search it. But that asks something of human nature that human nature has never delivered. Power over another man’s secrets is power over the man himself, and history shows that such power is abused, not occasionally, but predictably.


Between these two lies a narrow road: watch the guilty with vigor, require cause before watching anyone else, and build the system on the assumption that some of its operators will be tempted.


---


## What the Fourth Amendment Says


The text is plain:

>
“The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated, and no Warrants shall issue, but upon probable cause, supported by Oath or affirmation, and particularly describing the place to be searched, and the persons or things to be seized.”


The language is absolute: the right *shall not be violated.* The grey has entered through two doors. The first is the word “unreasonable,” which courts have stretched to fit the moment’s needs. The second is the “third-party doctrine” (*Smith v. Maryland*, 1979), which held that information you hand to a phone company or a bank carries no reasonable expectation of privacy. In 1979, that meant a list of dialed numbers. Today, it would mean nearly your whole life, because nearly everything passes through third parties. The Supreme Court began to recognize this in *Carpenter v. United States* (2018), requiring a warrant for historical cell-phone location records, but the doctrine still largely stands.


The principle behind the amendment has not changed. Our “papers” today are emails, texts, cloud backups, and search histories. If the Fourth Amendment does not protect them, it no longer protects papers at all.


---


## Why FISA Exists


The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act of 1978 was not created to expand surveillance. It was born from the discovery of abuse. The Church Committee investigations of the mid-1970s revealed decades of warrantless wiretapping and covert programs aimed at Americans: civil-rights leaders including Martin Luther King Jr., antiwar activists, journalists, and political opponents of those in power. FISA was Congress’s attempt to balance security and liberty: a special court would approve surveillance of foreign powers and their agents, with judicial review, so the executive branch could no longer decide on its own whom to watch.


That balance was sound in principle. The trouble came later.


In 2008, Congress added **Section 702**, which allows the government to collect communications from foreigners reasonably believed to be abroad without individual court orders. The court approves the *program* each year, not the *targets*. When a foreign target talks to an American, the program collects and stores the American’s side of the conversation too. The FBI then developed the practice of searching that stored database using Americans’ names, emails, and phone numbers, without a warrant. Critics call this the “backdoor search,” and the name fits: the government obtains what the Fourth Amendment’s front door would have required a warrant to obtain.


The abuse has been documented, not merely feared. Declassified rulings of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court disclosed that FBI personnel ran more than 278,000 improper searches of these databases between 2020 and early 2022, including searches on protesters, political donors, and a sitting member of Congress. Earlier, the Justice Department’s Inspector General found serious errors and omissions in the FISA applications used to surveil a former presidential campaign adviser. These were not foreign terrorists. They were Americans, and in several cases, Americans engaged in politics.


---


## Where Things Stand Today


As of this writing, Section 702 has lapsed. After months of short-term extensions, the House rejected a further extension on June 11, 2026, and the statute expired on June 12, the first lapse since it was enacted. Seven Republican senators joined most Democrats in refusing to advance a renewal that lacked a warrant requirement. Collection continues under court certifications that run into March 2027, and intelligence officials are pressing Congress to act before Thanksgiving.


Meanwhile, a second front has opened: encryption. The British government ordered Apple to provide access to its end-to-end encrypted iCloud backups; Apple withdrew that protection from British users rather than build a back door, and its legal challenge continues before Britain’s Investigatory Powers Tribunal. Canada’s Bill C-22, the “Lawful Access Act,” passed the House of Commons in June and is now before the Canadian Senate. The Canadian government says it does not require back doors; Apple, Signal, Mozilla, and the chairmen of the U.S. House Judiciary and Foreign Affairs Committees warn it could compel exactly that, with effects reaching American users.


Former House Judiciary Chairman Bob Goodlatte put the encryption problem well: “Calling access lawful does not make the underlying technology safe.” A key built for the police can be stolen by a criminal, copied by a hostile foreign service, or misused by a corrupt official. Mathematics does not check credentials.


So the question is live, and it is before Congress now.


---


## The Moral Test


I judge every policy by three questions, in order. Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? And would I want it done to me, judged not by momentary appetite but by the self committed to righteousness?


**Watching a foreign terrorist cell abroad.** *Righteous:* yes; the ruler is charged with restraining the evildoer. *Loving:* yes; it protects neighbors who would otherwise be murdered. *Would I want it done to me?* If I were plotting murder, the better part of me, the part that answers to God, would want to be stopped. This passes.


**Searching an American’s private messages without cause, because they happen to sit in a database.** *Righteous:* no. It takes what hasn’t been lawfully given and treats a citizen as a suspect without evidence. *Loving:* no. It exposes a neighbor’s private life, his medical worries, his family quarrels, his confessions, to strangers with power over him. *Would I want it done to me?* No man would. This fails at the first question.


**Building a hidden key into everyone’s encryption.** *Righteous:* no. It punishes the innocent many in order to reach the guilty few, and it creates a tool that will fall into wicked hands. *Loving:* no. The woman hiding from a violent stalker, the pastor in a hostile country, the whistleblower, the ordinary family: all are made less safe. *Would I want it done to me?* No. This fails.


The test does not condemn surveillance. It condemns surveillance without cause.


---


## The Heart of the Matter: Omniscience Belongs to God


Scripture describes God as the One before whom *“all things are naked and opened”* **(Hebrews 4:13)**, who knows our thoughts afar off and our words before they are on our tongue **(Psalm 139)**. The believer finds this comforting rather than terrifying for one reason: the One who sees everything also loves perfectly and judges justly. Perfect knowledge is safe only in the hands of perfect love.


No man has that. When one man knows nearly everything about another, he has the power to manipulate, shame, blackmail, or destroy him. That is why total surveillance is not merely risky but presumptuous. It reaches for an attribute that belongs to God alone.


Consider David. He was a man after God’s own heart, Israel’s greatest king. He walked on his rooftop in the evening and *saw* what was not his to see. Sight joined to power led him to adultery, and adultery led him to murder **(2 Samuel 11)**. If David could fall that way, so can a career analyst with a search bar and a grudge, or a political appointee in an election year.


Christ taught us to pray, *“Lead us not into temptation.”* A righteous law follows that prayer: it doesn’t set temptation before officials and then hope they resist. James Madison made the same point in *Federalist No. 51*: “If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on government would be necessary.” We have neither angels to govern nor angels to be governed. So we need controls.


You might ask: if men will capitulate to temptation even when the penalty is severe, what good do penalties do? The answer is that severity alone is a weak deterrent; *certainty* is a strong one. A man who knows every search he runs is logged, reviewed by someone outside his chain of command, and traceable to his name will rarely abuse the system, even with a moderate penalty. A man who believes no one will ever check will often abuse it, even if the penalty is prison. The design goal is to make abuse difficult to do and nearly certain to be found.


The high-trust society remains the goal. But trust is built on faithfulness verified over time: *“He that is faithful in that which is least is faithful also in much”* **(Luke 16:10)**. Agencies that operate under warrants and audits, and are seen to follow the rules, will earn trust. Agencies that demand trust without verification will keep losing it.


---


## What I Propose


- **Require a warrant to search Americans’ data.** Before any agency searches Section 702 data (or any foreign-intelligence database) using an American’s name, number, or account, it must obtain a judge-issued warrant based on probable cause. Surveillance of foreigners abroad continues unchanged.

- **A true emergency exception.** When lives are at immediate risk, agents may search first and must apply to the court within a short, fixed period. If the court finds the search unjustified, it destroys the results and reviews the agent’s conduct.

- **Close the data-broker loophole.** Government may not buy from commercial brokers what it would need a warrant to compel. Location histories, browsing records, and similar data purchased on the open market are still Americans’ papers.

- **No back doors.** Congress should prohibit any federal agency from compelling a company to weaken encryption or build hidden access into its products, and should direct our diplomats to resist foreign mandates (British, Canadian, European, or other) that would weaken the security of systems Americans use.

- **Log everything, audit independently.** Every query of a surveillance database should be logged with the name of the person who ran it and the reason given. An inspector general independent of the agency should audit those logs and should publish aggregate results to Congress and the public.

- **Real penalties for abuse.** Knowingly misusing surveillance data, especially for political advantage, personal gain, or blackmail, should be a felony, with mandatory dismissal from government service. Americans who were unlawfully surveilled should be notified and given a right to sue.

- **Heightened protection for political life.** Surveillance targeting candidates, campaigns, elected officials, journalists, or houses of worship should require approval at the highest level of the Justice Department and notify the relevant congressional leaders of both parties.

- **A real adversary in the secret court.** The FISA court hears only the government’s side. Independent advocates, with security clearances and access to the full record, should be able to argue for privacy in significant cases.

- **Keep the sunsets.** Short reauthorization periods force Congress to examine the program regularly. That accountability is worth the inconvenience.

- **Limits on artificial-intelligence analysis.** Machine analysis can now search and cross-reference oceans of stored data in seconds, finding patterns no human team could find. The warrant requirement must cover automated searches of Americans’ data just as it covers manual ones. The more powerful the tool, the more important the restraint.


---


## Answering the Objections


**“A warrant requirement will cost lives.”** This is the strongest objection, and I take it seriously. In fast-moving terror cases, minutes can matter. That is why I propose a genuine emergency exception. And where the government has real evidence that an American is working with a terrorist group, probable cause is not hard to show; judges grant such warrants routinely. What the warrant requirement stops is not the investigation of the guilty but the browsing of the innocent. I will not pretend the cost is zero. There may be rare cases where the delay of seeking a warrant matters. But a free people accepts some risk as the price of liberty, just as we accept that police must get warrants to enter homes even though some criminals escape as a result.


**“If you have nothing to hide, you have nothing to fear.”** Every honest person has things that are private without being wrong: a medical fear, a marital struggle, a confession to a pastor, a business plan, a political opinion held quietly. The danger lies not in what the innocent man has done but in what a dishonest official might do with what he learns. The question is not whether you have something to hide. It is whether *they* have something to gain.


**“Abolish it all; government cannot be trusted with any of it.”** A government that refuses to watch its real enemies abandons its duty to protect its people. The answer to abuse is restraint and accountability, not blindness.


**“The back door will only be used against criminals.”** A back door does not know who is walking through it. Once built, it is available to every criminal hacker and hostile government that can steal it, and to every official tempted to misuse it.


---


## Why the Concern Is Real


Some will say these worries are overblown, that the abuses were exceptions, and that the agencies have reformed. I do not doubt that most of the people who work in our intelligence services are patriots doing hard work honorably. But the case for strong protections does not rest on the character of the average officer. It rests on four facts.


First, the record. From the abuses the Church Committee uncovered, through the improper database searches disclosed in recent years, the pattern has repeated in every generation. Reforms were promised each time.


Second, the incentives. Information about a political opponent, a journalist, or a critic is enormously valuable to anyone in power. Wherever something valuable sits unguarded, someone eventually takes it.


Third, the scale. Each year more of life moves into digital form, and artificial intelligence makes stored data searchable in ways no one imagined when these laws were written. The haystack has become a filing cabinet with an index.


Fourth, the drift. Powers granted for an emergency rarely shrink when the emergency passes. What was adopted to fight foreign terrorists becomes, over time, a general-purpose tool. Each expansion seems small; the total is not.


These are not reasons to despair. They are reasons to build the system correctly now, while Congress is still deciding.


---


## The Citizen’s Part


Laws alone will not secure liberty. Two duties fall to ordinary citizens.


The first is on the jury. The jury is the people’s final check on the government. A juror who learns that the government’s case rests on evidence gathered by violating the very rights the Constitution guarantees is entitled to weigh that in his verdict, and to refuse to convict where conscience forbids it. A government that knows juries will not reward lawless searches has one more reason to keep within the law.


The second is in our own lives. The high-trust society I described at the beginning does not come from statutes. It comes from righteous people who tell the truth, keep their word, and refuse to exploit what they know about one another. The more of us who live that way, the less any of us will need protection from the watchers, and the more trustworthy the watchers themselves will be, since they are drawn from among us.


---


## Closing


Watch the wicked. Protect the innocent. Require a warrant before reading an American’s private words. Refuse to build master keys. And design every part of the system on the sober assumption that some of those who run it will be tempted.


This is my position: if someone shows me a better way to protect both the nation and the privacy of its citizens, I will gladly consider it. The burden lies on those who would take away citizens’ security in their papers, not on citizens who want to keep it.
