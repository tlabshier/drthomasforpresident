# 261115 - Loving Your Unlovely Neighbor - The Poor, Criminal, Mentally ill, Addict

# Loving the Neighbor in the Hard Cases


### Charity, Homelessness, and Prison in a Nation Without Forced Charity

Thomas Lee Abshier, ND · Nov 15, 2026

## My Position

In *Not Yours to Give* I argued that government may not take from one man to give to another, and that care for the poor belongs to love freely given. That leaves the harder question, the one under all the others: **how should we love our neighbor when loving him is hardest?** It is easy to love the neighbor who is grateful, sober, and able to work. The test comes with the homeless man, the addict, the mentally ill, the vagrant, and the convict.

The Bastiat way joined to the Bible way is no small task. It would be hard even if we were starting fresh, and we are not. We are starting with a large class of people who have been taught dependence, many of whom are addicted, ill, or disabled. And the man who tries to help them himself, by bringing the homeless into his home or employing the unemployable, can bring chaos into his own life.

I stand on this: **the answer is neither the government check nor the lone volunteer, but a division of labor in love.** The individual gives and stays personally near. The church teaches, disciples, and appoints deacons for the work of care. Specialized ministries do the hardest work with training and structure. The government keeps its own sword for its own work: protecting the public, and restraining the man who is a danger to himself or others. And for the prisoner, the goal is not merely punishment but the conviction of conscience, the gospel, and restitution to the one he wronged.

I test each part with three questions: *Is it righteous?* If so, *is it loving?* And *would I want it done to me?*

## The Tithe: To the Church, or Administered Ourselves?

If government stops forcing charity, the tithe must carry the load. So who should hold it, the church or the giver?

Scripture's own pattern is divided. Israel gave more than one tithe, and each had its own purpose and its own administrator:


 	- **The tithe for the ministry.** *"And, behold, I have given the children of Levi all the tenth in Israel for an inheritance, for their service which they serve, even the service of the tabernacle of the congregation."* **(Numbers 18:21)** The Levites received it because they had no land of their own. They in turn gave a tenth of what they received to the priests **(Numbers 18:26)**.

 	- **The tithe the giver enjoyed himself.** At the feasts, the family ate its tithe before the Lord: *"thou shalt eat there before the LORD thy God, and thou shalt rejoice, thou, and thine household"* **(Deuteronomy 14:26)**.

 	- **The tithe for the poor, held locally.** *"At the end of three years thou shalt bring forth all the tithe of thine increase the same year, and shalt lay it up within thy gates: And the Levite... and the stranger, and the fatherless, and the widow, which are within thy gates, shall come, and shall eat and be satisfied."* **(Deuteronomy 14:28-29)** This was not sent to a temple treasury. It was stored *within thy gates*, in the giver's own town.


So how much did the Levites actually need to administer the Lord's worship? Enough to live, since they had no inheritance, and enough to keep the tabernacle. But the tithe for the poor was distinct, local, and close to the giver. My concern about the modern church is that its giving has drifted toward maintaining buildings rather than serving as a collection point for the poor and a school for discipling them in the ways of the Lord. Church buildings are not wrong. But a congregation whose giving mostly maintains its own walls has confused the tabernacle with the purpose of the tabernacle.

My recommendation follows the pattern:


 	- **Give to your church for its ministry**: the support of those who teach, and the upkeep of what the congregation genuinely needs.

 	- **Give to your church's deacon fund for the poor of your own community**, and ask to see how it is used.

 	- **Give directly to ministries that specialize in the hard cases**: rescue missions, recovery homes, the Salvation Army, food banks, crisis pregnancy centers, and prison ministries.

 	- **Keep a portion for the neighbor God puts in front of you**, the person you meet whose need you can see and judge yourself.


That is my own reading of the pattern, not a command, and each family must decide its own proportions before God.

## The Deacon Model: Keeping the Church from Becoming a Soup Kitchen

If the church takes up the care of widows and orphans, does it become a soup kitchen and stop being a church? The apostles faced exactly this problem. When the daily distribution to widows overwhelmed them, they did not abandon the widows, and they did not abandon the Word. They created an office:

*"Then the twelve called the multitude of the disciples unto them, and said, It is not reason that we should leave the word of God, and serve tables. Wherefore, brethren, look ye out among you seven men of honest report, full of the Holy Ghost and wisdom, whom we may appoint over this business."* **(Acts 6:2-3)**

That is the answer. The church does not become a soup kitchen. It appoints deacons, men of proven character and wisdom, to run the work of care, while the elders keep to teaching and prayer. The deacons can run the church's own care and can also partner with the specialized ministries that already do this work well.

Paul's instructions on widows show the deacons' work was not indiscriminate. Families were to care for their own first: *"If any man or woman that believeth have widows, let them relieve them, and let not the church be charged; that it may relieve them that are widows indeed."* **(1 Timothy 5:16)** The church's own roll of widows had criteria: age, character, and a record of good works **(1 Timothy 5:9-10)**. Care was real, but it was ordered. The family came first, then the church, and the church's help went to those who truly had no one else.

That order is the foundation for everything that follows: **family first, then the church through its deacons, then specialized ministries, and only as a last resort, and for a limited time, the state.**

## The Samaritan Model: Near Enough to See, Wise Enough Not to Be Consumed

There are two opposite dangers in caring for the hard cases. The first is getting too close. A man who brings the addict or the violently ill into his own home, or who tries alone to mentor and employ the chronically homeless, can see his own family, finances, and peace destroyed. Scripture warns of this: *"Make no friendship with an angry man; and with a furious man thou shalt not go: Lest thou learn his ways, and get a snare to thy soul."* **(Proverbs 22:24-25)** Even in restoring a brother, Paul adds a caution: *"considering thyself, lest thou also be tempted."* **(Galatians 6:1)**

The second danger is getting too far away. If we hand the poor entirely to institutions and never see them, they become "other" to us: a category rather than a neighbor. Then we lose the very thing charity was meant to give us, which is love for a real person. Jesus did not let His disciples keep that distance: *"Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me."* **(Matthew 25:40)**

The Good Samaritan shows the way between them:

*"And went to him, and bound up his wounds, pouring in oil and wine, and set him on his own beast, and brought him to an inn, and took care of him. And on the morrow when he departed, he took out two pence, and gave them to the host, and said unto him, Take care of him; and whatsoever thou spendest more, when I come again, I will repay thee."* **(Luke 10:34-35)**

Notice what he did. He met the man in person and gave immediate help with his own hands. He took him to a place equipped to care for him. He paid for that care freely, out of his own pocket. He did not move the man into his own house or abandon his own journey. And he promised to come back. **Personal encounter, then capable care freely paid for, then follow-up.** That is the model for the individual Christian. He stays near enough to see his neighbor's face and know his name. He trusts the long, hard work to those equipped for it, and he keeps coming back.

## Will Not Work, Cannot Work, Cannot Find Work

Paul's rule is famous: *"if any would not work, neither should he eat."* **(2 Thessalonians 3:10)** But look at the word: *would* not. The rule concerns the man who is unwilling, not the man who is unable. It assumes he can work and that someone would hire him. That assumption fails for many of the people we are talking about. So we must separate three groups and love each one differently.

**Those who will not work.** For the able man who refuses, the loving thing is the consequence Paul names, with the door to work always open. Charity that feeds a man who refuses work feeds his refusal. The goal is the one Paul gave the thief: *"let him labour, working with his hands the thing which is good, that he may have to give to him that needeth."* **(Ephesians 4:28)** The aim is not only that he stops taking, but that he becomes a giver.

**Those who cannot work.** The disabled, the elderly without family, and the severely mentally ill are not lazy, and they are not to be judged by Paul's rule. They are the widows and orphans of our time, and they are owed care. Their care follows the order above: family, church, ministry, and only as a last resort the state.

**Those who cannot find work.** Some are willing but have no record, no skills, no address, or a criminal history that closes every door. For them, the answer is work built for them: church-run and ministry-run businesses that hire people others will not, training programs tied to real jobs, and Christian employers willing to take a supervised risk. Gleaning in Israel was exactly this: the landowner left the margin, and the poor came and worked to gather it.

### The size of the hard core

The hard cases are real, but they are not the whole picture. In January 2025, about 745,652 Americans were homeless on a single night. About a quarter of homeless adults met the federal definition of serious mental illness, and nearly one in five had a substance use disorder. The truly hard core is serious, but it is a minority. Many of the homeless are families and working people who fell through, and they can be restored by ordinary neighborly help.

### Where the sword belongs

Some hard cases fall within the government's own work, under the protection test. A man who is a danger to himself or to others because of severe mental illness or addiction needs protection, and so does the public around him. Civil commitment with proper due process, the enforcement of public order, and the clearing of camps that endanger health are the state's rightful work, because they protect life. The state should do that work firmly and humanely, and then hand the man, as soon as he is safe, to the ministries that can do what the state cannot: heal his heart.

## Prison: Conviction, the Gospel, and Restitution

A prison exists first to protect the public from those who have done violence or theft, and that is the state's rightful work. But protection is not the whole of justice. I believe the deepest punishment a man can receive is the conviction of his own soul that he has violated another person, and that conviction is also the beginning of his healing. *"For godly sorrow worketh repentance to salvation not to be repented of."* **(2 Corinthians 7:10)** *"A broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise."* **(Psalm 51:17)**

**Restitution belongs at the center.** Biblical justice did not chiefly cage the thief. It required him to repay the one he wronged, and to repay more than he took: *"If a man shall steal an ox, or a sheep, and kill it, or sell it; he shall restore five oxen for an ox, and four sheep for a sheep."* **(Exodus 22:1)** When Zacchaeus met Jesus, his repentance took exactly this form: *"if I have taken any thing from any man by false accusation, I restore him fourfold."* **(Luke 19:8)** Restitution makes the offender face the person he injured and do something real to make it right. Prison work should be paid, and part of every wage should go to the victim.

**The gospel changes prisons.** The strongest evidence comes from Louisiana State Penitentiary at Angola, once called the bloodiest prison in America. In 1995 its warden invited New Orleans Baptist Theological Seminary to open a Bible college inside the walls, funded by private donations. Its graduates now serve as inmate ministers throughout the prison, and the prison reports that violence fell by roughly two-thirds over the following fifteen years. A study of a similar Bible college in Texas found that participants averaged about one fewer disciplinary conviction each. I should be honest about the limit of this evidence: the men admitted to these programs are chosen for good conduct, so part of the effect may come from who is selected. But the transformation of Angola's culture is hard to explain any other way. So far, only Louisiana and Mississippi allow inmates to form and lead their own congregations. Other states should follow.

**Many men in prison turn to Islam.** In a national survey of state prison chaplains, about half of those who reported religious switching said the number of Muslims was growing in their prisons. Nearly as many said the same of Protestant Christians. Prison is a place where men are searching. Islam offers a demanding discipline of submission and order, which many men in prison have never known. I believe the gospel offers what that discipline cannot: a Savior who forgives the guilty, and a love that reaches even enemies. *"Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you."* **(Matthew 5:44)** Every prisoner's freedom of conscience must be protected. The answer is not to restrict any faith but to out-teach and out-love: to bring the gospel in with more seriousness, more discipline, and more love than any rival.

**The church must go in.** *"I was in prison, and ye came unto me."* **(Matthew 25:36)** *"Remember them that are in bonds, as bound with them."* **(Hebrews 13:3)** Prison ministry, mentoring after release, and churches willing to receive former prisoners are not optional work for a church that takes Christ at His word. And the man coming out must have somewhere to go: a church family, a job built for him, and a sober place to live. Otherwise the cycle starts again.

## Objections, and Why the Concern Remains Urgent

**"The church cannot handle this."** Not as it is now. A church whose giving mostly maintains its buildings, and which has handed the poor to the state for three generations, has lost the habit. But the church did this work for most of its history, and the apostles showed how to organize it without abandoning the Word. Building that capacity again is the work of a generation, which is why the transition in *Not Yours to Give* is a generation long.

**"Specialized charities will become bureaucracies too."** Some will. The difference is that a charity lives on free gifts, so it must keep earning the trust of its givers. A government program lives on taxes and never has to. The giver's freedom to move his gift is the best guard against waste.

**"Restitution lets the rich buy their way out."** Restitution does not replace protection. The violent man must still be restrained. Restitution adds what prison alone cannot give: something real for the victim, and something real for the offender's conscience.

**"Faith programs violate the separation of church and state."** Participation must be voluntary, every faith's freedom must be protected, and the programs at Angola are privately funded. Religious freedom does not require the state to keep the gospel out of prisons. It requires the state not to compel it.

The concern is urgent because the present arrangement has failed the very people it was meant to serve. Homelessness reached a record high in 2024, and chronic homelessness has risen even as government spending on beds has grown. Many Americans sense something is unnatural about cities that spend more each year and see more people sleeping in the streets. That sense is right. Money cannot do what only love can, and a system that treats the poor as clients rather than neighbors will keep producing clients.
