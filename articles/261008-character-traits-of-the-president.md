# What a President Must Be


## Traits, Abilities, and Duties for a New ERA


In the summer of 2024, Tulsi Gabbard was asked in a [Modern Wisdom Interview](https://www.msn.com/en-us/news/other/a-new-way-to-learn-about-religion-with-experts-worldwide/vi-AA1ZmRss?ctsrc=dgst&ocid=edgntpruby&pc=EDGEDB&cvid=6ac871273f0f49a5aa71f5bc0001ffb1&uxmode=ruby&ei=12) what she thought of Kamala Harris as a candidate for president. I am not writing to re-argue that election. What caught my attention was that, in explaining why she thought one candidate unfit, she laid out a fairly complete description of what fitness for the office requires. Her points, stated in the negative, were these:


- A president must not be **easily manipulated and controlled by unelected powers**.

- A president must not feel the **need to prove strength**, because a leader who must show he is strong will spend the lives of soldiers to do it — and in an age of nuclear-armed rivals, one spark can be enough.

- A president must put **the interests of the people he serves ahead of his own political interests**, in every office he holds.

- A president must **believe in the Constitution and in fundamental freedom**.

- A president needs **real understanding**, not merely skill at calculating his own advancement.

- A president must have **the courage to say what is plainly true** when his party and his peers stay silent. Leaders who go along with what they privately know is wrong are not leaders; they are followers.


I agree with every one of these. But a list of what a president must not be is only half an answer. A man can avoid all six faults and still have nowhere to lead. The traits matter because of what they serve. So in this paper I want to do three things: state the moral standard that gives the traits their purpose, describe the character and abilities the office requires, and walk through the constitutional duties of the presidency — showing how I would use each of them to bring the country to adopt the policies I propose under the frame I first used in 2003: *A New ERA — Energy, Righteousness, and Accountability.*


I hold these positions firmly, and I hold them open to a better argument.


---


## 1. The Standard Behind the Traits


Every policy I propose rests on one test: **would my neighbor feel loved by this act in the public square?** Not flattered, not managed, not bought — loved. Would it help him provide for his family, keep his freedom, and live as a man responsible before God rather than as a ward of the state?


That test is also the test of a president. Each of Gabbard’s warnings describes a way a leader stops loving the people he governs:


- The man controlled by unelected powers serves *them* instead of his neighbors.

- The man who must prove his strength spends other men’s sons to buy his own reputation.

- The man who puts his political interest first treats the people as means to his career.

- The man who disregards the Constitution treats the people’s freedoms as obstacles.

- The man who stays silent about what is true lets his neighbors be harmed so that he will not be criticized.


Jesus described the difference between rulers who lord it over people and leaders who serve them: “whosoever will be great among you, let him be your minister” (Matthew 20:26). The presidency is the highest office in the land, which makes it the highest place of service, not the highest place of privilege. Everything below follows from that.


---


## 2. Character: What the Man Must Be


### He must fear God more than he fears men


Gabbard’s sharpest point was that every leading figure in one party knew a particular policy was wrong and none would say so. That is not a failure of intelligence. It is a failure of fear — they feared losing their position more than they feared doing wrong. The only reliable cure I know is a man who fears God more than he fears the press, his donors, or his party. “The fear of man bringeth a snare: but whoso putteth his trust in the LORD shall be safe” (Proverbs 29:25).


This is not a religious test for office; the Constitution forbids one, and rightly. It is a description of what makes a man hard to bend. A man who believes he will answer for every decision to a Judge who cannot be lobbied has a reason to do right when no one is watching and when everyone is watching.


### He must not be for sale, and must not be capturable


Gabbard warned of “unelected powers” that control weak leaders. Those powers work through money, through flattery, through the career ladder, and through information — controlling what the president is told. A candidate who owes his campaign to large donors begins his term with debts.


I take no donations. That is not a gimmick; it is the simplest structural protection I can give against capture. But money is only one channel. A president must also choose advisers who will tell him he is wrong, insist on seeing the evidence himself on the questions that matter most, and be willing to remove officials — elected or appointed — who serve an agenda the people did not choose.


### He must be strong enough not to need to look strong


There is a kind of weakness that disguises itself as toughness: the leader who fears being seen as soft and so reaches for force to prove otherwise. Gabbard, who served in uniform, named the cost — it is paid by the men and women who are sent. A secure leader can afford restraint. He can wait, negotiate, and refuse to be provoked, because his sense of himself does not depend on the next headline. That kind of steadiness is what deters adversaries and also what keeps a nation out of wars it does not need to fight.


### He must be honest in proportion


Courage to tell the truth is not the same as volume. A leader who exaggerates every problem into a catastrophe trains the people to stop listening, and when a real catastrophe comes, his warning sounds like the others. I call exaggeration *lying by proportion*: the facts may be true, but the weight given them is false. A president should say strong things when the danger warrants it, say ordinary things in an ordinary voice, and say “I don’t know” when he does not.


### He must hold himself accountable first


The *A* in ERA applies to the man in the Oval Office before it applies to anyone else. A president who demands accountability from contractors, agencies, and officials but exempts himself and his friends is teaching the nation that accountability is a weapon, not a principle. He must publish his reasoning, admit his mistakes, and refuse to use the pardon power to protect himself or his own.

## 3. Abilities: What the Man Must Be Able to Do


Character is necessary but not sufficient. A good man without the right abilities can do great harm with good intentions.


### Understanding, not mere calculation


Gabbard distinguished intelligence from being “calculating” — skilled at the maneuvers that advance a career. The ability a president needs is understanding: the capacity to see how things actually work. A president today will face decisions about nuclear energy, artificial intelligence, the money supply, biology, and war. He does not need to be an expert in each, but he must understand enough to ask the right questions, recognize when experts disagree, and notice when he is being given a conclusion instead of evidence. My own life has been spent across medicine, physics, and ministry; I do not claim mastery of everything, but I know what it takes to work through a hard technical question to its end, and I know the difference between a result and a guess.


### Judgment of men


A president governs mostly through the people he appoints. Thousands of positions are filled at his direction. The ability to recognize character and competence — and to recognize their absence — determines whether his policies are carried out or quietly undermined. Good intentions in the Oval Office mean little if the agencies are run by men who do not share them.


### Persuasion by reason


A president in our system cannot command the country to adopt anything. Congress writes the laws and controls the money. The states hold most of the power over daily life. Courts check all of them. And the people choose everyone. A president leads chiefly by persuasion. That means he must be able to explain — patiently, in plain language, with evidence — why a policy is right and what it will cost. He must persuade by reason first and by appeal to the heart second, and never by manipulation.


### Administration


The executive branch is the largest organization on earth. A president must be able to set clear goals, measure whether they are met, and change course when they are not. A plan that cannot be administered is a wish.

## 4. Duties: What the Constitution Requires, and How I Would Use Each


The duties of the president are not a matter of opinion; they are written in Article II. Here is how I understand each, and how each would serve a New ERA.


### The oath: preserve, protect, and defend the Constitution


The president swears to “preserve, protect and defend the Constitution of the United States.” Note what the oath is to: not to the party, not to the people’s momentary wishes, not to his own program, but to the Constitution. Every policy I propose must fit inside that oath. Where a policy would require power the Constitution does not give the federal government, the honest course is to persuade the states or to propose an amendment — not to stretch the law until it means what I want.


The moral reason is simple. The Constitution is the agreement by which a free people consented to be governed. A president who ignores it when convenient is telling his neighbors that their consent does not matter. That is not love; it is rule.


### “Take care that the laws be faithfully executed”


This is the president’s daily work, and it is where much of my **Energy** program begins. The federal government already has a new licensing framework for advanced reactors. A president cannot rewrite safety science, but he can direct the agencies under him to tell builders exactly what evidence will be sufficient, set deadlines for decisions, and staff the review offices to meet them. Faithful execution of a law includes executing it promptly. A license that is never decided is a law that is not being executed.


The same duty serves **Accountability**. Faithful execution means one standard of justice — the rules that would charge a sergeant must apply to every cabinet officer and every president, of every party. It means that when a senior official is not charged, the reasoning is published so the people can judge. And it means the president does not demand the prosecution of his opponents by name; prosecutions must rest on evidence before grand juries, not on the wishes of the man in power.


### Commander in chief


The president commands the armed forces, but the Constitution gives Congress alone the power to declare war (Article I, Section 8). I take that division seriously. A president who respects it will not send Americans into sustained combat without the consent of the people’s representatives. This is Gabbard’s point about strength in constitutional form: the framers deliberately made it hard for one man to take the nation to war.


The commander in chief’s duty runs in two directions — to defend the country and to protect those who defend it from being spent carelessly. In a world with several nuclear-armed rivals, the second duty may be the more important one. Abundant domestic energy also serves this duty: a nation that does not depend on hostile regimes for its fuel has fewer reasons to fight over it.


### Recommending measures to Congress


The president is to give Congress information on the state of the Union and “recommend to their Consideration such Measures as he shall judge necessary and expedient.” This is the constitutional home of the platform. I would bring Congress concrete legislation: financing for energy and water infrastructure structured as capital investment rather than consumption, with payment only against delivered results; public-trust conditions on every company that receives that financing — open books, deferred executive reward, and clawback of money paid against fraud; a constitutional amendment forbidding a president to pardon himself, his family, or his own officials; and election rules the voter can verify with his own eyes.


A recommendation is not an order. It is the beginning of an argument. My duty is to make that argument well enough that the people’s representatives, and the people who elect them, are persuaded.


### The veto


The veto is a duty of judgment, not a tool of spite. I would use it against spending that buys consumption with borrowed money, against laws that exceed the Constitution’s grant of power, and against laws that would harm the neighbor the law is supposed to protect — and I would explain each veto in writing.


### Appointments


The president nominates judges, ambassadors, and officers of the United States. These appointments outlast any term. I would look for men and women who understand that their authority is a trust, who will follow the law as written, and who will tell the president when he is wrong. In the agencies central to the ERA program — energy, justice, the treasury — I would look especially for people who have built things and been held accountable for results.


### The pardon


The pardon exists for mercy — to correct injustice that the ordinary process could not. It does not exist for self-protection or for rewarding friends. I would use it for that purpose only, publish the reason for every pardon, and support the amendment described above to make the limit binding on my successors.


### The duty the Constitution does not list: telling the truth


No clause of Article II says the president must tell the people the truth. But every other duty depends on it. The people cannot consent to what they do not understand, and they cannot judge what is hidden from them. This is where **Righteousness** enters the presidency most directly. A president has the largest platform in the country. He should use it to name what is right and what is wrong in public life, to give his reasons, and to invite argument — not to impose his views, but to lead the conversation a free people must have about how to live together. Gabbard’s complaint was that leaders were silent about something they knew to be true. The cure is a president who will speak, with reasons, and accept the consequences.

## 5. How I Would Lead the Country to Adopt a New ERA


Putting these together, here is how I understand the task.


**First, by persuasion, not decree.** A president who governs by executive order alone builds on sand; the next president erases it. Lasting change comes when Congress passes it, the states adopt it, and the people support it. My job is to make the case so clearly that they do.


**Second, by starting where the president has authority.** Energy licensing, the faithful and equal enforcement of the law, the conduct of the executive branch, and the use of the pardon are within the president’s own hands. I would begin there, so the people can see results before being asked for more.


**Third, by building on energy.** Abundant energy is the foundation of the rest of the platform. With it, a free people can produce water, food, housing, and the computing power of the coming age. Without it, every other promise is a promise to divide a shrinking pie.


**Fourth, by keeping accountability in front of everything.** Any program that moves large sums of money will attract those who want to steal it. Every dollar must be public, every payment tied to delivered results, and every official — beginning with the president — answerable for what he does.


**Fifth, by relying on righteousness as the motive.** No system of audits can catch every thief, and no law can make men good. A nation whose leaders fear God and love their neighbors needs fewer auditors and fewer prisons. That is why this campaign is as much about character as about policy. “When the righteous are in authority, the people rejoice” (Proverbs 29:2).


**Sixth, by trusting the people as the final check.** The people elect, and the people sit on juries. I regard the jury’s power to acquit in conscience — to refuse to convict under an unjust law or an unjust prosecution — as a cornerstone of a free republic, and I believe jurors should be told they hold it. A president who believes in self-government should welcome the people’s check on his own administration.


---


## Conclusion


Tulsi Gabbard described what a president must not be: controllable, insecure in his strength, self-serving, careless of the Constitution, calculating rather than wise, and silent when the truth is costly. I would state the same thing positively. A president must fear God more than men, refuse to be bought or captured, be strong enough not to need to look strong, speak truthfully and in proportion, understand how the world actually works, judge men well, persuade by reason, and hold himself accountable first. And he must do all of this in service to the duties the Constitution gives him — faithfully executing the laws, defending the nation without spending its people carelessly, recommending good measures to Congress, and telling the truth.


Those are the standards I hold myself to. I offer them, and the New ERA program they serve, as positions I am prepared to defend — and to change, if someone shows me a better argument.
