# What a Congressional Majority Is For


### A position paper on the Democratic agenda for the 120th Congress


*Occasioned by Annie Karni, “Here’s What Democrats Plan to Do With Their Power if They Win Back Congress,” The New York Times, 23 October 2026*


Thomas Lee Abshier, ND — 24 October 2026


**Resource:** [The New York Times article](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/23/us/politics/democrats-congress-majority-agenda.html)


---


## Plain language summary


House Democrats expect to win in November and have told the *Times* what they would do first: restore expired health-insurance subsidies, reverse the Medicaid cuts in last year’s tax law, end the President’s tariffs, force a vote on the Iran war, investigate corruption, ban congressional stock trading, and then move to tax credits, a higher minimum wage, and limits on ICE.


I take each item on its merits, using the same three questions I apply to my own proposals: Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? Would I want it done to me?


Where I stand:


- **The Iran war — agree, strongly.** Congress must vote to authorize the war or end it. Eight months of war without a vote is a breach of the Constitution no matter which party holds the White House.

- **Tariff power back to Congress — agree on the process, not the purpose.** Congress should own trade policy. But I would use that power to write a lawful, permanent fair-trade tariff, not to abolish tariffs.

- **Stock-trading ban — agree, and go further.** No member, spouse, or dependent should own individual stocks while in office.

- **Oversight — agree, with just weights.** Investigate everything they named, and apply the same standard to their own side’s pardons and enrichment.

- **Health subsidies and Medicaid — disagree with simple reversal.** Keep the work requirement for able-bodied adults. Protect the disabled, children, and the frail elderly. Attack the price of care rather than subsidize it without end.

- **Minimum wage, leave mandates, refundable credits — disagree.** These place the cost of charity on employers and the tax code, and they push the poorest workers out of their first jobs.

- **The strategy itself — disagree.** A plan built on “what unites us and divides them” treats law as a campaign weapon. A majority is for governing, not for staging vetoes.


*Provenance: Claude drafted this paper in my voice at my request on 24 October 2026, responding to the Times article and building on my earlier papers on Trump’s record, the administrative state, moral capitalism, and the ERA framework. Where I take a position that is still open to a better argument, I say so.*


---


## Why answer the other party’s agenda


Because it deserves an answer, and because most of my readers will not get one.


The article is a useful document. The Democratic leaders it quotes were unusually specific. They named bills, sequences, and motives. A citizen who wants to vote on substance rather than on mood should be grateful for that, and should take the list seriously enough to judge it item by item rather than accepting or rejecting it as a party package.


I am not a Democrat, and on several of these items I disagree sharply. On three of them I agree, and on one of those — the war — I agree more strongly than many in my own camp will like. A man who only finds merit in his own side’s proposals has stopped thinking and started cheering. I published a paper last month applying five tests to a Republican scorecard. The same tests apply here.


The question underneath the whole article is one the reporter asked well: what is a majority *for*? The answer I will defend is that a majority is a trust, given by the people for a term, to make laws that are righteous, to restrain the executive when it exceeds its authority, and to account honestly for what it spends. Each item below is weighed against that.


---


## PART ONE: The constitutional items — where I agree


### 1. The Iran war: authorize it or end it


**The facts.** The United States and Israel struck Iran on 28 February 2026. The administration notified Congress on 2 March, which started the War Powers Resolution’s sixty-day clock. On 1 May the President told Congress he needed no authorization because a ceasefire was in effect, and the Speaker said we were not at war. The ceasefire did not hold; the President declared the truce over in July. As of this week, Iran’s Revolutionary Guard reports striking ships in the Gulf, the Navy is escorting tankers and blockading Iranian ports, a third carrier is on its way, and planners are reportedly preparing renewed strikes after the election.


The cost is not abstract. Twenty-one American service members have been killed and at least 865 wounded as of 10 October. The Congressional Budget Office put the Pentagon’s war costs at about $38 billion as of September. Regular gasoline averaged $4.27 a gallon this week against $2.98 the day before the war began.


Congress has never voted to authorize any of it.


**The law.** Article I, Section 8 gives Congress, not the President, the power to declare war, and the power to raise and fund armies. The President commands the forces Congress provides; he does not decide alone whether the nation goes to war. The Founders did this on purpose. Madison wrote that the executive is the branch most interested in war and most prone to it, and that the Constitution therefore vested the question in the legislature. A President may repel a sudden attack. He may not conduct an eight-month campaign of choice on his own authority.


**The moral justification.** A war is the gravest thing a government does. It sends sons to die and takes the lives of other men’s sons. It is righteous only if it is just — a just cause, a last resort, proportionate means, and a reasonable hope of achieving a lawful end. And a just war in a republic must be waged with the consent of the people, given through their representatives. A war conducted without that consent fails the first question before we ever reach the others. It asks the soldier to die for a decision his own representative never made.


Apply the third question. If I were the father of one of those twenty-one, I would want to know that my representative stood up in the House and voted, by name, that the cause was worth my son’s life. That is the least a nation owes its dead.


**My position.** I stand with the Democratic leaders on this item, and I would ask Republicans to stand with them too: Congress should hold a recorded vote, in both houses, early in the new session, on a specific authorization with stated objectives and a time limit. If the authorization fails, the war ends, and Congress should use its power over appropriations to see that it does. This is not a partisan position. I would demand the same of a President of my own party, and I would demand it of myself.


On how I would vote, I hold my judgment open to a better argument than I have heard, but here is where I lean. Protecting free navigation through an international strait against attacks on neutral shipping is a defensible object of limited force. Regime change from the air is not achievable, has never been authorized, and should not be pursued. My July paper on the war concluded that the campaign had inflicted heavy damage without reaching its larger objectives. Nothing since has changed that assessment. Calls this week from abroad for the United States to overthrow Iran’s government should be answered plainly: that decision belongs to the American Congress, and the case for it has not been made.


### 2. Tariffs: return the power to Congress — and then use it


**The facts.** On 20 February 2026 the Supreme Court held that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize the President to impose tariffs. The administration replaced the struck-down duties with a temporary surcharge under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, which by law lasts 150 days unless Congress extends it. The Court of International Trade ruled in May that the administration had exceeded even that authority. Congress did not extend it, and it lapsed on 24 July. That same day, new duties of 10 to 12.5 percent took effect under Section 301 on goods from sixty economies, covering nearly all American imports, on the ground that those nations had not banned imports made with forced labor.


**The law.** Article I, Section 8: “The Congress shall have Power To lay and collect Taxes, Duties, Imposts and Excises.” A tariff is a tax on Americans who buy foreign goods. The Constitution puts the power to tax in the branch closest to the people, for the same reason it puts the war power there.


**Where I agree.** The Democrats propose to restore congressional power over trade policy. On that principle they are right, and the courts have already said so twice this year. Trade policy made by executive proclamation is policy made without the consent of the taxed. It is also, as I argued in my scorecard paper, written on sand: what one President imposes by proclamation the next removes by proclamation, and no business can plan a factory around it.


**Where I part company.** The Democratic plan is to “end Mr. Trump’s tariffs.” I would not end them; I would make them law, at a level Congress can defend.


I have argued since 2004 that the United States imposes what I call *reverse tariffs* on its own producers: the costs of our labor, safety, environmental, and tax rules, which a factory in America must bear and a factory in a nation without those rules does not. When we then admit those foreign goods duty-free, we do not have free trade. We have a rule that punishes Americans for keeping the standards Americans chose. The righteous principle is a just weight (Proverbs 11:1): the same measure for the American producer and the foreign one. A fair-trade tariff equal to the cost difference our own rules impose, lifted when the exporting nation meets comparable standards, is a just weight. A ban on goods made by forced labor is a moral duty, not a trade tactic — no Christian should want to clothe his family with the work of slaves.


Is it loving? Tariffs raise prices, and the poor feel that first. That is a real cost, and I do not wave it away. But a nation that cannot make its own steel, medicine, or microchips has made its working men dependent on the goodwill of rivals, and that dependence costs the poor most of all when the ships stop — as the Strait of Hormuz has shown us this year with oil.


**My position.** Congress should pass a statute that (1) reclaims tariff-setting as a legislative act, (2) enacts a permanent ban on forced-labor imports, and (3) sets fair-trade duties by a published formula tied to the cost of our own standards, reviewed on a recorded vote every few years. That gives the Democrats what they say they want — congressional authority — and gives the country what it needs: a durable, honest trade law instead of a cycle of proclamations and lawsuits.


### 3. A stock-trading ban: yes, and a complete one


**The facts.** In July the House passed, 232 to 198, a Republican-led bill barring members, spouses, and dependent children from buying new shares of individual companies while in office. It lets them keep what they already own. Its fate in the Senate is uncertain. Most Democrats opposed it as too weak and want a complete ban. The *Times* reports both parties’ Senate leaders see room to pass “a serious ban.”


**The moral justification.** “Thou shalt take no gift: for the gift blindeth the wise, and perverteth the words of the righteous” (Exodus 23:8). A legislator who writes the rules for an industry while holding its stock has a gift in his pocket every time he votes. Even if he is honest, the people cannot know it, and a republic runs on the people’s ability to trust that a vote was cast for them. I listed “no insider trading” among my congressional reforms three years ago. Would I want my own representative trading on what he learned in a closed briefing? No. Would I accept the same rule for myself if elected? Yes.


**My position.** I agree with the Democrats who say the House bill does not go far enough. Members, spouses, and dependent children should hold no individual stocks at all while in office: broad index funds, Treasury bonds, or a genuinely blind trust, and nothing else. Grandfathering existing holdings leaves the largest conflicts in place. Violations should forfeit the profits and carry a criminal penalty for concealment. This should pass with both parties’ votes in the first month of the new Congress, and if it does not, the voters should ask each member why.


### 4. Oversight: yes — with just weights


**The plan.** Representative Raskin, who would chair Judiciary, names three early targets: a “pardon industry” around the President, no-bid public contracts, and anonymous cryptocurrency flowing to the government and the President’s family. He expects subpoenas to be ignored and plans to rely on whistleblowers and private parties.


**Where I agree.** Oversight is not a partisan weapon. It is a constitutional duty. The power to fund the government carries the duty to know how the money is spent and whether the laws are being faithfully executed. A pardon sold for money is corruption of the most direct kind. A contract awarded without competition to a friend of the powerful is theft from the taxpayer. Money moving secretly toward the family of a sitting President is exactly what the Constitution’s emoluments clauses were written to prevent. Each of these deserves investigation, and if the facts are as alleged, they deserve exposure and law.


Righteousness here is plain: “Ye shall do no unrighteousness in judgment… in righteousness shalt thou judge thy neighbour” (Leviticus 19:15). The ruler is not exempt from the law he administers. If anything, he is held to a higher standard.


**The condition.** Just weights. An investigation that applies a standard only to the other party is not oversight; it is opposition research with subpoena power. The previous President issued sweeping pardons to members of his own family at the end of his term. If the Judiciary Committee examines pardons as a source of corruption, it should examine the practice, not just the practitioner, and it should propose a law that binds every future President of either party. The same goes for contracts and for crypto: the remedy is a statute requiring disclosure of any payment to an officeholder’s family, not a hearing that ends when the cameras leave.


**On impeachment.** The article reports pressure from Democratic voters to impeach, and Mr. Raskin’s reply that his party is “sober about the constitutional arithmetic.” I would put the matter differently. Impeachment is a judgment on conduct, not a count of votes. If the evidence shows high crimes — evidence that would persuade an honest juror who did not vote for either side — the House should impeach whether or not the Senate will convict, because the House is accountable for its own judgment. If the evidence does not show that, the House should refuse to impeach no matter how loudly its own voters demand it. Neither the arithmetic nor the base is the right test. The facts are.

## PART TWO: The spending items — where I disagree


### 5. Health subsidies and Medicaid: protect the helpless, keep the work requirement, attack the price


**The plan.** Restore the enhanced Affordable Care Act subsidies that expired at the end of 2025, and reverse the roughly $1 trillion in Medicaid reductions in last year’s domestic-policy law.


**What those programs are.** The enhanced subsidies were passed in 2021 as a temporary pandemic measure and extended once. They do not lower the price of care. They pay a larger share of an insurance premium from the Treasury, and the money goes to the insurer. The Medicaid changes in last year’s law did several different things at once: required able-bodied adults without young children to work, study, or volunteer about eighty hours a month to keep coverage; required more frequent eligibility checks; limited the tax arrangements some states use to draw extra federal money; and reduced federal funding in other ways. The Congressional Budget Office projected that the law would leave millions more people uninsured within a decade.


A blanket reversal treats all of these as one thing. They are not.


**What is righteous.** “If any would not work, neither should he eat” (2 Thessalonians 3:10). Paul did not write that to the sick, the crippled, the widow, or the child. He wrote it to able men who would not work. A work requirement for able-bodied adults is not cruelty; it is the oldest principle of Christian charity, which always distinguished between the man who cannot and the man who will not. Charity that asks nothing of the able does not love him. It teaches him to be less than he was made to be. I would keep the work requirement.


Verifying eligibility is also righteous. Public money is the neighbor’s money, taken by force of law; spending it on people who are not eligible is not compassion toward them, it is injustice toward the one who paid.


**What is loving — and where the law may fail the test.** Would I want it done to me? If I were disabled, or caring for a disabled child, or in a nursing home at the end of my life, I would not want my care to depend on paperwork I could not complete or a state budget that just lost its federal match. Where last year’s law falls on the helpless rather than the able, Congress should repair it. That is a narrow, targeted bill, not a reversal of the whole law, and it would draw votes from both parties.


**The deeper problem neither party will name.** Subsidies treat the symptom. Americans cannot afford insurance because they cannot afford care, and they cannot afford care because prices are hidden, competition is restricted by licensure and certificate-of-need laws, and the insurer-hospital system is, as I wrote in 2009, closer to a government-protected cartel than to a market. Pouring more money into premiums raises the price everyone else pays. The loving thing is to make care cheaper: posted cash prices, freedom to buy across state lines, direct primary care, health savings that belong to the patient, and the revival of church and charity hospitals and clinics that once cared for the poor without a federal form.


**My position.** I would not restore the enhanced subsidies as a permanent entitlement. I would phase any temporary help down on a fixed schedule while price-transparency and competition reforms take effect, so no family is dropped off a cliff. I would keep the Medicaid work requirement and eligibility checks. I would repair the provisions that fall on the disabled, children, and the frail elderly. And I would ask the churches of this country a question no Congress can answer for them: if the government stepped back, would we step forward? Until the answer is yes, the state will keep the duty we gave away.


### 6. Minimum wage, leave mandates, and refundable credits: charity by the wrong hand


**The plan.** Raise the minimum wage; pass mandatory sick leave, family leave, and vacation leave — Representative Casar wants “to be passing so much,” one a week; expand refundable tax credits, meaning payments to people who owe no income tax; expand the low-income housing credit.


**Why I disagree.** Each of these takes a duty that belongs to the family, the church, and the community and assigns it either to the employer or to the tax code. Each is a reverse tariff in the sense I described above: a cost laid on the American who hires, and not on the foreign competitor who does not.


A minimum wage does not guarantee anyone a job at that wage. It forbids the job that is worth less. The young man with no skills and no record, whose first job would have been worth less than the new floor, does not get a raise. He gets no job, and he loses the years in which he would have learned to work. That is not love toward him, however good it sounds.


Mandated leave is a benefit paid by the employer, which in a small business means paid by the owner and by the workers who are not hired because the mandate made hiring more expensive. A large corporation can absorb it; a family business often cannot. Regulations of this kind are one of the moats large companies use to keep small ones out, as I argued in my paper on the administrative state.


A refundable credit is a welfare payment routed through the tax code, where it is harder to see, harder to audit, and easier to defraud. I would rather the country argue honestly about what we owe the poor than hide the answer in the tax form.


**What I propose instead.** Stop taxing a man’s labor, and fund limited government through rent on the scarce things God gave to all — land, minerals, the radio spectrum — as I set out in my administrative-state and moral-capitalism papers. That leaves the worker his whole wage, removes the government from the middle of every paycheck, and makes room for a common dividend as AI and robotics make goods abundant. And it returns to the family and the church the joy and the duty of caring for their own.


I hold this position firmly, and I hold it open to a better argument. If someone can show me a minimum-wage increase that does not cost the least-skilled their first job, I will reconsider.


### 7. ICE, Medicare for All, Medikids


The article lists these as the next round, without detail, so I will be brief.


**ICE accountability.** Every armed officer of the government should be accountable for how he uses force, and I have no objection to clear rules, body cameras, and real consequences for abuse. But I have argued that the border has been substantially secured while interior enforcement was halted, and that the one measure that would end illegal employment — mandatory E-Verify with prosecution of the employers who knowingly hire — has been missing from every party’s agenda for forty years. If Democrats want accountability, I would add it there: hold the employer accountable, not only the officer. Abolishing ICE, which some candidates have campaigned on, would abandon enforcement of laws Congress passed. If Congress wants different immigration laws, it should pass them.


**Medicare for All and “Medikids.”** Senator Kim asks who will look him in the eye and say he does not want to give health care to kids. I will, respectfully, look him in the eye and say this: I want every child cared for, and the first duty to care for a child belongs to his parents, then to his church and community, and to the state only when those fail. A federal program that takes that duty away by default does not love the child more. It teaches the family that the child is someone else’s responsibility. Make care cheap enough that a working family can afford it, and help the families who still cannot through charity and, as a last resort, through the states — not a national insurance monopoly.


### 8. Artificial intelligence


The article notes that Democrats will have to decide how to regulate AI. I have written five papers on that subject this year. My short answer: the danger in AI regulation is regulatory capture, in which the largest companies write the rules that keep smaller competitors out. Any AI law should be judged first by whether it protects citizens from surveillance, manipulation, and loss of human control, and second by whether it entrenches the incumbents. I would welcome a bipartisan bill that does the first without the second.

## PART THREE: The strategy — what a majority is for


### “Signs it, we win. Vetoes it, we win.”


The most revealing sentence in the article belongs to Rahm Emanuel, who advised the House leadership: the whole strategy, he said, is what unites us and divides them.


I understand the logic. It is how both parties have legislated for a generation. House Republicans voted more than sixty times to repeal or gut the Affordable Care Act when they knew it would not become law. Democrats now propose to pass bills they know the Senate filibuster will block, in order to force a veto that can be used in 2028. Representative Ross says the plan is to make the President look bad until he “chickens out.” The plan bypasses committee hearings for the first batch because the bills are, in the leadership’s words, consensus items within their own caucus.


I disagree with this way of legislating, and I would ask the leaders of both parties to abandon it.


**Is it righteous?** A law is a command backed by the force of the state. To use the law-making power chiefly to produce a campaign advantage is to use the gravest power the people grant for a purpose they did not grant it. A bill that skips hearings because it is popular within one caucus has skipped the step in which the other half of the country is heard. Speed is not a sin. Some of these bills have been debated for years, and a war-powers vote cannot wait for committees to organize. But a “shock-and-awe” sprint designed to “demonstrate within hours” is a demonstration, and a legislature is not a stage.


**Is it loving?** “What unites us and divides them” treats half of one’s fellow citizens as the opponent to be divided rather than the neighbor to be persuaded. A strategy that succeeds by deepening division will be met by the same strategy from the other side when power changes hands — and power always changes hands.


**Would I want it done to me?** No. And neither party would. That is the test, and both have failed it.


### What I would ask of a Democratic majority


If the Democrats win the House, I would ask them to govern as though the bills they pass might become law and stay law. That means:


- **Lead with the constitutional items, where they are right.** A war-powers vote, a tariff statute that returns trade to Congress, and a complete stock-trading ban can draw Republican votes. Write them to do so — not to force a veto, but to pass.

- **Separate the targeted from the sweeping.** A bill repairing Medicaid for the disabled and the frail elderly can pass; a bill reversing a whole law cannot. Choose the one that helps people.

- **Investigate by rules that will bind your own side.** Every oversight finding should end in a proposed law that applies to every future President.

- **Give the other side a hearing.** Committee consideration is not delay. It is the means by which a law is made good enough to last.


### What I would ask of my own readers


Do not judge this agenda as a package. Judge it item by item, by the same standard you apply to your own side. Where the Democrats are right — on the war power, on Congress’s authority over trade, on corruption and stock trading — say so out loud, and press your Republican representatives to vote with them. Where they are wrong — on charity by mandate, on subsidies without end, on a strategy of division — say that too, with reasons and without contempt.


And remember that no majority, of either party, can do what only a changed people can. A Congress can restore the war power to its rightful place. It cannot make a nation that loves its neighbor, raises its children, cares for its own poor, and keeps its word. That is the work of the church and the family, and it is the premise of my candidacy. The law can restrain the wicked. Only grace can change the heart.
