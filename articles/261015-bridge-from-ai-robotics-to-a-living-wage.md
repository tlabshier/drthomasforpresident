# 261015 - Bridge from AI-Robotics to a Living Wage

# A Bridge Across the Robot Age: A Living Wage, Retraining, and the Road to Broad Ownership


*Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | 15 October 2026*


We are entering an age in which machines will do most of the work. AI-driven robots will farm, build, manufacture, haul, and increasingly do the clerical and professional work that once required trained men and women. The goods will exist. The question is how a man who no longer has a job can purchase a claim to his share. I propose an answer: pay a living wage to those the economy has released, train them for the work that still needs doing, finance the transition with taxes on production and public debt used for productive purposes, and let the market keep deciding what gets made. I offer it as the best plan I have found, and I hold it open to a better one.


## The problem


For two hundred years, every labor-saving machine has been followed by new work. In 1900, about forty percent of American workers were on farms. Today it is under two percent. The freed workers went to work making cars, radios, refrigerators, and computers, and providing the services of a modern economy. Much of that new work exists because sales and marketing persuaded people to want things they hadn’t wanted before. Our employment system is calibrated to appetite.


In our system, a man claims a share of the nation’s production in one of four ways: he sells his labor, he owns something productive, he receives charity, or the government transfers it to him. As robots replace labor, the first route narrows. If ownership of the robots stays concentrated, the owners receive nearly all the income, and everyone else depends on them or on the government.


## Why the other answers fall short


One answer is a modern Jubilee: give every family a share of the nation’s productive capital. The goal is right. Scripture’s picture of peace is *“every man under his vine and under his fig tree.”* But someone owns the existing vines and fig trees. To hand them to everyone else, the government would have to take them from their owners. That is coercion, and in principle it is no different from taxing the productive to support the unproductive. A just plan cannot begin by seizing what men have lawfully built.


Another answer is time: if machines do the work, everyone works less. But shorter hours distribute nothing on their own. A man who works fewer hours earns less, and the man who works none earns nothing. Unless something carries goods from the few who produce to the many who don’t, shorter hours simply describe unemployment politely. Producers will not give their goods away. If the government taxes producers so non-workers can buy those goods, then producers become the only people in the nation carrying a real burden of work and responsibility. That feels unfair and unmotivating.


So both answers point to the same conclusion. At some point, the nation will need its government to help its people move from a world where nearly every man works for his bread to one where machines produce most of it. The question is how to build that bridge without destroying the motivation, property, and freedom that made the abundance possible.


## The proposal


The plan keeps the capitalist system running while it carries the nation across. Little would change in how goods are made, priced, and sold. Five things would be added.


- **A living wage for those the economy releases.** When a machine takes a man’s job, he receives a wage sufficient to live on. He is not cast out of the game because the game no longer needs his old skill. As machines make goods and services more plentiful and cheaper, the living wage adjusts with them, so it buys a decent life without being fixed to a dollar figure that grows obsolete.

- **Retraining as the condition and the purpose of that wage.** The man between jobs trains for work that still needs doing: building, installing, maintaining, and supervising the robotic systems themselves, or work the machines have not yet reached. He works that skill until it too becomes obsolete, and then he trains again. The living wage is a bridge to the next work, not a couch.

- **Financing comes from taxes on production and public debt.** Producers pay taxes, as they do now. As their workforces shrink, their labor costs fall, and they can carry a larger share of the cost of supporting those who no longer work. Where taxes fall short, the nation borrows. Public debt is rightly used to increase productive capacity, and training a man to be productive is an investment in capacity, as surely as building a factory.

- **Markets and the financial industry continue to allocate capital.** Producers still have to read what people want, adjust their output, borrow for new equipment, and bear the risk of being wrong. Banks and investors still judge which AI-robotics ventures deserve capital. The government does not decide how many shoelaces to make.

- **Work is rewarded according to service and risk, within limits.** The entrepreneur who builds and runs the productive machinery still earns more than the man on the living wage. He can still buy the house on the shore, in the mountains, or far out on the prairie. That reward incentivizes work and risk-taking rather than simply collecting the living wage. But in an economy where machines do most of the work, much of a producer’s output comes from the nation’s common inheritance of knowledge, infrastructure, and order. His reward should be generous, but not unlimited, because his work has become, in part, a public service.


Over time, if this works, the economy produces more with fewer workers. Most people receive a living wage, some work because they want what it pays, and the transition moves the nation toward a society in which the machines’ fruits are broadly shared.


## The moral justification


Every public policy should answer one question: would my neighbor feel loved by this act in the public square?


Consider the man whose job a machine has taken. He did nothing wrong. He showed up, learned his trade, and did it well. The nation chose to replace him because the machine was cheaper, and the nation as a whole will be richer for that choice. If we tell him he’s on his own now, he will not feel loved. He will feel discarded by the very prosperity his work helped build. A nation that reaps the gain of automation owes something to the men who bear its cost.


Paul’s rule still stands: *“If any would not work, neither should he eat.” ***(2 Thessalonians 3:10)**. But notice whom it addresses. It addresses the man who *will not* work, not the man for whom there is no work. That is why this plan ties the living wage to retraining. The man who refuses to learn, refuses to work when work is offered, and refuses to participate is in a different position from the man the economy has released. A just system keeps that distinction.


Consider also the entrepreneur. He took the risk, built the business, and made the machines productive. If we take everything he earns, he will not feel loved either. He will feel robbed and stop building. So the plan leaves him his reward, and it asks him to carry more of the public burden only as his own labor costs fall.


We have done something like this before. After the Second World War, the GI Bill paid millions of returning servicemen to go to college and trade school. The nation financed it while it was carrying the war debt. Those veterans became engineers, teachers, builders, and businessmen, and the investment repaid itself many times over in the productive capacity it created. The plan I propose is the same idea applied to a different kind of transition. Even some of the strongest defenders of free markets have proposed an income floor. Milton Friedman argued for a negative income tax, a payment to those below a set income, as a simpler and less intrusive alternative to the maze of welfare programs.


## The hard parts


A plan is only as good as its answers to the strongest objections. Here are the ones I take most seriously.


### Printing money and the honest measure


The largest risk is financing. If the government borrows or creates money to pay people who are not producing, and the machines have not yet produced the goods those dollars will chase, prices rise. Inflation is a hidden tax. It takes from every man who saved, without a vote and without a law. I have called it the modern form of clipping coins, the *“divers weights”* the Bible condemns. I cannot propose printing money without holding myself to that standard.


The principle that resolves this is that the true limit is not dollars but real goods. Money created to pay a living wage is honest only if the goods it buys actually exist. When robots produce more food, housing, and power than the people who still work can buy, putting money in the hands of those who can’t buy it doesn’t raise prices; it lets the abundance reach the people it was made for. When robots haven’t yet produced that abundance, the same money only bids up prices on what exists. So the pace matters. The living wage should grow as productive capacity grows, and borrowing should go first to what increases that capacity: training, equipment, and infrastructure. That is the same rule a prudent family follows: borrow to build, not to consume. Debt that builds a workshop can be repaid from what the workshop produces. Debt that pays for this month’s groceries cannot.


### How ownership broadens without confiscation


The goal of broad family ownership is right, and I rejected taking existing property to reach it. But an honest path exists. When the nation borrows to finance new productive capacity, the people who carry that debt should own a share of what it builds. If public credit finances a robotic factory, a power plant, or a training program that makes a company more productive, the public should hold a stake in the return, held in a citizens’ fund that pays dividends to every family. Alaska has done something like this since 1982 with its oil revenue: the fund is owned in common, and every resident receives a dividend as a return on that ownership, not as charity.


No one’s existing property is taken. The owner of today’s factory keeps it. But the new capacity of the robot age, to the extent the public finances it, belongs in part to the public that financed it. Over a generation, that is how the living wage could become, in large part, a dividend on ownership rather than a transfer from producers. That is the transition from a wage economy toward distributism, accomplished by building rather than seizing.


### Fairness to producers


If producers carry the tax burden while most citizens receive a living wage, producers may feel like the nation’s only workers and stop producing or move elsewhere. The plan has to keep their reward real. Their tax burden should rise only as their labor costs fall, so automation still pays them. The public stake described above should come from new capital the public helps finance, not from a claim on what they built alone. And the reward limit should be a gentle slope, not a ceiling, because a ceiling tells the most capable people in the nation that, past a certain point, their work is worthless to them.


### Motivation


A guaranteed wage weakens the most ancient motivator: hunger. Some men will take the living wage and do nothing. That is why the wage is tied to retraining and participation, and why work must still visibly pay more than the living wage. But I do not think hunger is the motivator God intended. Before the fall, God put Adam in the garden to dress it and keep it. Work was given as stewardship and delight, and the curse added toil and the threat of want. A nation that works only because it is hungry will decay when it is no longer hungry. A nation that works because it loves God, loves its neighbor, and loves the world He made will flourish. No economic plan can create that heart. But a plan can avoid crushing it, and it can give a man time and training to find the work he was made for.


### Whether retraining works


Government retraining programs have a mixed record. For example, programs for workers displaced by trade have shown uneven results in studies of afterward earnings. Training works best when it ties to real jobs employers need filled and when employers help design it. The plan should pay for training that leads to work, measure what happens to the men who take it, and drop what doesn’t work.


## Conclusion


The robot age will force a choice. We can leave the distribution of its abundance to whoever owns the machines, and watch a growing share of our people fall outside the game. We can seize the machines and hand them out, and destroy the motivation and property that built them. Or we can build a bridge: a living wage for those the machines release, training to bring them back into productive work, public debt used to build rather than consume, a public stake in what that debt builds, and markets left free to decide what gets made.


I favor the bridge. It keeps the free market working while the nation crosses. It honors the man who lost his job through no fault of his own, and it honors the man who took the risk to build. It moves us toward broad ownership by building new capacity rather than taking existing property.


I hold this the way I hold every position I take: as the best argument I have found so far, and I am open to a better one. The hardest questions are the pace of the living wage, the honest limits of public borrowing, and how to keep the producer’s reward real. Those deserve the nation’s best minds, and I invite the debate.

## Plain Language summary


As robots take over most work, people still need a way to claim a share of what the machines produce. This plan pays a living wage to people whose jobs are automated, ties it to retraining for work that still needs doing, and funds it through taxes on producers and public borrowing to build productive capacity. Markets still decide what gets made, and producers still earn more than the living wage. The essay names the biggest risk: printing money causes inflation unless the goods actually exist, so the wage should grow only as robotic output grows. It also offers a non-coercive path to broad ownership: when public money finances new productive capacity, the public owns a share and receives dividends, as Alaska’s oil fund does.
