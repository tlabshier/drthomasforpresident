# 261113 - Four Hour Air Traffic Grounding

# When the Sky Went Quiet:


### The FAA Ground Stop, an Aging System, and Who Should Pay to Fix It

**By Thomas Lee Abshier, ND |** *November 13, 2026*


---


## What Happened

On Friday afternoon, November 13, 2026, around 4 p.m. Eastern, the Federal Aviation Administration stopped every departing flight at every airport in the United States. The FAA said it issued the order "due to extraordinary circumstances and for reasons of safety" after communication problems hit the Air Route Traffic Control Centers — the regional facilities that guide aircraft between airports. Planes already in the air landed safely. Planes on the ground stayed there.

The stop was lifted shortly after 8:30 p.m. Eastern. Depending on whose clock you use, it lasted between four and five hours. More than 10,000 flights were delayed or canceled, and the airlines warned the disruption would carry into the weekend. It was the first nationwide ground stop since January 2023, and it lasted roughly three times as long.

That evening, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin explained it at the White House. Secretary Duffy said the cause was an "incredibly old air traffic control system." In his account, switches and routers in the FAA's telecommunications network were overloaded with data and began giving controllers inconsistent information, so the FAA shut the system down rather than direct traffic on bad data. He noted that some switches replaced recently did not fail. Secretary Mullin said there was "zero evidence" of a cyberattack and that the failure appeared to be mechanical. Duffy then asked Congress for $50 billion to build a "state-of-the-art" air traffic control system, on top of the $12.5 billion already approved in the One Big Beautiful Bill.

You have asked three questions: whether the diagnosis is credible, what I would do about it as president, and whether a $50 billion rebuild should come out of public funds or be paid for by the industry that uses the system. I will take them in order.

## The Question Under the Question

Most people who sat in a terminal on Friday were not asking about routers. They were asking two deeper questions. First: are the people responsible for the systems my life depends on taking care of them? Second: when those systems fail, am I being told the truth about why?

Both are moral questions. A steward is judged by the condition of what was entrusted to him, not by the eloquence of his explanation after something breaks:

*"Be thou diligent to know the state of thy flocks, and look well to thy herds."* **(Proverbs 27:23)**

So I will answer the technical question, but I will judge it against the steward's standard.

## Is the Diagnosis Credible?

My answer: **The FAA's "aging system" diagnosis may be the general cause of Friday's failure. But the specific cause of Friday's failure has not been established, and the official explanation should be treated as a working hypothesis until someone independent confirms it.**

### Why the general diagnosis may hold up

The best evidence that the system is old and fragile does not come from Friday's press conference. It comes from years of documentation by parties with no stake in this week's funding request.


 	- **The Government Accountability Office's 2024 assessment.** After the 2023 outage, the FAA reviewed all 138 of its air traffic control systems. It found 51 of them (37 percent) unsustainable and another 54 (39 percent) potentially unsustainable — more than three-quarters of the systems controllers depend on. The GAO also found that 58 of those 105 weak systems critically affect the safety and efficiency of the national airspace.

 	- **Newark, April–May 2025.** The radar and radio links serving Newark's airspace failed several times. The FAA blamed old copper telecommunications lines and a facility move made without adequate testing.

 	- **The Northeast, September 21, 2026.** Less than two months ago, a single circuit failed. When the FAA switched to its backup, it found an Amtrak construction crew in New Jersey had cut the backup fiber cable. Ground stops rolled across Newark, JFK, LaGuardia, Philadelphia, and Boston for more than eight hours, and more than 7,000 flights were delayed or canceled.

 	- **January 2023.** The national NOTAM system (the system that sends hazard notices to pilots) failed after contract workers accidentally deleted files, and the whole country was grounded for about ninety minutes.


This record shows a network patched for decades, carrying old technology alongside new, with single points of failure where there should be several independent paths. Secretary Duffy's account of overloaded switches giving inconsistent data fits that record. His remark that the newly replaced switches held up also fits it: if old hardware is the weak link, the new hardware should be the part that survives.

### Why the specific cause is not yet established

Here I apply the standard I use for every contested claim on this site: grade the evidence by its kind. What we heard on Friday night was testimony from interested parties, given within hours, before any investigation was finished. That does not make it false. It does make it unconfirmed.

Four things keep the question open:


 	- **The explanation arrived before the investigation did.** Secretary Duffy said in the same briefing that his team was still working out what had happened and what caused it. The FAA's own leaders did not brief the press and released no technical detail. The announced diagnosis also supports the funding request announced in the same breath. That does not prove the diagnosis wrong, but it means someone who does not benefit from it needs to check it.

 	- **An overload needs a cause.** Old equipment fails under load, but something has to produce the load. When reporters asked why the routers would be overwhelmed on an otherwise normal travel day, Duffy did not have a ready answer. Secretary Mullin offered a different account: that newer equipment and technology connected to the old system had overwhelmed it. Those are two different stories. One says old equipment wore out. The other says the upgrade process itself set off the failure. Neither official said which new technology was meant.

 	- **"Nobody claimed it" is weak evidence.** Secretary Mullin reasoned that cyberattackers usually take credit and that there was no chatter. Criminal groups often do claim their attacks. State actors probing American infrastructure generally do not, because their aim is to learn and to position themselves, not to advertise. Over the evening, his own assessment moved from "there is actually no cyber attack" to "nothing completely certain right now." Absence of evidence a few hours after an event is not evidence of absence.

 	- **The timing invites questions.** The failure came days after the FAA rolled out a new AI-assisted traffic management tool called SMART, and on the same day as an unrelated Microsoft outage. Officials say neither was connected: Duffy said SMART is not plugged into the communication network, and Mullin said there was "no connection" to Microsoft. Those may well be correct. They are still claims that an independent review should confirm, not assume.


### Why the concern remains plausible

The public's unease is not paranoia. It has a rational basis. When a whole nation's airspace can be stopped by one category of network equipment, it means the system was designed, or allowed to decay, without the redundancy a system of this importance requires. That is true whether Friday's trigger was a worn router, a botched integration of new equipment, or a hostile probe. The same weakness that lets a failing circuit stop the Northeast for eight hours would let an adversary do the same thing deliberately. Admiral James Stavridis made this point after the 2023 outage: whether or not it was a cyberattack, it showed what one could look like.

A second, older reason for unease remains. This system's modernization has been promised before. The Advanced Automation System of the 1980s and 1990s was abandoned after billions were spent. NextGen, launched in the early 2000s, has run for two decades without delivering the promised transformation. Senator Tammy Duckworth, who supports more funding, has pointed to that bipartisan history as the reason lawmakers are wary. Money has been spent, and the outages continue, so citizens have reason to ask where it went before they agree to send more.

My conclusion: **the system is genuinely aging and fragile. That is established. The exact cause of Friday's failure is not.** I believe the most likely explanation is an engineering failure at the boundary between old and new equipment. I am open to correction by an independent forensic finding, and I believe the American people are owed one.

## The Moral Test

Every policy I propose on this site goes through three questions in order: *Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? Would I want it done to me?*

**Is it righteous?** Government is established to protect life and to serve the common good **(Romans 13:4)**. The skies above us are a shared highway. No airline can run its own traffic control, and no traveler can check whether the radar works. When people are forced to depend on one shared system, whoever governs it has a duty to keep it sound. Neglecting that duty until it fails, and then asking for an emergency appropriation, is not righteous stewardship. Keeping it in repair is. So yes: rebuilding this system is a legitimate and necessary act of government.

**Is it loving?** Love in public policy means caring for the people who depend on the system — the pilot, the traveler, the family waiting at the gate, the shipper whose medicine or machine part is on a cargo flight. It also means caring for the neighbor who pays for the system. Taxing a Montana rancher who never flies to pay for a business traveler's convenience is not loving. Neither is letting the system decay because no one wants to send the bill.

**Would I want it done to me?** If I flew every week, I would want to pay for the system I use, in proportion to my use, and I would want the money spent on that system and nothing else. If I never flew, I would be willing to pay my share of what protects the nation as a whole, such as defense and emergency control of the airspace, but not for someone else's ticket. That test points to the answer on funding.

## Who Should Pay: Public Funds or the Industry?

The question is usually framed as a choice: either the public pays, because the cost is too high for business, or business pays and passes it on to the customers.

### Users already pay for most of it

Most people do not know that the FAA's capital budget is already funded mostly by aviation users. The Airport and Airway Trust Fund, created in 1970, collects a 7.5 percent tax on domestic airline tickets, a per-segment fee, taxes on aviation fuel, a cargo waybill tax, and international arrival and departure taxes. In fiscal year 2024, those taxes brought in about $18.3 billion. The trust fund pays for the FAA's Facilities and Equipment account, which buys radars, radios, and telecommunications equipment. So the choice is not between taxpayers and business. Every passenger who buys a ticket is already paying for air traffic control.

The problem is not that users don't pay. The problem is how the money moves. Congress still has to appropriate trust fund revenue year by year. That produces piecemeal, stop-and-start projects, and a system that is always being patched and never rebuilt. Duffy himself has said Congress tied the $12.5 billion to specific uses and the department "can't switch pots of money."

### It is not too big for business

Fifty billion dollars sounds enormous, but it is not too large to be financed privately. Electric utilities, pipelines, and railroads finance capital programs of that scale through bonds repaid from their revenues. Canada's air traffic system, NAV CANADA, has been run since 1996 as a nonprofit, self-financed corporation. It is funded by user fees, holds an AA bond rating, and borrows against its revenue to modernize all at once instead of facility by facility. Its size is not the obstacle. Its structure is what makes it work.

### There is a real public share

Air traffic control is not only a commercial service. It is also national defense infrastructure. The same radars and controllers that guide airliners also watch the approaches to American cities. On September 11, 2001, the air traffic system cleared the entire sky in a matter of hours. Military aircraft, homeland security flights, and emergency operations use the system without buying tickets. It is fair for the general treasury, meaning all of us, to pay for that share, and it should be stated and accounted for as a defense cost, not hidden inside a transportation bill.

### My position

**I hold that the rebuild should be paid for mainly by the people who use the system, through the existing trust fund taxes, financed up front with revenue bonds so the job is done in one coherent build rather than over decades of annual appropriations. A defined, published share for defense and security should come from general funds.** The constitutional basis is plain: Congress is given power over commerce among the states and over providing for the common defense (Article I, Section 8), and no money may leave the Treasury except by appropriation (Article I, Section 9). The moral basis comes first and is just as plain: those who benefit should pay in proportion to their benefit, and those who are protected should pay for their protection.

I also hold that, over time, the system should be separated from the agency that regulates its safety. More than a hundred countries keep the air traffic operator organizationally separate from the safety regulator, because an agency that runs a system cannot be trusted to grade its own work. I lean toward a self-funded, government-chartered nonprofit corporation, not a for-profit company, with a board that includes airlines, general aviation, controllers, and the public, and with the FAA remaining as the independent safety regulator.

I hold this view open to a better argument, and the opposing arguments are serious:


 	- American airspace is roughly twice the size of Canada's and far busier, so success in Canada does not guarantee success here.

 	- Private pilots and small airports fear a fee-funded operator would raise costs or close towers. NAV CANADA has reduced service at some smaller locations when traffic fell.

 	- NAV CANADA has had its own controller shortages and delay events, and critics cite declining international audit scores for Canada's safety oversight.

 	- When air travel collapsed in 2020, fee-funded systems lost their revenue overnight. Any such corporation would need reserves and a government backstop for a national emergency.

 	- Controllers' unions strongly oppose privatization.


These objections do not change my view, but they shape it. Any charter I proposed would guarantee access for general aviation, keep the safety regulator independent, require a reserve fund, and give the government emergency control of the airspace, as it holds now.

### Is $50 billion the right number?

I do not know, and I do not believe anyone has yet shown the public that it is. As recently as last year, Secretary Duffy said the plan required $19 to $20 billion beyond the $12.5 billion already approved, about $31.5 billion in total. On Friday night the figure was $50 billion. That may reflect a fuller accounting, or it may be a number spoken in the heat of a crisis. In September 2026, the GAO reported that the FAA's new modernization effort needs better cost and schedule planning. It found the first phase already estimated at $10.6 billion, with part of it unfunded, and the largest overrun in telecommunications — the very part that failed on Friday.

*"For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost, whether he have sufficient to finish it?"* **(Luke 14:28)**

A wise builder counts the cost before he builds. I would not sign a blank check. I would not refuse to build, either.

## What I Would Do as President

My platform, The New ERA Vision, rests on three words: Energy, Righteousness, and Accountability. This event calls for the third. Here is what I would do.

**1. Order an independent investigation, with a public report within sixty days.** The agency whose budget depends on it should not write the findings. I would direct the Department of Transportation's Inspector General to lead, with forensic support from cybersecurity specialists outside the FAA and an outside engineering review. I would require the report to answer the questions left open on Friday: what produced the overload, whether a recent upgrade or patch played a part, how the new equipment interacted with the old, and whether any outside party was involved. The public should see the conclusion and the evidence.

**2. Make redundancy the first priority.** Before anything else is built, no single failure — a router, a circuit, a cut cable — should be able to stop the national airspace or a major region of it. That means telecommunications on physically separate routes, backups that are tested regularly rather than discovered broken during a crisis, and published reliability figures each quarter so the public can see whether the system is improving. Redundancy can be bought relatively quickly and for a fraction of the full rebuild, and I would ask for it immediately.

**3. Put a fully costed plan before Congress.** Before the full appropriation, I would put a complete plan before Congress and the public. It would set out what will be replaced, in what order, at what cost, with what milestones, and who is accountable if a milestone slips. Funds would be released in phases as milestones are independently verified. The urgent work goes ahead now; the rest is paid for as it is proven.

**4. Fund it honestly.** I would ask Congress for revenue-bond authority backed by the trust fund's aviation taxes, so the rebuild can be done at once and paid for by its users over its useful life, with a separate, stated appropriation from general funds for the defense and security share. I would also propose a charter separating the operator from the regulator, with the protections described above, and I would ask for a full debate on it rather than forcing it through.

**5. Do not neglect the people.** New radars do not direct airplanes; controllers do. The system is short about 3,500 controllers. Controllers themselves have said that new equipment solves only part of the problem. Hiring, training, and keeping controllers must be funded alongside the hardware.

**6. Stop assigning blame and start taking responsibility.** This system declined under presidents and Congresses of both parties for forty years. Pointing to the last administration is easy and accomplishes nothing. As president, I would take ownership of the system's condition from day one, and I would ask Congress to do the same.

## Conclusion

Friday's ground stop was handled safely. No one was hurt, and the controllers and technicians who brought every airborne plane down deserve our thanks. But a nation that can be grounded by its own routers has been warned, and this is not the first warning. The GAO warned in 2024, Newark warned in 2025, and the Northeast warned in September.

The diagnosis of an aging system is credible. The specific cause should be independently confirmed. The rebuild should be done, but counted before it is built, paid for mainly by those who use it, paid by all of us for the part that defends us, and governed so that the operator is never left to grade his own work. That is what diligent stewardship of the flock looks like, and it is what I would ask of the government I hope to lead.

## Sources and Their Limits

This essay was written the evening of the event, based on reporting gathered within hours. The account of the cause rests almost entirely on statements by Transportation Secretary Duffy, Homeland Security Secretary Mullin, and President Trump. As administration officials requesting the funding, they are interested parties, and their statements have not been independently verified. The FAA released no technical detail. I found no independent technical analysis of Friday's failure, from mainstream or alternative sources, because none had been published yet. The questions I raise about the official account come from what reporters and officials said at the briefing, not from any independent investigation. The background record — GAO reports, the trust fund, and past outages — rests on government documents and established reporting, which are more reliable but still official. The privatization debate draws on advocacy on both sides (Reason Foundation in favor; NBAA and others opposed), and I have tried to present each side's strongest case. If a fuller investigation changes the facts, I will revise this essay.
