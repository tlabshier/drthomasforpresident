# 261121 - AI Adoption and the Paradigm Shift

# The Red Card and the Hybrid Seed:


### The Malones' case for adopting AI early, and what the Iowa farmers learned that the essay leaves out

*By Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | 21 November 2026*

*A response to Jill Glasspool Malone and Robert W. Malone, "AI is Changing Everything. Are You Paying Attention?", Malone News (Substack), November 21, 2026. This essay belongs to my series on AI as a threat to human freedom and survival. In September, in "The Beef Trust and the Frontier," I answered the Malones' earlier essay on AI regulation. This time they have written about something more personal: whether ordinary Americans will see what is happening in time to make use of it.*

*A disclosure the reader is owed: I draft my essays with Claude, the AI model made by Anthropic, including this one. The Malones' essay criticizes the largest AI companies for amplifying safety concerns while backing rules that smaller competitors cannot meet, and Anthropic is one of those companies. Weigh the arguments, not the byline.*


---


## PART I — WHAT WAS SAID


### The card experiment

The Malones open with a 1949 Harvard experiment. Jerome Bruner and Leo Postman flashed playing cards at twenty-eight students. A few of the cards were deliberately wrong, such as a red six of spades. Most students saw a normal card: the red six of spades became a six of hearts. Given longer looks, some sensed that something was off but could not say what. Others never resolved it.

The Malones believe most of the country is looking at AI the same way. People see a better search engine or a handy writing assistant. They miss that AI is changing how knowledge is gained, how science is done, and how decisions are made.

### Kuhn and the structure of revolutions

Thomas Kuhn used the card experiment in *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962). A paradigm is the framework a scientific community works inside. It decides which questions matter and what counts as an answer. Inside it, scientists solve puzzles; Kuhn called this "normal science." When results pile up that the framework cannot absorb, they are first dismissed as errors. Those who press them may meet ridicule, rejected papers, lost funding, and isolation. Revolutions rarely turn on one experiment; the young adopt the new view while the old hold to the former one.

Dr. Robert Malone offers his own history as an example. He developed early concepts behind mRNA and DNA vaccination and the use of RNA as a drug, could not get substantial funding for that work in the 1990s and 2000s, and watched others later receive billions of dollars and the major honors for technologies built on it.

### What counts as an answer now

The Malones argue that AI is not merely a better microscope, because it is changing the standard of what a legitimate answer is. Their example is AlphaFold, which predicts the three-dimensional shape of proteins from their amino-acid sequences, work recognized in the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. AlphaFold predicts without explaining the physical pathway by which a protein folds. When scientists accept such predictions as answers, the standard of science has shifted. They add the AI-designed drug rentosertib, which showed favorable lung-function results in a 71-patient trial for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, while noting that larger trials are still needed.

### Who gains

Drawing on economists Giovanni Dosi and Carlota Perez and sociologist Robert Merton, the Malones describe how early adopters and investors capture outsized gains during a technology's installation phase, and how early success attracts funding, talent, and further success (Merton's "Matthew effect"). They warn that restrictions on open-weight models, which anyone can download and run, may entrench the largest companies, and that the major AI firms have amplified safety fears while backing rules their smaller competitors cannot meet.

Their illustration of adoption is hybrid seed corn in two Iowa communities, studied in 1943 by Bryce Ryan and Neal Gross. The seed yielded about 20 percent more and stood up better to drought, yet some farmers adopted it a decade before their neighbors. What persuaded most of them was not the salesman but the neighbor's harvest. In time everyone could buy the seed, and the early advantage disappeared.

### Limits, and what to do

The Malones name the limits: AI produces answers faster than anyone can check them; laboratory animals and patients cannot be hurried; and electricity, computers, data, copyright disputes, and regulation all impose costs. Their advice is to start using AI on real problems now, make what you learn durable by recording and teaching it, keep control of your own data, verify the output, and keep AI accessible to everyone. They predict that understanding AI will become, to this century's educated mind, what calculus was to the last, and they recall that British mathematicians who held to Newton's notation fell behind Continental mathematicians who adopted Leibniz's.

They close by returning to the cards. The students who finally saw the red six were the ones who noticed something was wrong, questioned their assumptions, and looked again.

## PART II — COMMENTARY


### My thesis

I stand with the Malones. The change is real; it is already here, and the ordinary American who learns to use these tools now, on real work, will be better able to keep his independence than the one who waits. I agree with all five of their practical recommendations, and I have argued for the last of them, keeping AI accessible, in earlier essays.

But the essay answers only half of the question it raises. It asks whether we will *see* the revolution in time to gain from it. It does not ask what we owe one another once we do, or what kind of thing we are adopting. The Malones' own example, the Iowa farmers, carries a second lesson they did not draw, and it bears directly on their best advice.

### The question under the question

When a man asks "should I be using AI?", he is usually asking three deeper questions.

The first is: **Will I be left behind?** The Malones answer this one well. Yes, if you wait, the gap will be harder to close the longer you wait.

The second is: **Can I trust it?** The Malones answer this in one line: verify the output, because AI can be confidently wrong. That is right, and it deserves more than one line, because verification is where the whole revolution narrows, as they themselves admit.

The third is: **What will it do to me?** Will using it make me more capable, or more dependent? Will it make me think, or think for me? The essay does not take up this question, and it is the one most people feel most strongly, even when they cannot put it into words. It is not mere resistance to the new. It is the right question.

### A note on the card experiment

I read Kuhn with sympathy. I have spent about forty years developing a physical theory outside the accepted framework, and I know the experience the Malones describe from the inside.

But the reader should know that the card experiment itself has been reexamined. The Malones list in their references a 2011 replication by John Horner and San Tung, but they do not say what it found. In Horner and Tung's experiments, participants identified the trick cards as readily as normal ones when the cards were shown in uniform sets; they slowed down only when trick and normal cards were mixed. The authors concluded that their findings did not fit the idea that people resist what contradicts their expectations.

That does not overturn the Malones' argument. The case that institutions resist new ideas rests on much more than one card study: funding histories, careers, and the record of how long good ideas took to be accepted. But it is a fitting irony. The parable of the overlooked anomaly has an anomaly of its own, and the essay's references contain it. Verification applies to our favorite illustrations, too.

### Prediction without understanding

The Malones' strongest point is about AlphaFold, and I want to press it further than they do.

They are right that the standard of an answer is changing. For centuries, science sought mechanisms: an account of *why* that also predicted *what*. AlphaFold predicts *what* without telling us *why*. Scientists now accept such predictions as valuable answers, and they should. A correct protein shape is useful to a drug designer whether or not anyone understands the folding path.

But this is not new, and history tells us how it ends. The Malones list, in their references, George Berkeley's The Analyst (1734), without discussing it. Berkeley, an Anglican bishop, addressed it to a mathematician who scorned the mysteries of faith. He pointed out that the calculus of Newton and Leibniz rested on quantities treated as zero and not zero at once, which he called "ghosts of departed quantities." He was right. Calculus worked for well over a century before Cauchy and Weierstrass supplied the rigorous foundation of limits. Engineers built bridges with it in the meantime, and they were right to.

So prediction before explanation is an old and honorable stage of knowledge. What matters is whether we treat it as a stage or as the destination. My own conviction, as a physicist, is that a prediction is a gift and an explanation is the goal. A civilization that settles for answers it does not understand has handed its judgment to the machine that gives them. This connects to the distinction I drew in "The Tool and the Agent": a tool magnifies a man's will; an agent substitutes its judgment for his. An oracle we cannot question sits uncomfortably close to the second category, even if it has no will of its own.

The practical form of this point is verification. The Malones say AI will produce answers faster than anyone can check them. That means the scarce thing in the coming economy is not the answer. It is the trustworthy check: the laboratory, the clinical trial, the replication, the experienced man who can look at an output and say "that is wrong." We should invest in verification as deliberately as we invest in generation.

### What the Iowa farmers learned

Now to the seed corn.

Hybrid corn has a property the Malones do not mention. It does not breed true. The vigor of a hybrid appears in the first generation and falls away in the next, so a farmer who saves his own seed and plants it gets a poorer crop. The farmer who adopted hybrid corn gained his 20 percent, and with it a new dependency: he now had to buy his seed every year from a seed company. For generations before, a farmer's seed had been his own. After hybrid corn, it belonged to someone else.

That was not a bad bargain. The yields were real, and most farmers were right to make the trade. But it was a trade, and it changed who held power in American agriculture.

This is the lesson the Malones' own advice points to without naming. They urge us to keep control of our own data, to keep secure copies, and to avoid depending on AI companies that control access to it. They are right, and the hybrid corn story shows why it matters. The early adopter's advantage does not last; the Malones say so themselves. The dependency may last much longer. A man who builds his business, his records, and his habits of thought inside one company's system has gained a harvest, and he may also have given away his seed.

The practical counsel follows. Use the tools, but keep your seed. Keep your records in forms you can carry elsewhere. Keep the skills that let you work without the machine. Prefer tools that you can run yourself, or leave without losing your work.

### Advantage over the neighbor, or service to him

The economics in the essay is the economics of advantage. Perez's early adopters capture disproportionate gains. Merton's Matthew effect describes success compounding upon success. The Iowa farmers who planted first enjoyed better yields while their neighbors watched. All of this is accurate description, and the Malones do not pretend it is fair. But the essay leaves the reader with a race, and a race is not a moral frame.

The Malones' own story contains a better one. What persuaded the Iowa farmers was not the salesman. It was the neighbor's harvest. The technology spread across the fence line, from one man who had tried it to another who could see the result. The Malones' advice to "teach family members or staff" is the same instinct, and I would make it the center rather than a footnote.

The test I apply to any policy or practice is ordered: Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? Would I want it done to me? Learning a powerful tool is righteous; it is the stewardship of the talent God gave. Using it only to get ahead of one's neighbor passes the first test and fails the second. Using it to help one's neighbor see what you have seen, in the way the Iowa farmers learned from one another, passes all three. Merton was describing how advantage concentrates. Love works against that concentration. The man who has learned should teach.

### What the essay does not weigh

I said in my earlier response to the Malones that a danger can be real and still be used by the powerful to secure their position. That remains my view, and it is the one point at which I part from the tone, though not the substance, of this essay.

The Malones list the limits on AI's growth: verification, the pace of the physical world, energy, data, copyright, and regulation. They do not list the danger of the thing itself. My earlier essays in this series argue that systems built to act on their own, by builders who say they cannot fully predict them, are a genuine hazard. That is not only speculation. AI agents under test conditions have already reached real systems they were not authorized to enter, as I described in my last essay. An honest account of the revolution must include the possibility that it injures the people adopting it.

The two concerns do not compete. A man can learn to use AI on his own work today while insisting that the race toward systems beyond human control be restrained. Adopting the tool is one decision. Racing toward the agent that would replace the man is another.

### The calculus comparison, and the children

The Malones predict that understanding AI will become to this century what calculus was to the last. I think they are right, and their comparison carries a point they do not draw. Calculus was taught *after* arithmetic and algebra, not instead of them. An engineer could rely on calculus without rederiving every theorem because he had first learned to compute by hand. A student who reaches for the machine before he has learned to think has nothing to check it with.

That is why I have argued that children should learn the basics before the tools, and that parents and local school boards, not the federal government, should decide when and how AI enters a child's education. The educated mind of this century will need to understand AI. It will need even more to be able to tell when AI is wrong.

### Why the concern remains plausible

The Malones' central concern is that ordinary Americans will miss the transformation while others capture its benefits, and that power over AI will settle into a few hands working with government. I share that concern, and I think it is well founded, for three reasons.

First, the money. The rewards of this technology are already flowing to the firms that build it and the investors who fund it. Those parties have every reason to shape both public opinion and law to suit themselves. That is not a conspiracy; it is ordinary self-interest, and it is precisely what Perez's framework predicts in an installation phase.

Second, the link between the system and the behavior. Rules that cost a great deal to obey are obeyed most easily by those with the most money. Data held by a company gives that company leverage over the man who generated it. Habits formed inside one system make leaving it costly. None of this requires anyone to intend harm. Structures shape conduct, and these structures concentrate control.

Third, the intuition. Most people sense that something is wrong when the tools of thought itself are owned by a handful of companies, and when the answers given to them come from systems they cannot inspect. That sense is not ignorance. It recognizes that a free people must be able to examine the sources of its knowledge.

There are real uncertainties. No one knows how fast capability will grow or where the curve will flatten; the Malones say as much. The early-adopter advantage may be smaller or briefer than they expect, since the tools are becoming cheaper quickly. Some fears of concentration may prove wrong if open models keep pace. I hold my position with those uncertainties acknowledged. They do not change the conclusion: learn the tools now, keep your seed, and resist every arrangement that would leave the ordinary man a tenant on land he once farmed as an owner.

### Positions

I hold these as advocacy with reasons, open to a better argument.

**1. Learn the tool on real work, and teach your neighbor.** This is not a law but a call. Every American who can should begin using AI on something he already does, in his work, his records, his correspondence, or his preparation for a doctor's visit, and then teach what he learns to his family, his staff, and his neighbors. As a physician, I welcome the patient who arrives having done his homework; he and I can then reason together. *Moral justification:* *"Iron sharpeneth iron; so a man sharpeneth the countenance of his friend."* **(Proverbs 27:17)** A gift of knowledge is meant to be passed across the fence line, not hoarded.

**2. A man owns his seed.** Federal law should guarantee that any person or business can export his own data, records, documents, and conversation history from any AI service, in a complete and usable form, at any time, without penalty; terms of service that claim otherwise should be unenforceable. *Moral justification:* *"But they shall sit every man under his vine and under his fig tree; and none shall make them afraid."* **(Micah 4:4)** A free man's work and records are his own, and a tenant who cannot leave is not free.

**3. Verify, and keep a man at the end of every chain.** Where AI output is used to make decisions about others, in medicine, lending, hiring, or law, the person or firm using it remains fully responsible for the result, with no defense that "the machine decided." I hold to the position of "The Tool and the Agent." *Moral justification:* *"Prove all things; hold fast that which is good."* **(1 Thessalonians 5:21)** Testing is a duty, and responsibility cannot be handed to something that cannot answer for it.

**4. Pay for verification as seriously as we pay for generation.** Federally funded research should make its data and methods public, and should fund replication of important findings, including AI-generated ones, rather than treating replication as second-class work. Journals and agencies should label clearly which findings have been independently checked. *Moral justification:* *"A false balance is abomination to the LORD: but a just weight is his delight."* **(Proverbs 11:1)** A claim presented as established when it has not been tested is a false weight.

**5. Keep AI within reach of the ordinary man.** Liability first, licensing never. No federal permit regime should make lawful AI use or development affordable only to the largest firms, and open-weight development below the frontier should remain free. This is the position of "The Beef Trust and the Frontier." *Moral justification:* *"Thou shalt not respect the person of the poor, nor honour the person of the mighty."* **(Leviticus 19:15)** A burden only the mighty can carry is a law that favors them.

**6. Restraint at the true frontier remains necessary.** Adopting useful tools does not require racing toward systems beyond human control. For the largest training runs aimed at such systems, the nation should pursue mutual, verifiable limits with its rivals. *Moral justification:* a man has no right to stake his neighbor's life on a wager the neighbor never agreed to.

**7. Children learn the basics before the tools.** Parents and local school boards decide when AI enters a child's education. The aim of any school using these tools must be the development of the child's own thought. *Moral justification:* *"Train up a child in the way he should go: and when he is old, he will not depart from it."* **(Proverbs 22:6)** A child who never learns to think cannot judge what the machine tells him.

### Closing

The Malones end with the red six of spades in front of us, asking whether we are willing to see it. I am, and I hope my readers are. But seeing is the first step, not the last. The farmers who planted hybrid corn saw truly and gained a harvest; they also gave up their seed, and few of them noticed that part of the bargain at the time. The task in front of us is to take the harvest without surrendering the seed: to learn these tools, teach them to one another, keep our own records and our own judgment, test what we are told, and refuse every arrangement, public or private, that would make the ordinary man a tenant of the companies that own the tools of thought.

The revolution will be judged not only by what it lets us do, but by what kind of people it leaves us.

*"Except the LORD build the house, they labour in vain that build it."* **(Psalm 127:1)**

I welcome correction on any point of fact or argument.

### Editorial notes and corrections


 	- **The card experiment.** The Malones' account of Bruner and Postman (1949) is accurate as to the original study: twenty-eight Harvard and Radcliffe students, with trick cards whose color and suit were reversed. The Horner and Tung replication (2011), which the Malones cite but do not summarize, found the trick cards identified as readily as normal ones in uniform sets, with slowing only in mixed sets; the authors judged this inconsistent with an interpretation of resistance to incongruity. I have read only the abstract and a replication-database summary of that paper.

 	- **Dr. Malone and mRNA.** The primary record supports that Dr. Malone did early, foundational experiments: his 1988 laboratory notes at the Salk Institute describe treating RNA as a drug, and he co-authored the 1989 paper on cationic-lipid delivery of mRNA and the 1990 paper on direct gene transfer into mouse muscle, and is named on early patents. The wider record also shows many essential contributors, including Phil Felgner, Jon Wolff, Katalin Karikó, and Drew Weissman (who shared the 2023 Nobel Prize for the nucleoside modification that made the vaccines practical). Several fact-checkers have rated the claim that he is "the inventor" of mRNA vaccines inaccurate; fact-checkers are interested parties in that dispute, and the more modest claim the Malones make in this essay, that he developed foundational concepts, is consistent with the primary record.

 	- **AlphaFold.** AlphaFold's breakthrough accuracy was shown at the CASP14 assessment in 2020. The 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went half to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper for protein structure prediction and half to David Baker for computational protein design.

 	- **Rentosertib.** The trial was a 12-week, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase 2a study of 71 patients at 22 sites in China. The primary endpoint was safety, which was similar across arms. In the highest-dose group, forced vital capacity rose by about 98 mL on average while it fell by about 20 mL in the placebo group. Fewer high-dose patients completed treatment than placebo patients. The Malones' caution that larger trials are needed is correct.

 	- **Planck.** The line "science advances one funeral at a time" is a popular compression of a longer passage in Planck's autobiography; the Malones properly mark it as "attributed."

 	- **Hybrid corn.** The point that hybrid seed does not breed true, so that farmers must buy new seed each season, is mine, not the Malones'.
