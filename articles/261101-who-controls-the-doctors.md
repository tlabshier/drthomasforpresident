# Who Controls the Doctors? Cutting the Strings


Nov 1, 2026 · @Thomas Lee Abshier, ND


## Plain language summary


Dr. Larry Miller, after sixty years and 130,000 patients, drew the American doctor as a puppet. Insurers, hospital administrators, drug companies, federal agencies, licensing boards, and trial lawyers hold the strings, and the patient lies sick on the floor while the money flows past him. I stand with Dr. Miller. The strings are real, and most can be measured. Four of five doctors now work for a hospital or corporation. The government’s price-setting relies on a committee run by the medical societies. Insurers make the average doctor ask permission about forty times a week. Industry fees pay for most of the FDA’s drug-review program. A JAMA study found that hospitals earned more when surgery went wrong.


Where I would soften the cartoon is the word “designed.” I cannot prove anyone sat down and planned for patients to stay sick. But a system that pays more for sickness managed than for health restored will produce sickness, whatever its builders intended. No man can serve two masters, and our doctors are being asked to serve five or six.


The cartoon also leaves out the deepest string: the food. Most chronic disease begins at the table, and the processed-food, medical, and advertising industries all profit along the way, from the craving to its consequences.


The remedy is to put the patient’s hand back on the doctor’s paycheck and to take the other hands off. Let patients hold their own health dollars. Let doctors own their practices and hospitals again. Bring the price-setting committee into the daylight. Hold insurers liable when a denial harms a patient. End the drug-review agency’s dependence on the companies it reviews. Protect a doctor’s honest professional judgment from board discipline for dissent. Measure who actually heals. Stop spending public money on the food that makes people sick. And the doctor and the patient must both find the courage to act as free men.


*Provenance: Claude drafted this essay in my voice at my request on 1 November 2026, from Dr. Miller’s diagram and post and from my earlier writing on this site (“Critique of the Medical Establishment,” “Government Healthcare,” “Reforming Healthcare – The Anecdotal Database,” and “Reforming the Administrative State”). I added the section on diet and edited throughout. These positions are mine, and I’m open to a better argument.*


**Resource:** Larry J. Miller, MD, “Who Controls the Doctors – We have become puppets” (diagram © Larry J Miller MD 2026), posted to Facebook October 24, 2026.


## The picture and the question under it


Dr. Miller’s diagram puts a red-faced puppet master at the center of American medicine. His strings come from Big Pharma, insurance companies, malpractice attorneys, hospital administrators, specialty boards, corporate practice, medical schools, the media, the FDA, the CDC, Medicare, Medicaid, licensing boards, “Big Government,” and what he labels “Bogue’s Research” — I take it as a play on *bogus*. Two worried doctors hang from the strings, holding prescription pads. An old woman lies on the floor in a hospital gown. Green arrows carry “$8 Trillion” from her up to the controllers. The puppet master’s rules are listed in a red box: electronic medical records, monitoring of all action, protocols to follow, and punishment for independent thinking. The caption beneath reads that the system is designed to keep patients sick, because it would go out of business if they got well.


His post is gentler than the drawing. Most doctors, he writes, are not to blame; they have become puppets in a medical-government complex, and patients must wake up and take control.


The doctor who shared this cartoon added one line: *Not so for vitalist naturopathic doctors. But otherwise, yeah, sad but true.* This may be true for the vitalistic naturopathic doctors, meaning those who use only natural modalities (exercise, sunlight, air, wildcrafted or grown herbs, counseling, water, and food), but that is not the naturopathic medicine that most of us practice, which is dependent upon supplements and equipment. I return to that point below.


You can check the cartoon’s particulars one by one, and I do that below. But the question under the cartoon is simpler and more important: **When my doctor writes my prescription, whose servant is he?** Is he serving me, the one who is sick, or the institutions that pay him, license him, audit him, and can end his career? A patient senses the answer in the exam room even when he cannot name it. He feels it when the doctor looks at the screen instead of at him, when the treatment he needs is “not covered,” when he hears the same protocol recited to every patient, and when ten years of prescriptions have produced management but not healing. That sense is what the cartoon reaches for.


## My stand


I stand with Dr. Miller’s thesis: **the American physician is no longer chiefly the servant of his patient, and the strings that have replaced the patient’s authority are real, identifiable, and mostly financial.** The result is medicine paid to manage chronic disease rather than restore health.


The areas of uncertainty are these. The word “designed” claims an intent I cannot prove; the evidence shows incentives, not a plan. The diagram’s “$8 Trillion” is close to what federal actuaries project spending will reach in the early 2030s, not what we spend today. And each string pulls with a different force; Medicare’s price-setting moves far more than a malpractice attorney does.


But I qualify the qualification. A system does not need a conspirator to do evil. Scripture says the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil, and a root works underground and without a plan. If you pay a man more when his patient gets worse, and less when he spends an hour teaching the patient to get well, you will get more sick patients and fewer hours of teaching — in the aggregate, regardless of the goodness of the individual doctor. The design is in the incentives, whether or not anyone signed the blueprint.


## Tracing the strings


I have graded the evidence by its kind. Where a number comes from an interested party — a doctors’ association measuring the burden on doctors, a government agency measuring itself — I say so.


**The money.** The United States spent about $5.3 trillion on health care in 2024. The CMS actuaries project $8.6 trillion by 2033, rising to more than a fifth of the whole economy. The CDC reports that about 90 percent of that spending goes to people with chronic or mental-health conditions. So the cartoon’s arrows point the right way. The great river of medical money flows through ongoing illness, not through cure.


**The employer’s string.** As of January 1, 2026, 82 percent of practicing physicians were employed by hospitals or corporate entities, up from 52 percent at the start of 2018. Only 18 percent still practice in physician-owned settings. (Source: Avalere Health for the Physicians Advocacy Institute — a physician advocacy group, but counting a fact that is easily checked.) An employed doctor works to his employer’s schedule, referral network, protocols, and revenue targets. That is not wickedness; it is what employment means. But it means the patient is no longer the boss.


**The price-setter’s string.** Medicare pays physicians by a fee schedule, and private insurers largely key their prices to it. The RUC, a committee convened by the American Medical Association and its specialty societies, recommends the relative value of nearly every service. CMS has accepted more than 94 percent of its recommendations since 1991. Critics, including physicians who sued to end its role, point out that specialists have long dominated the committee. That is one reason a procedure pays far more than an hour spent understanding a patient’s life. A private committee of interested parties effectively prices American medicine, and the public cannot see its deliberations.


**The insurer’s string.** In the AMA’s 2025 survey, physicians reported about 40 prior-authorization requests per week, consuming about 13 hours of physician and staff time. Nearly four in five had seen patients abandon treatment because of these hurdles, and more than a quarter reported that prior authorization led to a serious adverse event — hospitalization, permanent harm, or death. The AMA is an interested party here, and these are self-reports. But the insurers’ own 2025 pledge to reduce the burden was a confession that the burden exists, and the AMA’s survey found the pledge had not yet delivered.


**The screen’s string.** The cartoon lists electronic records that “monitor all action.” In a study of more than 200,000 physicians led by AMA researchers, outpatient doctors averaged 5.8 hours in the electronic record for every 8 hours of scheduled patient time; primary-care doctors averaged 7.3. Much of that time is documentation for billing, quality metrics, and audit — the record serves the payer and the regulator at least as much as it serves the patient.


**The regulator’s string.** In fiscal year 2022, user fees paid by drug companies funded 66 percent of the FDA’s human-drugs program budget, according to the Department of Health and Human Services’ own analysis; one estimate put industry fees at about 80 percent of drug reviewers’ salaries in 2018. The government’s 2025 Make America Healthy Again report — an official document, though one written to make a case — states that 9 of 10 recent FDA commissioners went on to work for industry, and it uses the phrase “corporate capture of medical knowledge.” When the reviewed pays the reviewer, and the reviewer’s next job may be with the reviewed, the review’s independence depends on extraordinary personal virtue.


**The industry’s string.** Drug and device makers reported $13.18 billion in payments and ownership interests to physicians and teaching hospitals in 2024, across more than 16 million records in the federal Open Payments database. Disclosure is good. But disclosure tells us the string exists; it does not cut it.


**The licensing board’s string.** “Punish doctors for independent thinking” sounds like exaggeration until one remembers California’s AB 2098, signed in 2022, which made it unprofessional conduct for a physician to spread COVID-19 “misinformation,” defined against the “contemporary scientific consensus.” In January 2023 a federal judge blocked it as unconstitutionally vague (*Høeg v. Newsom*), and the legislature repealed it later that year. The law is gone, but its passage showed that the string runs where the cartoon says it does. A board that can take a man’s livelihood for departing from consensus has power over his conscience.


**The perverse incentive, measured.** The most damning evidence is not a survey but a peer-reviewed accounting study. In 2013, *JAMA* published a study by Sunil Eappen and colleagues, with Atul Gawande as senior author, of surgical patients in a hospital system. Patients who suffered a complication generated a hospital contribution margin 330 percent higher when privately insured and 190 percent higher under Medicare than patients without a complication. The authors concluded that our system perversely penalizes reducing harm. That is the cartoon’s caption — “would go out of business if patients got well” — stated by Harvard physicians in one of the country’s leading medical journals.


**The cost to the puppet and to the trust.** By the AMA’s measure, physician burnout peaked during the pandemic and fell to about 42 percent in 2025 — better, but still two doctors in five. And a 50-state survey published in *JAMA Network Open* found that the share of American adults who trusted physicians and hospitals fell from 71.5 percent in April 2020 to 40.1 percent in January 2024. Patients feel the strings even when they cannot see them.


## Where the cartoon overreaches, and what it misses


First, the naturopathic exception. Naturopathic physicians who practice for cash, outside insurance, are indeed free of most of these strings: no prior authorization, no RUC, no employer’s protocol, little EHR auditing for billing. That freedom is real, and I have lived it for thirty years. But we are not string-free. We are licensed by state boards, and in many states we are not licensed at all, so we practice at legal risk. And because most of us depend on supplements and equipment, we have strings of our own: the temptation to sell supplements from our own shelves is a conflict of interest of exactly the kind I criticize in the drug-company payment. A vitalist is not made righteous by his philosophy. He is made righteous by serving his patient over his profit, which is the same test every doctor faces.


Second, the puppet image excuses too much. Dr. Miller is generous to say most doctors are not to blame, and I agree that the system, not the man, is the main cause. But a doctor is not wood and string. He is a moral agent who took an oath. In my 2023 summary of the conversation between Drs. Laibow and Mihalcea, I noted their conviction that doctors must find the courage to disobey demands that harm patients. I still hold it. The strings explain the pressure; they do not abolish the conscience.


Third, the cartoon leaves out the patient’s own string. Patients who want a pill rather than a change of life pull on the doctor too. Dr. Miller’s closing line — “Don’t be a victim, be a victor” — is the right correction, and it belongs in the solution.


## The string the cartoon leaves out: the fork


As a naturopathic physician, I stand outside the hospital-insurance system to some extent. The vitalistic ND attempts to restore health through diet and natural means. I have written more extensively on this on my medical site, [naturedox.com](https://www.naturedox.com/), but in short, I believe most chronic disease is diet-induced. Better access to doctors will not solve the problem of diet causing illness. That will change only with a paradigm shift.


My naturopathic mentor, Dr. Norman Easley, once said, “Getting people to change their diet is like getting someone to change their religion.” So I don’t offer this solution as easy. For the case in full, see *Eat to Live* by Dr. Joel Fuhrman and *How Not to Die* by Dr. Michael Greger. Dietary treatment is inexpensive, but it requires willpower, and many believe it is too difficult, or that life is not worth living without the pleasure of eating the foods they desire.


This is where diet intersects with the cartoon. Entire food-preparation industries profit by making foods craveable and convenient, and that is not merely my impression. A 2023 study in the journal *Addiction* used the tobacco industry’s own documents to show that from 1988 to 2001, food brands owned by tobacco companies were 29 percent more likely to be fat-and-sodium “hyperpalatable” and 80 percent more likely to be carbohydrate-and-sodium hyperpalatable than other foods. The same researchers estimate that about two-thirds of the American food supply is now hyperpalatable. In a 2019 NIH inpatient study, people offered an ultra-processed diet ate about 500 more calories a day than when offered unprocessed food, without being told to eat more.


So, in the big picture, three large industries feed on the same weakness. The processed-food industry profits from the craving. The medical industry profits from its consequences: the pain, disability, and early death. The advertising and media industry profits from selling both. None of them needs to plan the harm for the harm to pay. An addiction to the pleasures of the flesh is not easily overcome, and its cure requires a change of heart, which requires a change of mind.


## Why the concern remains plausible


Even where a particular link cannot be proven, the overall concern stands on four supports.


The** first** is the money motive, which is documented rather than imagined: a system that earns more from complications than from clean outcomes, and that spends nine of every ten dollars on people living with chronic illness.


The **second** is the link between law and behavior. Section 6001 of the Affordable Care Act still bars new physician-owned hospitals from Medicare and freezes existing ones at their 2010 size; hospital associations oppose its repeal. State certificate-of-need laws require new competitors to ask permission from boards that incumbents often influence. These laws protect institutions from the doctors who would compete with them, and the 82 percent employment figure is their fruit.


The **third** is the revolving door, the user fee, and the price committee — arrangements in which the regulated help pay for, staff, or advise the regulator.


The fourth is the intuitive sense of unnaturalness that every patient knows. A man is not well because his numbers are managed. He is well when his body has recovered its order. A medicine that rarely uses the word *cure* for chronic disease has lost sight of what the patient came for.


## The moral ground


My policy test has three questions, in order. Is it righteous? If so, is it loving? Would I want it done to me?


Jesus said that no man can serve two masters, for he will hate the one and love the other, or hold to the one and despise the other. That is the moral heart of this problem. The healer’s duty is to the one he heals; this is the covenant underneath the Hippocratic oath and every honest physician’s conscience. When we construct a system in which the doctor’s livelihood depends on serving the payer, the employer, the regulator, and the vendor, we have not merely made medicine inefficient. We have built a structure that tempts a good man to betray his patient a little every day. That is not righteous.


Is it loving? No. The patient who abandons treatment because of a denial, the patient whose complication improves the hospital’s margin, the patient who receives a protocol instead of a physician’s judgment — none of them feels loved.


Would I want it done to me? I would not want to lie on that floor in a hospital gown while the strings were pulled above me. I would want a doctor who answers to me, knows me, is free to tell me the truth, and is paid more when I get well.


The laborer is worthy of his hire — and the hire should come from the hand he serves.


## The solution: put the patient’s hand back on the paycheck


The principle is simple: **the doctor will serve whoever pays him, licenses him, and judges him. So let the patient pay him, let the law license him narrowly, and let a jury of his neighbors, not a captured board, judge him.** Every proposal below follows from that. Some fall to the President and the federal agencies; some require Congress; some belong to the states, where the President’s role is to lead by voice.


**1. Patient-held health dollars.** Since January 2026, direct primary care memberships of up to $150 a month for an individual and $300 for a family are compatible with health savings accounts. That is a good first step. I would expand HSAs to every American, lift the cap on direct-care memberships, and let Medicare and Medicaid beneficiaries direct a defined portion of their benefit into an account they control, paired with catastrophic coverage for the rare large expense. Direct primary care already shows the model: thousands of practices, an average fee near $100 a month, panels a fraction of the size of an insurance-based practice, and a doctor whose only boss is the patient. When the patient holds the money, the doctor looks up from the screen.


**2. Let doctors own their work again.** Repeal Section 6001’s ban on physician-owned hospitals. Urge the states to repeal certificate-of-need laws. Pay the same for the same service regardless of whether a hospital owns the building (“site-neutral payment”), so that buying up practices is no longer a pricing strategy. Independent practice should be a real choice, not a nostalgic one.


**3. Bring price-setting into the light.** As long as Medicare sets prices, the valuation of medical services must be public, recorded, and balanced, with primary care and patients represented, and CMS should treat any single private committee as one voice among many rather than as an oracle. Long term, patient-held dollars and posted cash prices will set honest prices better than any committee.


**4. Make insurers accountable for their denials.** If an insurer overrides a physician’s order, it is practicing medicine, and it should bear a physician’s liability for harm. Require that denials of medically necessary care be signed by a licensed physician in the same specialty, decided within fixed deadlines, and exempt doctors with good approval records from routine prior authorization altogether (“gold-carding”). Accountability belongs to whoever makes the decision.


**5. End the FDA’s dependence on the companies it reviews.** Fund drug review by appropriation, not industry fees; or, consistent with my administrative-state proposals, allow competing independent certifiers who carry legal liability for negligent approval and are subject to random public audit. Impose a long cooling-off period before any commissioner or senior reviewer may work for a company he regulated. The reviewer should owe nothing to the reviewed.


**6. Protect the doctor’s conscience.** No licensing board should discipline a physician for honest professional opinion that departs from consensus, absent proof of fraud or actual patient harm. Any license revocation should be appealable to a jury, which is the citizen’s ancient protection against captured authority. And patients should be free to choose any licensed or traditional practitioner, with full disclosure, for their own care. Health freedom is the patient’s freedom first.


**7. Give the record back to the patient.** The medical record belongs to the patient. Strip federal documentation requirements down to what clinical care actually needs, so that the doctor’s hours go to the person rather than the audit.


**8. Measure who actually heals.** Our research system funds what can be patented, and so it rarely tests what cannot. I have proposed an open national Anecdotal Database in which every practitioner and patient can record conditions, treatments, and outcomes — verified and searchable — so what works, conventional or natural, becomes visible to everyone. That breaks the monopoly on medical knowledge without censoring anyone; it answers “bogus research” with more data, not less. Diet, which no one can patent, is exactly the kind of treatment such a database would bring into view.


**9. Pay for health, not for sickness.** Where public money pays for care, stop paying extra for preventable complications, and reward clinicians whose patients actually recover: diabetes reversed, weight regained toward health, medications reduced rather than added.


**10. Stop paying for the food that makes us sick.** I would not ban any food; a free man may eat what he chooses. But the public should not pay for his poison. At least 18 states have already received federal waivers to keep soda and candy out of SNAP purchases; I would make that the national rule, and apply the same test to school meals and to the farm subsidies that make processed ingredients artificially cheap. Require honest front-of-package labeling of ultra-processed food, so the buyer knows what he is eating. And the President should use his voice to teach the nation what the naturopathic tradition has long held: the first prescription is a change of diet.


**11. Restore the character of both parties.** No law can make a doctor brave or a patient responsible. Doctors must remember the oath and accept the cost of keeping it. Patients must take charge of their own health — what they eat, how they move, what they believe, how they live — and stop demanding a pill for every consequence of how they choose to live. The church and the family belong in the healing ministry too. Dr. Miller is right: be a victor, not a victim.


## Objections


*The poor cannot afford to pay their own doctors.* Then let public help come as money the poor control, not as permission they must beg for, and let the church and community do what they once did. A poor man with an account and a free doctor is better served than a poor man with a card and a captive one.


*Employed doctors enjoy stability and fewer business burdens.* True, and no one should be forbidden to take a salary. The goal is not to abolish employment but to end the laws and payment rules that make independence nearly impossible, so that employment is chosen, not forced.


*Prior authorization prevents waste.* Some of it does. That is why the remedy is accountability for the one who denies, not abolishing judgment. A payer who is confident in his denial should not fear owning it.


*Without industry fees, the FDA would be slow.* Then fund it honestly, or let independent certifiers compete. Speed bought from the reviewed is not a bargain the patient chose.


*Restricting SNAP purchases is paternalism toward the poor.* No one is forbidden to buy soda with his own money. The question is only whether his neighbor should be taxed to buy it for him, and then taxed again to treat the disease it causes.


I hold these positions as my best judgment for now. If someone shows me a better way to put the patient’s hand back on the doctor’s paycheck, I will take it.


## Sources and their limits


This essay leans on the mainstream record: federal agency data (CMS, CDC, HHS/ASPE, Open Payments, USDA), peer-reviewed journals (*JAMA*, *JAMA Network Open*, *Journal of General Internal Medicine*, *Addiction*, *Cell Metabolism*), and the surveys of physician organizations (AMA, Physicians Advocacy Institute). Each has limits. The AMA and PAI are advocates for physicians and measure burdens on physicians; their numbers are self-reports. Federal agencies measure their own programs. The MAHA report is an official document written to make a case, and its critics dispute parts of it. The Eappen study examined one hospital system in one period; it shows how the incentive works, not that every hospital profits from every complication. The NIH diet study was small (20 adults over four weeks); it shows the mechanism, not the effect size across a nation. My belief that most chronic disease is diet-induced rests on clinical experience and on the dietary literature summarized by Drs. Fuhrman and Greger, not on any single trial. And what the mainstream record leaves unmeasured — the effectiveness of unpatentable therapies like diet, the private reasons a doctor stays silent, the patients who quietly give up — is exactly where curation could hide evidence. Dr. Miller’s sixty years of practice and my thirty are firsthand reports. They are not proof, but they are leads worth following, and they point the same direction as the data.
