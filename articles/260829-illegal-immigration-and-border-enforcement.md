# Enforce the Law. Count Honestly. Love the Stranger.

### A presidential position paper on immigration

Thomas Lee Abshier, ND — 29 August 2026

*Occasioned by Tucker Carlson's interview with Greg Bovino, former chief of the U.S. Border Patrol, released this week. Mr. Bovino served thirty years, led the interior enforcement operations of 2025, and left the government under circumstances he describes as retaliation. He deserves a hearing, and he deserves a serious response.*

---

## PART ONE: The number, and why my argument does not rest on it

Mr. Bovino states that 107 million illegal aliens now live in the United States, roughly a third of the population, and Mr. Carlson accepts the figure on the strength of his thirty years.

I think that number is probably too high, b**ut the size of the number is not material to my argument.** Whether the figure is 12 million or 40 million, every proposition in this paper holds. If the number is at the low end, the policies below are achievable and overdue. If it is at the high end, they are urgent. Nothing I propose depends on winning the arithmetic, and I decline to let the debate divert into a fight over a statistic no one can settle, while the underlying breach goes unaddressed for another decade. That fight is convenient for the people who prefer the subject changed.

The policy is not in dispute. Successive administrations of both parties enacted and tolerated arrangements that admitted very large numbers of people outside the law. The motives I cannot prove from outside the walls of power, and neither can anyone else. The possibilities range from the mundane to the sinister: industry pressure for cheap labor; a genuine but unexamined belief that America has too much and owes the world the difference; administrative collapse under volume, with asylum law and court rulings producing releases nobody chose; the calculation that a future electorate was being assembled; or, at the worst end, officials who traded the nation's boundary for money or power. Ordinary institutional incompetence explains a great deal in Washington and should always be considered first. But incompetence sustained for twenty years, always failing in the same direction, always to the benefit of the same interests, stops looking accidental — and no one in authority has yet offered an explanation that accounts for the pattern.

One thing I know with certainty: a nation is defined by its boundaries. When the boundary fails, the nation's character changes at the least, and its sovereignty ends at the worst.

---

## PART TWO: What is true, and why it matters

The grievance is real, and most of official Washington still refuses to say so.

**Laws duly passed are not being enforced.** The Immigration and Nationality Act is not an agency regulation. It is a statute, enacted by the people's representatives. When an administration decides which statutes it will enforce and which it will quietly suspend, it has claimed a power no executive possesses — the power to repeal by inaction. This is true no matter which party does it, and both have. It is the same disease I described in "Legality Is Not Righteousness": the executive treating law as an instrument of his will rather than as his master.

**The wage argument is serious and has an honorable pedigree.** It is not a nativist invention. Organized labor made it for decades; the restriction of the 1920s was pressed in part by unions to tighten the labor market; and in the 1990s the Commission on Immigration Reform, chaired by Barbara Jordan — a black Democratic congresswoman and civil rights icon — concluded that credibility requires deporting those who should be deported, and recommended *reducing* legal immigration levels to protect low-wage American workers. When the labor supply expands faster than the economy absorbs it, the people who lose are the Americans at the bottom, disproportionately black and Hispanic citizens. Anyone who calls this argument racist has not read its history.

**The costs were imposed on people who were never asked.** The benefits of cheap labor accrue to employers and shareholders. The costs land on the schools, emergency rooms, housing markets, and neighborhoods of working people in Springfield, Charlotte, and Yakima — communities with no lobby and no say. Mr. Carlson's observation stands: the zip codes where the decision-makers live are not the zip codes absorbing the change. That is government without consent, and it is a moral offense independent of any question about immigrants themselves.

**Selective enforcement corrodes law for everyone.** Why should an ordinary citizen scrupulously obey a thicket of rules he needs a lawyer to understand, while a category of people is exempted from the most basic requirements by executive discretion? A legal system that is rigorous with the compliant and lax with the defiant has planted the seeds of rebellion. A government used as a tool against the righteous will not long prosper.

I take these seriously. Here is what I would do.

---

## PART THREE: The position

### 1. Count honestly, in public, every quarter

You cannot govern what you refuse to measure, and the vacuum of trustworthy official data is precisely why the population figure has become a shouting match rather than a fact. I would direct a standing, transparent accounting of the unauthorized population and its components — border entries, visa overstays, removals, returns, self-departures — published quarterly, with the methodology stated in full and the model open to outside audit by parties who disagree with each other. If the honest number is 12 million, say 12 million. If it is 40 million, say 40 million. If enforcement is failing, the numbers will say so, and no press office will be able to gaslight anyone in either direction.

The first duty of an honest government is to tell the truth about the size of the problem. The second is to publish how it arrived at the figure, so that citizens can check the work rather than being asked to trust the institution.
### 2. Enforce against employers first

**No one crosses a desert for a job that does not exist, a benefit that will not be paid, or an enforcement regime that will actually remove him.** The magnet is employment, the employment is knowingly illegal, and the employer is an American citizen or corporation with an address, a bank account, and something to lose. Yet enforcement has almost always fallen on the migrant and almost never on the man who hired him, because the migrant has no lobby and the employer writes checks to campaigns.

I would make E-Verify universal and mandatory, and pair it with real criminal liability — not settlements, not fines absorbed as a cost of doing business, but personal prosecution of the officers and owners who knowingly staff their operations with illegal labor, with escalating penalties and debarment from federal contracts. Apply the same principle I applied to the AI companies: **accountability that lands on a nameable person.**

Do this and the problem contracts without a single mass roundup, because the reason to be here evaporates. Refuse to do it, and no amount of enforcement against the powerless will ever work, because you will be bailing a boat while your donors drill the holes. Every administration that has promised enforcement while sparing employers has run a confidence game, and the country should stop being fooled.
### 3. Secure the border and keep it secured

Physical barriers, sensors, and manpower where each is appropriate — the right mix is an engineering question, not an ideological one. Mr. Bovino is right that a secure line means little without interior enforcement, because the incentive to attempt the crossing is set by what happens after a successful one. Border and interior are one system and must be funded as one.
### 4. Fix asylum by fixing adjudication

The asylum system is broken not because the standard is wrong but because the queue is measured in years, which converts a legal process into a de facto grant of residence. Fund immigration courts until a claim is heard in weeks. Detain or closely supervise during that window. Grant genuine claims generously and promptly; deny false ones promptly and remove. Speed *is* the reform. A system that decides quickly is both more humane and more enforceable than one that decides eventually.
### 5. Removal priorities, stated plainly

Criminal aliens first. Triage the threat and remove the violent immediately, because a citizen's physical safety outranks every other consideration and protection is the government's first duty. But that cannot be the whole program, because a policy that removes only felons announces to the world that everyone else may stay. So: criminals, then recent entrants, then those under final orders of removal who have absconded, and then the remainder of those present unlawfully. Either the law is enforced, or it is not a law. Published priorities, applied consistently, so that everyone — including those weighing the crossing — knows the rules in advance.
### 6. No amnesty before enforcement, and no permanent underclass either

In 1986 the country accepted a bargain: amnesty now, enforcement later. The amnesty was delivered, and the enforcement never came, which is why no restrictionist has trusted a word since, and why the entire debate has been frozen for forty years. Any future consideration of status must therefore be sequenced in the opposite order: **enforcement first, verified by the honest quarterly numbers above, and sustained for years.** Then, and only then, may Congress debate what to do about long-resident, non-criminal people whose children have grown up here.

Two hard questions must be faced, not deferred.

**Birthright citizenship.** I hold that the Fourteenth Amendment's "subject to the jurisdiction thereof" was never intended to confer citizenship on the children of those present unlawfully or here as visitors, and that birth tourism is an abuse of the nation's hospitality that any self-respecting country would end. I also state plainly what changing it requires: the Supreme Court held otherwise in *United States v. Wong Kim Ark* in 1898, and that holding has stood for more than a century. It can be revisited by the Court or settled by constitutional amendment. It cannot be undone by executive order. I would pursue the amendment and welcome the litigation, prospectively — I do not propose to strip citizenship retroactively from Americans who have held it their whole lives.

**The children already here.** Roughly five million children live with an unauthorized parent. The courts have ruled that they are citizens. This ruling must be revisited. Refusing to confront this is how we got what we now have: a permanent shadow population of millions who can be exploited precisely because they cannot go to the law. **A caste living outside the protection of the law is intolerable in a Christian nation. It is also exactly what cheap-labor employers want, which is why it persists.**
### 7. Set legal immigration levels deliberately, and assimilate in earnest

Congress, not the executive and not the courts, should set annual levels by a stated formula tied to labor-market conditions and assimilation capacity, reviewed on a fixed cycle. Levels are a legitimate policy question, not a moral test, and the right answer in 1924 differs from the right answer in 2026. Selection should weigh skills, English, and self-sufficiency.

And whoever is admitted should be *made an American*. Not merely processed — formed. Fluency in English, American history and civics taught without apology, and an oath of allegiance that means what it says: exclusive political loyalty to this country. Newcomers should be taught the moral inheritance this nation was built on, which is unmistakably Judeo-Christian, and should be expected to live within it — to honor the Sabbath of their neighbors, respect the equal dignity of women, submit disputes to our courts rather than to any rival authority, and raise no claim of a separate law within our borders.

Let me be precise about the limit here, because the distinction matters and I will not be vague about it. **I would require allegiance and conduct. I would not require profession of faith.** No man should be barred from entry, from commerce, or from public office for his religion — Article VI forbids a religious test for office. What I would refuse admission to is not a creed but a conduct: anyone who intends to live under a competing legal authority, who holds that the laws of this republic do not bind him, or who comes with hostility to the nation itself. That is not a test of the heart. It is a test of allegiance, and every nation on earth applies one.

A country that admits people and asks nothing of them has not been generous. It has been indifferent, and indifference produces exactly the fragmentation that separates men from hospitality.
### 8. Two things I will not do

**I will not deploy the military for domestic civil immigration enforcement.** The Insurrection Act exists for insurrection. Posse Comitatus exists because a free people does not police itself with soldiers, and because any power you create will be inherited by the administration you fear most. This is the same test I applied to surveillance: if you would not hand an authority to your enemy, do not hand it to your friend. If civil enforcement lacks manpower, fund and staff civil enforcement. That is a budget problem, and budget problems are solvable without amending the American constitutional order.

**I will not permit masked, unidentifiable federal agents on American streets.** An officer enforcing the people's laws draws his legitimacy from those laws and should be willing to be seen doing it. Visible identification and badge numbers, always. And in the same breath: doxxing an officer, publishing his family's address, or threatening his children is a federal crime and should be prosecuted as one, vigorously, along with any organization funding it. Officers hide their faces because we failed to protect them. Fix the failure rather than normalizing the mask.

**And on the honesty of political promises:** if a candidate campaigns on enforcement and then governs otherwise, the voters are owed an explanation from him, not from his critics. I make no promise here that I am not prepared to be held to. If elected, I invite anyone to hold me to this document. Until then, I remain open to being persuaded otherwise.

---

## PART FOUR: What Scripture actually requires

I would be a poor Christian candidate if I gave you the policy and skipped the Book. Scripture affirms both halves of this question.

**Nations and borders are real and legitimate.** God "hath made of one blood all nations of men… and hath determined the times before appointed, and the bounds of their habitation" (Acts 17:26). Nations are His idea, not a fallen invention. And the civil magistrate bears the sword to restrain evil and enforce law (Romans 13). A government that will not enforce its own statutes is failing a duty God assigned it. Open borders are not a biblical position; they are an abdication of one.

**And the sojourner is under God's particular protection.** "The stranger that dwelleth with you shall be unto you as one born among you, and thou shalt love him as thyself; for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt" (Leviticus 19:34). "He doth execute the judgment of the fatherless and widow, and loveth the stranger… Love ye therefore the stranger" (Deuteronomy 10:18-19). These are not suggestions, and they are not confined to those with papers. God repeatedly ties His own reputation to the treatment of the vulnerable outsider, and He does it in the very books that establish Israel's borders and laws.

Both are true. And **America has failed both commands at once.**

We did not enforce the law. We built a system that quietly beckoned millions to come and break it, so that employers could pay them beneath what an American would accept and hold their status over them. We took the labor, pocketed the savings, and let the costs fall on working communities that were never consulted. Then we blamed the people who came for coming. That is not conservatism, and it is not compassion; it is the worst of both, and the guilt belongs to the whole country and not to one party.

And we did not love the stranger, because our government made it impossible to know who the stranger was. This is the part rarely said aloud. **A high-trust society runs on the presumption of good character, and that presumption is a public good that the government is charged with protecting.** When admission is granted without vetting allegiance, without any process of formation, and without the honest expectation that a newcomer will become one of us, the ordinary citizen loses his ability to extend trust — not from malice, but because he has been given no basis for it. He is forced to hold his neighbor at arm's length. The men who dismantled the vetting did not merely admit people; they destroyed the condition under which those people could have been welcomed. That is a real injury, and it was inflicted on the immigrant as much as on the citizen.

I want to distinguish two things that are constantly confused in this debate, because the confusion generates most of the heat.

**Love is owed to every man. Trust is earned by every man.** Every person on this soil is an image-bearer of God, and I am commanded to love him — to deal justly with him, to feed him if he is hungry, to speak to him as a man and not as a category, and to remember that Christ died for him. None of that is contingent on his papers. But love does not require me to hand a stranger my keys, my daughter's safety, or my nation's franchise. To trust without verification is not charity; it is the abdication of a stewardship God gave me. A father who cannot tell the difference is not loving his household. He is neglecting it.

So the remedy is not to choose one command over the other. It is to keep both: **a border that is real, a law enforced against the powerful first and then against everyone, an entry gate that actually functions, and a people who treat every soul within their reach as someone Christ died for — including the ones we are lawfully sending home.**

You can deport a man and still speak to him as a man. You can enforce a law and still feed a hungry child.
## The close

I do not think this is the end of America, though it could be. If we cannot say what an American is, we have no identity left to defend, and a nation that cannot define its membership has already dissolved in everything but name.

So the questions before us are these. Do we have the will to say publicly that this nation was founded on Christian principles and to live as though we believed it? Do we understand that the First Amendment forbade Congress to establish a national church — not to acknowledge God, not to reason from Scripture in the public square, and not to teach our children the moral inheritance that produced our liberties? Do we have the wisdom to see the difference between loving a stranger and entrusting him with the country, and to stop pretending that refusing the second is a failure of the first?

We were told, correctly, that a nation without borders is not a nation. We are also told that we lack mercy toward the alien. Here is my perspective, and it begins by refusing a confusion both sides keep making. **The state is not the church, and the magistrate is not the Redeemer.** Christ's office was to bear the penalty of sin in Himself. The magistrate's office is the reverse: God gave him the sword expressly to punish the evildoer and shield the innocent (Romans 13:4). A government that declines to enforce its own laws is not imitating Christ's mercy. It is deserting the post God assigned it, and the cost of that desertion is paid by people who never consented to it — the citizen underbid at work, and the immigrant himself, kept in a shadow where no law will protect him.

So when I am told to be merciful as God is merciful, I answer that I am not God. I cannot bear another man's penalty, and I hold my office rather than His. Mercy is not mine to grant in the eternal sense, and a ruler who pretends otherwise has confused the two kingdoms and will govern badly in both.

What mercy does require of a nation is this: that the man who entered unlawfully be dealt with as a man. Patiently. Humanely. With honesty that our own employers and our own appetite for cheap labor called him here, and that we are not innocent parties administering justice to a guilty one. And then, having treated him so, that we require his entry be regularized through the lawful gate — or that he return and come in through it. That mercy is owed him because he is a man, not because he has earned it. It governs how we treat him. It does not repeal the door.

The problem will not be solved by pretending the laws we already adopted need not be observed. We are either a nation of laws or we are subject to the caprice of whichever man holds the pen. And if we have an illegal immigration problem, we should size it honestly — neither minimizing it to protect an administration nor inflating it to win an argument. **We cannot repair what we refuse to see.**

Count honestly. Enforce the law at the top of the ladder and then at the bottom. Secure the border and mean it. Decide asylum cases in weeks. Ask newcomers to become Americans in more than name. Keep soldiers out of civil policing and masks off of federal officers. And treat every soul involved — the border agent, the working family in Charlotte, the Guatemalan man at the Home Depot, the citizen child whose mother has no papers — as someone Christ died for.
