# Truth in Proportion: September 11 at Twenty-Five


Oct 14, 2026 · Thomas Abshier, ND


## Plain language summary


Jeremy Boreing marked the twenty-fifth anniversary of September 11 with a long speech making three claims. First, we know who did it: nineteen al-Qaeda hijackers under Osama bin Laden, and the theories blaming the CIA or Israel are false. Second, the “forever war” did not begin in 2001; militant Islam has made war on Christendom and the West for 1,400 years, and on America since 1785, before we had done anything to provoke it. Third, the West is losing because it no longer believes its own cause is good, and a people with no cause will lose to one that has one.


I agree entirely with the first claim, largely with the second, and with the third at its root. But the speech is built of true facts arranged in a way that leaves out things a Christian must say. The institutions that lied about COVID also told us Iraq had weapons it did not have; that lie is part of why a quarter of the country now believes the government did 9/11. The just-war tradition Boreing invokes judges not only the cause but also the conduct, so the number of civilians killed is not the whole measure of morality, but it is not irrelevant either. The phrase “forever war” is wrong as history, but right as a complaint that Congress handed its war power to the President in 2001 and never took it back. And the West has lost not freedom first; it has lost righteousness, from which freedom grows.


My positions: tell the truth about September 11 and the lack of connection to Iraq; restore trust by having institutions confess their own lies, including Iraq, COVID lab leaks, vaccination side effects, and the SSTF; openly publish and acknowledge Islam’s doctrine of conquest; judge each war: does it meet the standards of a Just Cause and righteous conduct; require Congress to declare or authorize each war against a named enemy with an expiration date; and give the young a cause worth living for, which is an expansion of the Kingdom of Heaven, not merely struggle.


*Provenance: Claude drafted this essay in my voice at my request on 14 October 2026, responding to Jeremy Boreing’s 9/11 anniversary speech. The positions stated are my own and are open to a better argument.*


---


## 1. Why I am answering this speech


Mr. Boreing’s speech largely articulates the points that reflect my perspective.


I held the same standard in my September paper on the Ukraine war: a statement can be entirely true and still deceive if it presents part of the truth as the whole.


## 2. The moral ground on which this paper stands


I judge any act of a nation in the public square by three questions, asked in order:


- **Is it righteous?** Is it what God establishes as just, judged by His law rather than our preference? “*Righteousness exalteth a nation: but sin is a reproach to any people”* **(Proverbs 14:34)**.

- **If it is righteous, is it loving?** Does it seek the true good of every neighbor it touches, including the enemy? *“Love your enemies”* **(Matthew 5:44)** does not forbid the magistrate from using the sword **(Romans 13:4)**, but it governs how the sword is used.

- **Would I want it done to me?** If I stood where the person on the receiving end stands, committed to righteousness and seeing with eternal eyes rather than momentary appetite, would I accept this act as just? This is *“thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself”* (**Leviticus 19:18)** made concrete.


Two further principles apply directly to this subject. The ninth commandment, *“Thou shalt not bear false witness against thy neighbour”* **(Exodus 20:16)**, binds a nation’s speech as it binds a man’s. And truth must be kept in proportion: emphasizing one true cause until it hides the others is a form of false witness.


## 3. What we know about September 11


On the central fact, Mr. Boreing is right, and I want to say it without qualification. Nineteen men, members of al-Qaeda, acting under the direction of Osama bin Laden and the planning of Khalid Sheikh Mohammed, hijacked four airliners and killed 2,977 people. The 9/11 Commission traced their entry into the country, flight training, funding, and movements. Bin Laden claimed the attack on video. Khalid Sheikh Mohammed claimed it in a military courtroom. The hijackers recorded martyrdom videos before they left Afghanistan. The passengers called their families from the planes.


To believe the attack was an inside job, a man must believe that thousands of people across five administrations of both parties, including firefighters, FBI agents, medical examiners, structural engineers, and the victims’ own families, have kept a perfect secret for twenty-five years without a single credible leak or deathbed confession, and that the men who planned the attack, celebrated it, and died for it were lying about having done it.


The conspiracy method Mr. Boreing describes is real. A stream of small questions (the passport, the put options, Building 7, the “dancing Israelis”) is raised faster than any ordinary person can look up the answers. The questioner does not need you to believe him. He needs you to stop believing anyone. Each of those questions has a documented answer in the public record, and the questioner rarely mentions that.


This is not a small error of opinion. To accuse Israel or the Jewish people of murdering 3,000 Americans, against the evidence, is to bear false witness against a neighbor, and the charge falls on people who are innocent of it. It also dishonors the dead by taking from them the truth of how and why they died. A Christian may not repeat such a charge because it feels like courage against the powerful. False courage is not courage.


## 4. Why a quarter of the country doubts


Mr. Boreing gives the right diagnosis of why so many now disbelieve the official account: the institutions charged with telling the truth lied about other things, and people reasoned, not unreasonably, that they might be lying about this too. He names the lies about masks, the virus’s origin, and the riots of 2020. On that much he is right. Trust in the federal government stood near three-quarters of the public in the early 1960s, and now stands under one in five.


But he omits the lie most directly tied to September 11 itself. The case for invading Iraq in 2003 rested on the claim that Saddam Hussein held weapons of mass destruction and had operational ties to al-Qaeda. The weapons were not found. The bipartisan Senate Intelligence Committee report of 2004 and the Robb-Silberman Commission of 2005 both concluded that the intelligence was badly wrong and that the government presented it to the public with far more certainty than it deserved. Whether one calls that a lie or a catastrophic failure of judgment, the American people were told something false by their government in order to take them to war, and a generation of young men went to fight on that basis.


When Mr. Boreing reports that over half of veterans say the Iraq war was a mistake or built on a lie, he places that finding among the falsehoods Americans have been fed. It is not a falsehood. It is a reasonable reading of what the government’s own investigations found. To dismiss it is to make the same error in the other direction: to defend the honor of a just war by refusing to admit the faults in a different and doubtful one.


You do not restore trust by demanding it. It is restored by confession. *“He that covereth his sins shall not prosper: but whoso confesseth and forsaketh them shall have mercy”* **(Proverbs 28:13)**. An institution that admits its lies about Iraq and COVID will be believed about September 11. One that admits nothing will keep losing ground to the man selling doubt.


## 5. The long war


On history, Mr. Boreing is substantially right, and the part most Americans don’t know is the most important.


In 1785, before the Constitution, Algerian corsairs captured American merchant ships and sold their crews as slaves. In March 1786, Thomas Jefferson and John Adams met with Tripoli’s ambassador in London and asked what America had done to provoke this. They reported his answer to John Jay: that it was written in the Qur’an that nations who did not acknowledge Islam’s authority were sinners, that it was the right and duty of Muslims to make war on them and enslave those taken prisoner, and that a Muslim slain in such battle was sure of paradise. America had no navy, no troops abroad, and no ally in the region. The war was not a reaction to American policy. It was declared on theological grounds.


America then paid tribute, and the price rose every year, because paying for peace teaches the extortioner that extortion works. Jefferson, who disliked standing armies, finally sent the Navy. The pattern recurs: when America answered the 1983 Beirut barracks bombing by withdrawing, the 1993 World Trade Center bombing as a criminal case, and the 1998 embassy bombings with cruise missiles into empty camps, bin Laden concluded that America was a paper tiger. He said so, and he declared war in writing twice, in 1996 and 1998. Weakness did not buy peace. It invited the attack.


The broader history, from the Arab conquest of Christian North Africa and Spain to the sieges of Constantinople and Vienna, is also substantially accurate, and Mr. Boreing is right that the Crusades were a counterattack against prior conquest, not an unprovoked assault. (A few of his dates and figures need correction; see the editorial notes.)


Here is where proportion requires more than the speech gives. Mr. Boreing does say that America has Muslim allies and roughly ten thousand Muslims serving in its military. But the speech’s overall shape presents “Islam and the West” as the two parties of a single 1,400-year war. A Christian must keep two truths together. The doctrine of conquest that the ambassador of Tripoli recited, and that bin Laden preached, is real, has deep roots in Islamic texts and history, and is to be opposed without apology. And the Muslim man living next door is my neighbor, made in the image of God, whom Christ commands me to love, and to whom I owe the truth of the Gospel offered freely, not a presumption of guilt. I wrote in 2019 that our defense against militant Islam is a culture-wide commitment to the principles of Christ, from which we share the love and freedom found in Jesus with our Muslim fellow citizens. I hold that still.


The distinction is practical, not sentimental. A nation that cannot tell the jihadist from the Muslim neighbor will either surrender to the first in the name of tolerance or persecute the second in the name of security. Both are failures of righteousness.


## 6. Body count and the just cause


Mr. Boreing’s sharpest argument is against Tucker Carlson’s claim that America’s response to September 11 killed enough civilians to equal dozens of September 11s. He says this measures morality by body count, which would make America the villain of the Second World War, since it killed far more Japanese than Japan killed at Pearl Harbor. He says morality in war depends on whose cause is just.


He is right that body count alone cannot decide which side is in the right. The stronger side usually kills more, and strength is not guilt. Shakespeare’s soldier Williams, whom Mr. Boreing quotes, asks the right first question: was the cause good?


But the just-war tradition he invokes, from Augustine through Aquinas to the Geneva Conventions, asks two sets of questions, not one. The first concerns going to war: just cause, legitimate authority, right intention, last resort. The second concerns conduct in war: discrimination, meaning the deliberate sparing of noncombatants, and proportion, meaning force matched to the threat and the objective. A just cause does not sanctify every act done in its name. I made this point in 2005 regarding depleted uranium munitions in Iraq: every war contains terrible acts, but there should never be grossly mismatched force related to threat.


So Mr. Carlson errs by treating the body count as the whole of the moral question, and Mr. Boreing errs by treating it as none of it. Civilian deaths do not tell us whether the cause was just. They do tell us, in part, whether the war was justly fought, and a people that will not ask that question has abandoned half of the tradition it claims.


Applying my three questions to the wars after September 11 yields different answers for different wars, which is the point:


- **Afghanistan, 2001.** *Righteous?* Yes. The Taliban sheltered the men who had declared war on us and killed 3,000 of our people, and refused to hand them over. Congress authorized force. *Loving?* Yes, toward the American people whom the government is bound to protect, and the war was loving toward Afghans insofar as it was aimed at their oppressors and not at them. *Would I accept it if it were done to me?* If my government harbored men who had murdered thousands of another nation’s citizens and refused to surrender them, I would recognize that nation’s right to come for them. The cause passes. The twenty years that followed, which drifted from punishing al-Qaeda into building a nation by force, must be judged separately.

- **Iraq, 2003.** *Righteous?* This is where the war falls short. It was sold on a threat that didn’t exist and a connection to September 11 that didn’t exist. A war begun on a false premise fails the first question, whatever good was done afterward and however honorably the soldiers served. Saddam Hussein was a murderer of his own people, but that was not the case put to Congress and to the nation.

- **Conduct in both.** Where targeting rules sought to spare civilians and commanders were held to account for breaches, our conduct was honorable by the standard of any war in history. Where strikes were made on thin intelligence and civilian deaths went uncounted and unacknowledged, it was not. A righteous nation counts its wrongs as carefully as its victories.


None of this diminishes the soldier. The man who served honorably in Iraq did what his country asked of him, and the fault for a war begun on a false premise lies with those who made the decision, not with those who carried it out. Every veteran who fought honorably in either war deserves his nation’s thanks.


## 7. What the “forever war” slogan gets wrong, and what it gets right


Mr. Boreing is right that peace has never been mankind’s normal state. Will and Ariel Durant reckoned that only 268 of the past 3,421 years of recorded history had been free of war. Peace is purchased by strength and lasts only as long as that strength holds. A nation that withdraws from the world does not find that the world leaves it alone. The vacuum it leaves is filled, and rarely by friends of freedom. Charles Lindbergh argued for staying out of the Second World War on September 11, 1941. Eighty-seven days later, Pearl Harbor ended the debate, and Lindbergh himself, to his credit, went to the Pacific and flew combat missions.


So the slogan “no more forever wars,” taken as a promise that America can simply come home and be safe, is false. It mistakes our absence for the absence of the threat.


But the slogan’s underlying complaint contains a constitutional truth that the speech does not address. Article I, Section 8, gives Congress, not the President, the power to declare war. The Authorization for Use of Military Force, passed on September 18, 2001, targeted those who planned, authorized, committed, or aided the attacks. For twenty-five years, presidents of both parties have stretched those sixty words to cover groups that did not exist in 2001 and countries that had nothing to do with September 11. Congress has let them, because a vote on war is a vote a member must answer for.


That is the real “forever” in forever war: not that the threat never ends, which is true and cannot be helped, but that the authority to fight never expires, which is a choice and should be corrected. A nation may be at war for generations against an enemy that will not stop, and still require its representatives to stand up and vote, by name, on each new enemy and each new country. That is not isolationism. It is self-government.


## 8. The cause worth living for


The most important part of Mr. Boreing’s speech is its last third, and I agree with him on its root. A civilization that no longer believes its cause is good will not defend itself and will not even replace itself. The birth rates he cites are evidence of this. Young men raised on comfort without purpose become restless and seek a cause in dark places. Orwell, reviewing *Mein Kampf* in 1940, saw that Hitler won a nation by offering struggle, danger, and death where his rivals offered only a good time.


But here is where I part from Mr. Boreing, and the difference is not small. Orwell describes danger, not a cure. Offering struggle as such is exactly what Hitler did. If the West answers its emptiness merely by finding a cause to fight for, it may find the wrong one, and the young men radicalizing in dark corners today are evidence that this is already happening. The question is not whether men will follow a cause. They will. The question is which cause is righteous.


Mr. Boreing names the American cause as freedom joined to duty and virtue, and he is right: freedom without duty becomes license, and license without virtue becomes rot. But this still leaves the question of where duty and virtue come from. They do not maintain themselves. Madison staked the whole American experiment on a people’s capacity to govern themselves according to the Ten Commandments. Freedom is the fruit; righteousness is the root. *“Where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is liberty”* **(2 Corinthians 3:17)**. A people cut off from that root will lose its freedom no matter how bravely it fights, because it will no longer know why freedom is worth having, or how to use it once it has it.


The cause I would offer the young is not struggle for its own sake, nor the nation for its own sake, nor even freedom for its own sake. It is Christ, and the righteous life He calls men to, lived out in families, churches, and a nation that defends the innocent, keeps its word, tells the truth about its own faults, and does not fear its enemies. That is a cause worth the arms and legs and heads, in Williams’s phrase, because it will still be good at the latter day.


The Durants were right: civilization is not inherited; each generation must learn and earn it anew. Ninety million Americans have been born since September 11. They cannot remember it; they can only be taught. We owe them the true account of that day, the true account of the long war of which it was a part, and the true account of our own faults. We should not give them a heroic story with the uncomfortable parts removed, because they will eventually discover the omissions, and then they will believe nothing we say.


## 9. Positions


- **Tell the truth about September 11, and refuse to be a false witness.** The government, schools, and every public voice should state plainly what the evidence shows: al-Qaeda planned and carried out the attacks. Accusing the innocent, whether the American government or Israel or the Jewish people, without evidence is bearing false witness, and Christians should say so even when the accuser is on their own side of the aisle.

- **Rebuild trust by confession, not by demand.** Federal agencies should publish full accounts of their own errors, including intelligence failures before the Iraq war and misstatements during the COVID pandemic, with names attached to decisions. Openness about our own lies is the only answer to conspiracy theories that will last.

- **Congress votes on every war.** Repeal the 2001 Authorization for Use of Military Force and replace it with authorizations that name the enemy, define the objective, and expire unless Congress renews them by recorded vote. The President retains his power to repel sudden attack; he does not acquire a standing power to wage war wherever he chooses.

- **Judge each war by both halves of the just-war tradition.** Ask whether the cause is just, and also whether the conduct spares the innocent and matches force to threat. Publish civilian casualty assessments honestly and hold commanders accountable for unlawful strikes. A just cause is strengthened, not weakened, by just conduct.

- **No tribute to extortion.** America should not pay ransom or tribute to terrorist regimes or groups, in money or in policy, because every payment raises the next price. Peace with those who have declared war on us is purchased by strength and kept by it.

- **Distinguish the doctrine from the neighbor.** Oppose, without apology, any doctrine that claims a religious right to conquer, enslave, or kill unbelievers, and require from every new citizen an oath of allegiance to the Constitution above any foreign or religious law. At the same time, defend the equal rights and the dignity of Muslim citizens, who are our neighbors, and offer them the Gospel freely, as we would want our faith offered to us.

- **Teach the true history, including our own faults.** Schools should teach the Barbary Wars, the long record of jihadist attacks on Americans, and the full story of September 11, along with the honest record of Iraq. A generation taught only shame will not defend its civilization. A generation taught only glory will feel betrayed when it learns the rest. Truth in proportion produces citizens who can love their country without lying about it.

- **Offer the young a righteous cause.** The answer to a generation hungry for meaning is not a new enemy but the old faith: Christ, the family, the church, honest work, and the defense of the innocent. Government cannot supply that cause, but it can stop teaching against it and make room for the families and churches that do.


These positions are taken on the evidence set out above. If it can be shown that the Iraq war rested on a just cause that the public record has hidden, or that a Congress forced to vote on each war would leave the nation defenseless against a fast-moving enemy, I will revise accordingly. I hold each position open to that argument and to any better one.

## Editorial notes


- **Source speech.** Jeremy Boreing’s remarks were delivered for the 25th anniversary of September 11 (the anniversary fell on a Friday, September 11, 2026). This paper uses names and quotations from Boreing and others he quotes (Alex Jones, Candace Owens, Tucker Carlson, Hasan Piker, Owen Shroyer), presented as he reported them; check them against the original sources for accuracy.

- **Constantinople fell in 1453**, not 1450.

- **Steel and jet fuel.** Structural steel loses about half its strength near 600°C (about 1,100°F); open jet fuel and office-content fires can exceed that. The speech’s figure of “90 percent” loss at 1,800°F is in the right direction but should be checked against the NIST NCSTAR 1 report before quoting. NIST attributed the Building 7 collapse to fire-induced thermal expansion that caused a key column to fail (NIST NCSTAR 1A, 2008).

- **“92 percent of American years at war.”** The Congressional Research Service list of instances of U.S. armed forces used abroad (beginning in 1798) includes many small actions, such as evacuations and anti-piracy landings. Counting any year with any such entry as a year “at war” produces a high figure; whether it is exactly 92 percent depends on the method and should be verified or softened before publication. The Durant figure (268 of 3,421 years without war) is from *The Lessons of History* (1968).

- **Tripoli ambassador.** Jefferson and Adams reported the ambassador’s statement in their letter to John Jay of March 28, 1786. The paraphrase above follows that letter.

- **Tribute share.** Estimates that tribute and ransom consumed up to about 20 percent of federal revenue around 1800 are widely repeated but vary by source and year.

- **Clinton remark.** On September 10, 2001, in Melbourne, Bill Clinton said he could have killed bin Laden but would have had to strike Kandahar and kill some 300 innocent people. The remark was recorded and later broadcast.

- **Iraq intelligence.** Senate Select Committee on Intelligence, *Report on the U.S. Intelligence Community’s Prewar Intelligence Assessments on Iraq* (July 2004); Commission on the Intelligence Capabilities of the United States Regarding Weapons of Mass Destruction (Robb-Silberman), *Report to the President* (March 2005); the Iraq Survey Group (Duelfer) report (2004) found no stockpiles.

- **2001 AUMF.** Public Law 107-40, September 18, 2001. Its operative text authorizes force against “those nations, organizations, or persons” the President determines planned, authorized, committed, or aided the September 11 attacks, or harbored them. Confirm its status, and whether the 2002 Iraq authorization has been repealed.

- **Polling figures** (Emerson College/Nexstar on 9/11 beliefs; Gallup June 2026 confidence in institutions; Pew December trust in government) are as reported in the speech and should be confirmed against the original releases.

- **Orwell** reviewed *Mein Kampf* in the *New English Weekly*, March 21, 1940. **Lindbergh**‘s Des Moines speech was September 11, 1941; he flew about fifty combat missions in the Pacific as a civilian technical representative in 1944.
