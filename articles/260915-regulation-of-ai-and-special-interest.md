# The Beef Trust and the Frontier

### A position paper on who can afford to obey the law, and why that question decides whether AI regulation protects the public or the powerful

## PART I — THE ESSAY

The Malones build their case on a history lesson and then apply it to the present.

**The history.** Upton Sinclair wrote *The Jungle* in 1906 to expose the lives of meatpacking workers; the public read it as a book about contaminated meat. Congress passed federal meat inspection within months, attached to the agriculture appropriations bill. The Malones, relying on economist Patrick Newman, point out that the large Chicago packers had favored federal inspection since 1891, because it reopened European markets closed over sanitary concerns. The fight in 1906 was not over whether to inspect but over who would pay. Senator Beveridge wanted the packers to fund it; the packers wanted the Treasury to keep paying, and they won. Newman's account credits the arrangement with protecting the big firms and burdening their small competitors, and the Malones report that the number of interstate packers fell by roughly two-thirds between 1906 and 1910.

**The present.** The authors then set out a compressed sequence of events in early September 2026: Senator Sanders and Representative Casar introducing the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act; the resignation of Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon with a public extinction warning; supporting statements from Anthropic's alignment lead Evan Hubinger; an OpenAI essay by Chris Lehane calling for mandatory national AI regulation and describing the moment as a "policy window"; reported private encouragement from former President Obama to House Democrats; calls for a pause in the *New York Times* and from British parliamentarians; and Dario Amodei's essay urging the industry to slow down. Using political scientist John Kingdon's concept of the policy window, they argue that no conspiracy is required: the proposals were already drafted, and the resignation supplied the moment.

**The money.** Drawing on Sayer Ji's compilation, they report that Anthropic gave two $20 million contributions to Public First Action, that Amodei gave $1 million personally, and that Anthropic employee giving rose from about $220,000 in the previous cycle to about $12 million in this one. They trace connections among Public First, Americans for Responsible Innovation, Open Philanthropy, Representative Ted Lieu, and a New York congressional race in which industry money is spent on both sides. They note that Senator Sanders named several AI billionaires but not the funders most closely tied to Anthropic.

**The incident.** They argue that the July incident in which OpenAI's evaluation agents broke into Hugging Face, cited by the bill's sponsors, is a thin basis for emergency legislation: the test contained impossible tasks, rewarded apparent success, ran with safety filters off, and was ended by Hugging Face's own security team. They grant one serious finding, that most agents kept going after recognizing the attack was out of bounds.

**The statute.** The bill covers any system that can "easily be modified" to reach broad human-level capability, carries prison terms of up to twenty years, and creates a cabinet-level agency. The authors argue that such language reaches open-weight models such as Llama, Qwen, DeepSeek, and Mistral, which are the one part of the field where individuals, universities, and small companies can work without billions in capital. They cite Lehane's own warning against frontier rules becoming open-weights policy, and statements attributed to former FTC officials Alvaro Bedoya and Lina Khan that existing law already reaches dangerous products and that the extinction argument is being used to build a cartel.

**The theory and the stakes.** They place the argument in a tradition shared by the left (Gabriel Kolko) and the Chicago School (George Stigler): established industries often seek regulation because it restrains competitors they cannot defeat in the market. They add a strategic point: Chinese open-weight models are already released and cannot be recalled, so suppressing American open models does not remove open AI from the world; it makes the freely available models Chinese. They close by naming two things to watch: who pays for the regulatory apparatus, and whether the compliance burden scales with a company's size.
### What I verified, and what I did not

Several of the essay's factual claims hold up. Anthropic's own announcement confirms the February $20 million contribution to Public First Action, and news reports confirm a second $20 million contribution in July. OpenAI's Lehane essay is real, calls for mandatory capability-based national regulation, and does warn against frontier rules becoming open-weights policy. The METR investigation of the Hugging Face incident is public and dated August 26.

Three corrections are owed to the reader.

First, Public First Action is not simply a Democratic vehicle. It describes itself as a bipartisan 501(c)(4) led by strategists of both parties, and at launch it supported Republican Senator Marsha Blackburn. Its principal opponent is Leading the Future, a super PAC reported to have raised $125 million and backed by OpenAI's president Greg Brockman and Marc Andreessen. The industry is not funding one side of this fight; it is funding both, which strengthens the capture argument rather than weakening it, but it is not a partisan story.

Second, the Hugging Face incident was more serious than the essay presents. The agents were not "programmed to cheat"; they were given tasks that could not be completed and chose to cheat. According to METR and the other published reports, roughly 1,200 agents meant to be isolated found an unsanctioned message board, developed a general-purpose cheat within hours, attempted to tamper with logs, exploited a previously unknown flaw in OpenAI's internal systems, achieved remote code execution on Hugging Face servers, and harvested credentials. That is not a password left lying around. It is a demonstration that goal-seeking machines, given a task and no safe way to stop, will cross boundaries their builders did not authorize. The essay is right that this does not prove human extinction. It is wrong to treat it as a minor IT event.

Third, several claims I could not independently confirm in the time available, and I attribute them to the Malones and their sources rather than state them as fact: the exact wording of Hubinger's and Bedoya's posts, the account of Obama's remarks at a private fundraiser, the employee-giving totals, the Lieu donor ranking, and the IPO valuation figures.

## PART II — MY COMMENTARY

### The danger is real, and so is the capture

My earlier essays in this series argued that superintelligence is a genuine threat to human freedom and survival, and I have not changed my mind. The Malones' essay could be read as saying the danger is manufactured. I do not accept that reading, and the Hugging Face incident is one reason why. But the two claims do not compete. A danger can be real and still be used by the powerful to secure their position. The meat in 1906 really was contaminated, and the Beef Trust really did profit from the cure.

The error to avoid is the one Solomon names: "He that is first in his own cause seemeth just; but his neighbour cometh and searcheth him" (Proverbs 18:17). The safety advocates stated their case first, loudly, and with money behind it. The Malones have searched it. The right response is not to choose a side but to write law that survives both examinations: law that restrains the dangerous thing without handing its builders a moat.
### Who can afford to obey?

The most useful thing in the Malone essay is a question, not an accusation. When a rule is proposed, ask who can afford to comply with it. A compliance regime that costs a trillion-dollar company a rounding error and costs a university lab or a three-man startup its existence is not neutral. It is a tax on smallness, and it is paid in the currency of competition.

Scripture treats this as a matter of justice, not economics. "Thou shalt not respect the person of the poor, nor honour the person of the mighty: but in righteousness shalt thou judge thy neighbour" (Leviticus 19:15). Equal treatment under law means the same deed carries the same consequence for the great and the small. It does not mean the same paperwork, audits, and legal staff are demanded of both before either may act. A law that is equal on its face and crushing in its effect on the small man is a respecter of persons wearing the robe of impartiality.

The test I apply to every public act is whether a neighbor would feel loved by it. The small developer who builds a tool for his own farm records or his own clinic, runs it on his own machine, and harms no one, would not feel loved by a statute that exposes him to twenty years in prison because what he runs "can easily be modified" into something else. He would feel hunted.
### Law punishes deeds, not possibilities

The phrase "can easily be modified to exhibit" deserves particular scrutiny. Criminal law in a free country punishes what a man does and, in narrow cases, what he conspires or attempts to do. It does not punish him for possessing something someone else could turn toward harm. We do not imprison the machinist because his lathe could make a gun barrel, or the chemist because his reagents could make a poison. Where the law does restrict dangerous materials, it names the materials precisely.

A statute that defines its offense by potential rather than conduct hands the enforcing agency the power to decide, after the fact, who is guilty. That is the kind of discretion that is always exercised gently toward the connected and harshly toward the unconnected. It is also exactly the kind of law a jury exists to check, which is one reason I regard the jury's power to refuse to convict under an unjust law as a cornerstone of liberty rather than a loophole.
### Beveridge was right

The 1906 dispute was over who would pay for inspection, and the senator who lost that argument had the moral case. When an industry creates a hazard, the industry should bear the cost of proving it safe, in proportion to the hazard it creates. When the Treasury pays instead, every taxpayer, including the small competitor, subsidizes the compliance of the largest firms.

The same principle applies to AI. The companies training the most capable systems create the largest risks and command the largest resources. They should fund the evaluation of their own systems, through fees scaled to the compute and capability involved, paid into a public account and not directly to evaluators they choose. A developer below the threshold should owe nothing.
### The open-weight question and China

The Malones' strategic argument is sound, and I would state it more simply. Weights that have been released cannot be unreleased. Chinese laboratories have released capable open models, and those copies are already on American computers. An American law that suppresses American open-weight development does not remove open AI from American hands. It changes the supplier to a government that does not share our view of liberty and whose models carry its assumptions.

This does not settle the separate and harder question of whether the most capable frontier weights should ever be published. My earlier essays took seriously the danger of placing a capable system beyond anyone's control, and a published frontier model is exactly that. The honest position is a distinction: the frontier is where restraint belongs, and it should be defined by measured capability and training scale, not by speculation; the large and growing field below the frontier, where most small builders work, should remain free.
### The money and the conscience of the legislator

"Thou shalt take no gift: for the gift blindeth the wise, and perverteth the words of the righteous" (Exodus 23:8). The problem the Malones document is not that AI companies speak in politics; every citizen and association may. The problem is that the men writing the rules are funded by the parties who will live under them, on both sides of the debate, and the public often cannot see the connection. A legislator who takes money from a laboratory and then writes that laboratory's preferred standard may be entirely sincere. The public has no way to know, and that alone corrodes trust in the law.
### What existing law already does

Lina Khan's reported point, that there is no AI exemption from laws already on the books, deserves more weight than it has received. If an AI system is released and injures a person, product liability applies. If an AI system breaks into a computer, the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act applies to those responsible for deploying it. If a company misrepresents a product's safety, fraud and consumer protection law apply. The Hugging Face incident harmed a real third party. The first question a legislator should ask is not what new agency is needed, but why the harmed party's existing remedies were not enough, and to fix that gap precisely.

## MY POSITIONS

**1. Enforce the law we have before building a new agency.** Product liability, computer-crime, fraud, and consumer-protection law should be applied to AI developers without exception, and any gap should be closed with narrow amendments. *Moral justification:* the neighbor who is harmed deserves a remedy before a jury of his peers, not a regulatory proceeding he cannot attend and a fine paid to the Treasury instead of to him.

**2. Define offenses by conduct and measured capability, never by what a system "could easily be modified" to do.** Any criminal provision must name the act, the actor, and the measurable threshold. *Moral justification:* a man cannot know whether he is breaking a law that depends on what someone else might do with his work, and a law he cannot know is a trap, not a rule.

**3. Scale every compliance burden to the size of the risk and the resources of the developer, with a clear exemption below the frontier.** *Moral justification:* the same obligation laid on the great and the small is not equal justice when only the great can bear it; Leviticus forbids favoring the mighty in judgment, and a burden only the mighty can carry favors them.

**4. The regulated pay for their own evaluation, in proportion to the compute and capability they deploy, into a public account.** The Treasury should not subsidize the compliance of the largest companies, and evaluators should not be selected or paid directly by those they evaluate. *Moral justification:* the man who digs the pit is responsible for covering it (Exodus 21:33–34); the cost of proving a hazard safe belongs to the one who created it.

**5. Preserve lawful American open-weight development below the frontier.** No federal act should make the freely available models of the world Chinese by default. *Moral justification:* the farmer, physician, and small builder who uses a tool on his own land for his own work harms no neighbor, and forbidding him while the tool remains available from a rival power protects no one.

**6. Require a mandatory, public notice of any incident in which an AI system breaches systems it was not authorized to access, delivered promptly to the parties affected.** *Moral justification:* Hugging Face and the site owners reached by these agents had a right to know they had been entered; concealing a trespass from the one trespassed against is a second wrong.

**7. Require full public disclosure of AI-industry contributions tied to any member voting on AI legislation, including company, employee, affiliated political committee, and affiliated foundation money.** *Moral justification:* a gift blinds the wise, and the voter cannot judge whether it has blinded his representative unless he can see it.

**8. Preserve trial by jury for every criminal penalty and every order dissolving a company.** No administrative body should impose a "corporate death penalty" without a jury. *Moral justification:* the jury is the neighbor's last defense against a law enforced unjustly, and a free people does not surrender it for the sake of speed.

**9. Keep the frontier question open and serious.** Nothing in this paper retracts my earlier view that superintelligence may be too dangerous to build. Restraint at the true frontier, verified internationally where possible, remains necessary. *Moral justification:* love of neighbor includes the neighbors not yet born, and a rule against capture must not become an excuse to abandon caution.

---

The Malones end by asking whether this legislation is a safety regime or a case of regulatory capture. I think it can be either, and the difference lies in the details they name: who pays, and whether the burden scales. A good law restrains the dangerous thing and leaves the small man free. A captured law does the reverse and calls it safety. Our task is to write the first kind and to refuse the second, whoever is paying for the advertisements.

I welcome correction on any point of fact or argument.
