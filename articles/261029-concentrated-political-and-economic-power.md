# 261029 - Concentrated Political and Economic Power

# Concentrated Power Is the Problem:


### What Two Forgotten Books Saw,

What a Righteous Nation Should Do About It


*A position paper by Dr. Thomas | 29 October 2026

*


*Responding to Drs. Robert and Jill Malone’s review of* Global Reach *(1974) and* Global Dreams *(1994)*


---


## My position


I stand with those who warn that economic and political power in the modern world has become concentrated in too few hands, and that this concentration has escaped the accountability a free people owes itself. This is not a baseless conspiracy theory. It is a documented trend, argued for more than fifty years by thinkers on the political left and the political right, and it is a moral problem before it is an economic one.


Robert and Jill Malone have just published a review of two books that made this case long before it was fashionable to dismiss it: *Global Reach: The Power of the Multinational Corporations* by Richard Barnet and Ronald Müller (1974), and *Global Dreams: Imperial Corporations and the New World Order* by Barnet and John Cavanagh (1994). Barnet was no right-winger. He served in the Kennedy administration and co-founded the Institute for Policy Studies, a progressive think tank. Yet his diagnosis is, in its essentials, the same diagnosis free-market critics, populists, and Christians concerned with justice make today.


Below I set out what is established, what is probable, and what is plausible but unproven. I mark where the argument must be handled with care. Then I explain why the concern remains reasonable even where the evidence is incomplete, and what I believe a righteous government should do. I hold these positions for the reasons given, and I will change them if presented with a better argument.


---


## The moral test


Every position paper on this site is measured against three questions, asked in order:


- **Is it righteous?** Does it conform to what God establishes as just?

- **If so, is it loving?** Does it serve my neighbor’s real good?

- **Would I want it done to me?** Not to my momentary appetite, but to the self committed to righteousness.


Concentrated, unaccountable power fails the first question before we reach the others. Scripture is consistent on this. The king of Israel was forbidden to multiply silver and gold to himself, and he was required to write out the law and live under it like every other man **(Deuteronomy 17:17–20)**. The prophets pronounced woe on those who join house to house and field to field until there is no place left for anyone else **(Isaiah 5:8)**. Divers weights and divers measures, a different scale for the strong than for the weak, are an abomination to the Lord **(Proverbs 20:10)**.


The principle beneath all three is this: **power must answer to those it affects, and the same rules must bind the great and the small.** When a few hundred decision-makers can move a town’s livelihood to another hemisphere (exporting jobs/industry), set their own internal prices to decide which nation collects their taxes, and help write the trade rules they will then profit from, they break that principle. It does not matter whether those decision-makers sit in a corporate tower, a government ministry, or a conference hall in Switzerland.


The third question makes it personal. Picture the furniture worker in Martinsville, Virginia, whose plant closed after China entered the World Trade Organization in 2001. He had no vote in the decision. He couldn’t see who made the decision. He was told it was efficient. He lost his job; the country got a cheaper product; the country became dependent on another country for its defense and/or industrial capacity. At what point does a local country’s unemployment rate and its capacity to mount a wartime mobilization, withstand a natural disaster, or resist economic/supply-chain/skilled-labor blackmail become the predominant consideration? This is much more complex than whether the unemployed worker doesn’t like being unemployed. These tradeoffs are at the heart of the local production vs. global supply and trade dilemma. We must answer the question of allocation of expensive domestic resources vs. procurement of cheaper labor and products from international sourcing.


---


## What is established


These points are well documented and not seriously disputed by honest observers.


**Corporations shift profits to escape taxation.** When one branch of a multinational company sells to another, the company sets the price, and with it decides where profits appear on paper. A Treasury official quoted in *Global Reach* put it bluntly: “Prices in an economic sense do not exist.” The proof that this problem is real is that governments around the world eventually acted on it. In 2021, more than 130 jurisdictions endorsed an OECD framework for a 15 percent global minimum corporate tax. Nobody builds a worldwide treaty structure to solve an imaginary problem.


**Global trade cost American communities dearly.** Economists Acemoglu, Autor, Dorn, Hanson, and Price estimated that rising Chinese import competition cost American communities roughly two million or more jobs between 1999 and 2011. The losses hit particular towns hardest, including the furniture and textile communities of Virginia and the Carolinas, and those towns lost their tax base, their institutions, and, in many cases, their families.


**Financial ownership is highly concentrated.** Three asset managers, BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street, now manage tens of trillions of dollars and cast a large share of the proxy votes at America’s major companies. Ordinary savers and pension funds largely own the money, but the managers cast the votes.


**Private profit, public loss.** In 1971 Congress guaranteed $250 million in loans to rescue Lockheed. Barnet and Müller called it “bankers’ socialism.” In 2008, the same pattern repeated on a vastly larger scale. Institutions kept their gains in good years and passed their losses to taxpayers in bad ones.


**The barriers came down by law.** Barnet and Müller urged separating commercial banking from other financial activity. Congress did the opposite with the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act of 1999, which allowed banking, investment banking, and insurance to combine under one holding company.


**The courts also joined money and politics.** In *Citizens United v. FEC* (2010), the Supreme Court ruled 5–4 that corporations and unions may spend without limit on independent political advocacy.


**The executives said what they wanted.** This is not inference. An IBM executive described national political structures as obsolete. Dow Chemical’s chairman wrote of his dream of buying an island that belonged to no nation, where his company would owe allegiance to no society, and told the authors he meant it. When men who control vast resources openly say they want to be free of every nation, believing them is not paranoia.


---


## What is probable


These points rest on strong evidence and sound reasoning, though the full picture is not yet public.


**Regulation tends to entrench the largest players.** Complex rules require lawyers, lobbyists, and compliance departments. Large firms can afford them and small firms cannot. As a result, regulation sold as a check on big business often becomes a moat around it. *Global Dreams* reported that 108 of 111 members of three important U.S. trade-advisory committees represented corporations or industry groups. The people writing the rules were the people the rules would serve.


**Public-private forums steer policy outside democratic channels.** The World Economic Forum, founded in 1971, describes its own mission as public-private cooperation. It has no power to pass laws. It does not need any. When heads of government and heads of corporations agree on priorities in private settings, those priorities later appear in national policy with the deliberation already done and the public already left out.


**Government and digital platforms coordinated on speech.** During the COVID period, federal officials pressed technology platforms over what content to suppress. The Supreme Court did not resolve the constitutional question in *Murthy v. Missouri* (2024), deciding the case on standing, but the contacts themselves are documented in the record. When a state can achieve through a private company what the First Amendment forbids it to do directly, it erases the line between public and private power.


---


## What is plausible and worth examining


**The degree of coordinated intent.** How much of this concentration is deliberate, planned among a small circle, and how much is the natural convergence of people whose interests run the same way? I don’t know, and I don’t think anyone outside those circles does. I am convinced the question is legitimate and that the people asking it deserve an answer, not a label.


---


## Where the argument must be careful


A strong argument becomes weak if it rests on a claim a critic can knock down. These are the boundaries I observe.


**The case does not require a secret world government.** The Malones call the phenomenon “globalized corporatism,” and the term is apt. Corporatism needs no hidden council. It emerges whenever powerful institutions find their interests overlap and cooperate to advance them, in public, in conference rooms, and through entirely legal lobbying. The trend is documented. Speculation about a single directing hand is a separate claim, and the trend does not depend on it.


**The nation-state is not dead.** Barnet and Müller predicted that corporations would reshape governments rather than abolish them, and history has borne that out. Nations still act when their leaders choose to. On January 7, 2026, the United States announced its withdrawal from 66 international organizations, including 31 United Nations bodies. On July 1, 2026, at the first joint review of the U.S.-Mexico-Canada Agreement, the United States declined to renew it in its current form. The agreement remains in force and now goes to annual review rather than an automatic 16-year extension. These actions show that sovereignty is a choice, not a lost cause.


**Globalization produced real goods and real harms.** Global trade lowered the price of many goods that poor American families depend on, and it lifted hundreds of millions abroad out of extreme poverty. I do not deny that. My objection is not to trade. It is to *who decides*, and to whether the people who bear the costs had any say in the decision.


**Citizens United did not permit unlimited contributions to candidates.** It removed limits on independent spending. The distinction matters, and an argument that blurs it will be corrected and dismissed.


**On the Senate staff report.** The Malones report that in September 2025, Democratic minority staff of the Senate Finance Committee cited Dr. Malone’s discussion of the New World Order as evidence of conspiracy thinking. I have not been able to locate the report independently, so I rely on their account here.


---


## Why the concern remains reasonable


Some will grant every point above and still say the concern is overstated. I disagree, for four reasons.


**The burden of proof belongs to those who concentrate power.** When someone gathers authority over other people’s livelihoods, savings, speech, and votes, he must show that the arrangement is just and accountable. Uncertainty about how far the concentration reaches does not reassure me. That is why we must examine it, because unaccountable power is, by its nature, hard to see.


**The incentive is structural, not hypothetical.** A firm that can escape a nation’s taxes, labor standards, and courts gains an advantage over every firm that cannot. Every competitive pressure in the system pushes toward escape. We do not need to assume bad men to predict the result. We only need to assume that men respond to incentives, which Scripture and economics both affirm.


**Answering an argument with a label is a sign of weakness.** In 1974, two respected progressive scholars could write a 500-page book on corporate power over national governments and be reviewed seriously in *Foreign Policy* and *Commentary*. Fifty years later, raising related questions can earn a man an official charge of conspiracy thinking. The concentration of power did not shrink in the meantime. It grew enormously. When a documented argument is met with a name instead of a rebuttal, the name-caller has usually run out of rebuttals.


**The intuition is sound.** Most Americans sense something is wrong when a decision made on another continent can empty a town, when no American company can manufacture the medicines we depend on, and when the platforms that carry our public debate answer to no voter. That sense is not ignorance. It is a moral perception: that a people who cannot make their own goods, govern their own credit, or speak freely in their own public square are not fully free. Friedrich Hayek showed why this perception matters. No central planner, whether in a government ministry or a corporate headquarters, can have the local knowledge needed to make good decisions for everyone. Power that is far from the people it governs will govern them badly, however well-intentioned.


---


## Where I differ from Barnet, and from some of his critics


Barnet’s remedy was more public oversight, more government ownership, and, in some versions, international institutions to supervise international corporations. I reject that. You do not cure a concentration of power by building a larger concentration above it. The largest corporations are best positioned to capture any global regulator, and the United Nations’ own attempt at a corporate code of conduct died without adoption.


Yet Barnet, in a 1976 interview, pointed toward a better answer: giving communities greater control over their own resources. That is the principle of subsidiarity, that decisions belong as close as possible to the people who bear their consequences. It is also a biblical pattern: Moses set rulers over thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens, and only the hardest cases came to him** (Exodus 18:21–22)**.


I also want to be honest with those who share my concern. Executive orders can be undone on the next inauguration day. A tariff can protect an industry or reward a donor. If we fight corporatism by handing favors to our own preferred corporations, we have changed the beneficiaries and kept the sin. The remedy must be durable, written into law, and it must bind our friends as well as our opponents. Practically, such a policy change requires a national/cultural shift before it will be accepted as a guiding principle for our political, economic, and cultural transactions.


---


## What I would do


My platform rests on **A New ERA: Energy, Righteousness, and Accountability.** Each part applies here.


**Energy.** A nation cannot rebuild its manufacturing on scarce and expensive power. Abundant domestic energy, beginning with thorium molten-salt reactors, is the foundation for bringing production home. Factories follow cheap, reliable power.


**Righteousness: honest weights.**


- Tax multinational profits where the real economic activity occurs, test transfer prices against what unrelated parties would actually pay, and staff enforcement to the task.

- End bailouts of private institutions. When a firm fails, its shareholders and bondholders bear the loss before a single taxpayer does. No one keeps the profit and hands off the risk.

- Restore the wall between ordinary deposit banking and speculative finance, so ordinary people’s savings are not collateral for other men’s wagers.

- Let retirement savers vote their own shares. Pass-through voting would return proxy power from three asset managers to the millions of people whose money it is.


**Accountability.**


- No treaty or international agreement can bind American law or taxation without Senate ratification. Congress should make executive withdrawals from international bodies that do not serve the American people durable by act of Congress.

- Trade-advisory committees must include small business owners, workers, farmers, and ordinary citizens in real proportion, not as decoration.

- Require public disclosure of corporate political spending. Even *Citizens United* upheld disclosure; voters have a right to know who is speaking to them.

- Make it unlawful for any federal official to pressure a private platform to suppress lawful speech, with real penalties for those who do it.

- Enforce the antitrust laws already on the books, and encourage community banks and local credit, so that the local laundry, farm, and machine shop can borrow on the same terms as a multinational’s subsidiary.


---


## The stakes


Barnet and Müller’s 1974 question is no longer whether multinational corporations can influence governments, shape economies, and move decisions beyond the reach of ordinary citizens. They plainly can, and they do. The question is whether a free people will reclaim the authority to govern their own work, savings, and speech.


Government exists to secure justice for its citizens, not to smooth the path of global commerce for those who have outgrown every nation. A righteous government keeps one scale for the strong and the weak. It keeps decisions near the people who live with them. It refuses to let any man, corporate or political, become so great that no one can hold him to account. That is what I mean by accountability, and it is what I am asking you to demand.
