# 261119 - Nuclear Energy is Cool

# Letting Go of a Bad Meme:


### Why I Stand for Nuclear Power

*A reflection on Isabelle Boemeke's TED talk, "Nuclear Power Is Our Best Hope to Ditch Fossil Fuels"*

**Thomas Lee Abshier, ND | 19 November 2026**

**Resource:** Isabelle Boemeke, "[Nuclear power is our best hope to ditch fossil fuels](https://www.ted.com/talks/isabelle_boemeke_nuclear_power_is_our_best_hope_to_ditch_fossil_fuels)," TED (2022) —


---


## Introduction

I recently watched a TED talk by a Brazilian model named Isabelle Boemeke, who calls herself the world's first nuclear energy influencer. Online, she plays a character named "Isodope." Isodope looks like an ordinary beauty influencer, starts a makeup tutorial, and halfway through the cleanser recommendation tells her viewers to stop shutting down nuclear plants. It is a strange way to argue for an energy policy. It also works. Her talk has been watched millions of times.

Her argument is simple. She says the world was sold a "bad meme," meaning a fixed belief that spreads from person to person and shapes how we see the world. The meme is "nuclear energy is bad." She did not set out to defend nuclear power. She grew up holding the meme like most people, and changed her mind after years of reading the research and asking scientists what they thought. She found that the scientists mostly agreed: nuclear is good, we need it, and people hate it.

I stand with her. I have held for many years that abundant, clean, reliable energy is the foundation of a prosperous and free nation. It is the "E" in the New ERA Vision: Energy, Righteousness, and Accountability. My own preference is the thorium molten-salt reactor, but this essay is not about any one design. It is about whether we will judge nuclear power by its record or by our fear of it.

I have checked her claims against current sources. Most hold; several have grown stronger since she spoke, and a few need correcting. The one serious objection is cost, which I address below.


---


## The Moral Question Under the Energy Question

Before the numbers, I want to name what is really at stake. An energy policy is a moral decision. It decides who breathes clean air, who gets light to study by at night, who has a refrigerator for medicine, and which nations must buy their fuel from tyrants. In judging any public policy, I ask three questions in order:


 	- **Is it righteous?** Does it agree with what God has established as good: truthfulness, stewardship of creation, protection of life?

 	- **If so, is it loving?** Does it do good to my neighbor, especially the poor and the weak?

 	- **Would I want it done to me?** Would I accept this policy if I were the one living with its costs?


The first question applies to how we form our beliefs before it applies to the reactors themselves. Scripture warns against reaching a verdict before hearing the case: *"He that answereth a matter before he heareth it, it is folly and shame unto him"* **(Proverbs 18:13)**. Most of us decided about nuclear power from movies, cartoons, and headlines, and never weighed the evidence. That is not a sin of the engineers. It is a failure of judgment among the public, and it has cost lives.

The same chapter tells us how to correct it: *"He that is first in his own cause seemeth just; but his neighbour cometh and searcheth him"* **(Proverbs 18:17)**. So I will search the case from both sides.


---


## Where the Meme Came From

Ms. Boemeke traces the fear to the anti-nuclear movement of the 1970s, which grew partly out of the terror of nuclear war. Children of the 1950s and 1960s did duck-and-cover drills under their school desks. It is no surprise that they came to associate the atom with the end of the world. Her analogy is a good one: saying "nuclear bombs are bad, so nuclear power is bad" is like saying "the electric chair is bad, so electricity is bad."

To be fair to the people who held the fear, it was not only about bombs. The fear was also built on real events and real questions: Three Mile Island in 1979, Chernobyl in 1986, Fukushima in 2011, the unanswered question of where the waste would go, and the fact that several nations built weapons programs on the back of civilian nuclear programs. A conscientious person in 1980 had legitimate questions.

What I fault is not that the questions were asked. It is that the answers came in and the meme never updated. Decades of operating data now exist. When the evidence changes, a truthful person changes his mind.

The meme also hurt the industry itself. Ms. Boemeke notes that bright young engineers stopped choosing nuclear engineering, and politicians earned points for closing plants. She also mentions *The Simpsons*: a generation learned what a nuclear plant was from Homer, a bumbling safety inspector surrounded by glowing green rods. I agree with her joke that the culture would have been better served if Homer had worked at a coal plant.

These figures already include Chornobyl and Fukushima. Nuclear is roughly 800 times safer than coal per unit of electricity, and in the same narrow safety class as wind and solar. Ms. Boemeke says nuclear is "one of the safest forms of energy," and that is exactly right. She does not overclaim.

Her airplane analogy fits the case. Plane crashes make headlines and car crashes do not, so people feel that flying is more dangerous, though the opposite is true. A nuclear accident is dramatic. The steady death toll from burning fossil fuels is not news.

**A correction on the fossil-fuel death toll.** Ms. Boemeke cites a study finding that 8.7 million people die each year from fossil-fuel air pollution, which she compares to "5.8 Chernobyls a day." The study is real: a 2021 paper by researchers at Harvard and three British universities. But it is the high estimate. It was double the Global Burden of Disease figure of about 4.2 million deaths a year from all outdoor fine-particle pollution, which includes dust and wildfire smoke. Honesty requires stating the range: the true number is somewhere in the several millions.

The Chernobyl comparison also depends on which death toll for Chernobyl one uses. The estimates range from about 4,000 eventual deaths to several times that. Even using the lower pollution figure and the higher Chernobyl figure, burning fossil fuels kills about as many people as a Chernobyl every day or two, every year, without headlines. Her number is at the top of the range, but her point stands. Fossil fuels are far more deadly.

**What closing plants actually does.** Ms. Boemeke says that when a nuclear plant is shut down, fossil fuels replace it. Germany is the clearest test case. After Fukushima, Germany shut down most of its reactors. Economists at UC Berkeley, UC Santa Barbara, and Carnegie Mellon found that coal replaced most of the lost nuclear power. They estimated about 1,100 extra deaths a year from the added air pollution and a total social cost of about $12 billion a year, far more than the benefits from reduced accident risk and waste costs. I would not say closures *always* raise emissions, as she does; it depends on what replaces the plant. But where the replacement is coal or gas, which has usually been the case, the result is more deaths and more carbon.

### Emissions

Ms. Boemeke says nuclear has the lowest life-cycle emissions of any energy source. The most thorough recent assessment, by the UN Economic Commission for Europe (UNECE), agrees. It puts nuclear at about 5.1 to 6.4 grams of CO₂-equivalent per kilowatt-hour, the lowest of any technology studied. Onshore wind ranges from 7.8 to 16, solar photovoltaics from 8 to 83, and coal from 751 to 1,095.

A small correction: her figure of 3 tons per gigawatt-hour is lower than the UNECE range of about 5 to 6. The IPCC's older median for nuclear, 12 grams, is about the same as wind. Either way, nuclear sits with wind at the very bottom of the scale, and its exact rank does not change the argument.

### Waste

The waste question is the most common objection I hear. Ms. Boemeke's answer, in Isodope's voice, is blunt: you may not know what to do with it, but scientists have studied it for decades. Since her talk, the facts have strongly vindicated her.


 	- **Finland.** In October 2026 the Finnish government granted an operating licence for Onkalo, the world's first permanent deep repository for spent fuel, 22 years after construction began. It is carved into bedrock about 1.9 billion years old, more than 400 meters down, and will hold up to 6,500 tonnes of spent fuel in copper canisters surrounded by clay. Its operator had not set an exact start date when the licence was announced, but it is expected to begin operating soon.

 	- **Sweden.** Sweden broke ground on its own repository at Forsmark in January 2025. It expects about ten years of construction before disposal begins.


The volume is also small. All the spent fuel from Finland's five reactors fits in one repository. In the United States, spent fuel sits in steel-and-concrete dry casks on reactor sites. Ms. Boemeke jokes that the casks block radiation so well she might do a photoshoot beside them. The joke is true. The green leaking barrels in our imaginations are a cartoon.

The U.S. problem is not technical. It is political. We chose a site at Yucca Mountain and then abandoned it. Finland and Sweden show that a nation with the will to decide can solve the problem.

### Speed

The newer objection is that nuclear takes too long to build. Ms. Boemeke answers with France, and the record supports her. After the 1973 oil shock, France launched the Messmer Plan. According to the French utility EDF, France built 56 reactors between 1974 and 1989, the fastest nuclear buildout in history. She also points out that a plant built over ten years can run for 80 years. We will need electricity long after any construction delay is forgotten. Her best line is an old proverb rewritten: the best time to build nuclear plants was ten years ago; the next best time is today.

The slow and expensive projects in the West, which I take up next, are mainly a failure of construction management, regulation, and lost skills. The physics is not the problem.

## The Hardest Objection: Cost

Ms. Boemeke calls cost "the most fashionable objection." It is also the strongest, so I want to set out the best case against nuclear before answering it.

**The case against.** The only new U.S. reactors in a generation, Vogtle Units 3 and 4 in Georgia, came online in 2023 and 2024. They were seven years late and cost $36.8 billion, about double the original estimate, making them the most expensive power project in U.S. history. Georgia ratepayers are still paying for it. Stanford's Mark Jacobson, the most prominent advocate of an all-renewable grid, estimates Vogtle's power at about $199 per megawatt-hour, compared with roughly $58 to $62 for new utility-scale solar and onshore wind. On that basis, he argues that every dollar spent on nuclear is a dollar not spent on cheaper renewables, which could cut more carbon sooner. This is a serious argument, made by a serious scientist, and Vogtle gave him real ammunition.

**My answer.** I accept that Vogtle was expensive. I do not accept that Vogtle shows what nuclear must cost. Four facts weigh against that conclusion:


 	- **Vogtle was a first build after a 30-year gap.** America had forgotten how to build reactors. The Columbia University review of Vogtle's construction found that the project never had a realistic schedule. An MIT analysis estimates that later AP1000s, built by experienced crews, could deliver power at about $66 per megawatt-hour without subsidy.

 	- **Other nations build for much less.** China now builds reactors at about one-eighth of Vogtle's cost. A nation that builds the same design repeatedly learns to build it cheaply. Ms. Boemeke's point about solar applies here: solar costs fell by about 90 percent once the world committed to building it at scale. Nuclear can travel the same curve if we let it.

 	- **Comparing raw costs is misleading.** Solar and wind produce power only when the sun shines, and the wind blows. A grid that relies on them needs batteries, backup plants, and extra transmission, and the cost of that "firming" is not included in the cheap headline price. Nuclear runs around the clock in every season. The honest comparison is between whole grids that keep the lights on at midnight in January, not between single plants at noon in June.

 	- **Renewables have grown, and still need a partner.** When Ms. Boemeke spoke, wind and solar supplied about 8 percent of the world's electricity. By 2025, according to the energy think tank Ember, they supplied about 17 percent, and solar's growth alone met most new demand. I welcome that progress, and so does she. But 17 percent is not 100 percent, and the remaining load is the hardest part: nights, winters, heavy industry, and the fast-growing demand from data centers and AI.


So I agree that cost matters, and today it is the honest obstacle. It is a problem of industrial will, regulation, and repetition, not of physics. My conclusion is the same as hers: build the same design over and over until it is cheap.

## New Reactors

Ms. Boemeke points to small modular reactors (SMRs), factory-built units that could be cheaper and faster to deploy. Her examples need updating:


 	- **China's Linglong One**, a 125-megawatt SMR on Hainan Island, was not yet finished when she spoke. CNNC finished its cold testing in October 2025 and targeted commercial operation in the first half of 2026. As of the latest records I could find, the international reactor database still listed it as under construction. It is close, but I cannot yet confirm that it is running commercially.

 	- **Poland** is still pursuing her vision of converting coal plants to nuclear. In October 2026, two Polish companies formed a joint venture to build up to six GE Vernova Hitachi BWRX-300 reactors, about 1,800 megawatts in total, at the site of a coal plant at Pątnów that is being phased out. They plan to reuse the site, its grid connections, and its workforce, as she described. Coal still supplied about half of Poland's electricity in 2025, so the change has only begun.


China also offers a milestone of special interest to me. In November 2025, its experimental thorium molten-salt reactor, TMSR-LF1 in Gansu Province, reported the first conversion of thorium into uranium-233 fuel inside an operating molten-salt reactor. I need to keep this in proportion. It is a 2-megawatt research reactor, not a power plant, and its conversion ratio is small. But it shows that the thorium fuel cycle, which America itself pioneered at Oak Ridge in the 1960s and then abandoned, works in practice. It is sobering that another nation is now finishing work we began.

At home, the federal government set a goal in May 2025 of quadrupling U.S. nuclear capacity from about 100 gigawatts to 400 gigawatts by 2050. That is the right direction. Whether it happens will depend on whether we keep that commitment through changes of administration.

## Energy, Independence, and Peace

Near the end of her talk, Ms. Boemeke offers what she calls a crazy idea: that we see nuclear power as a force for energy independence and even peace. I do not think the idea is crazy. A nation that makes its own clean power does not need to buy oil and gas from dictators, or fund their wars by doing so. Europe learned this lesson painfully after Russia invaded Ukraine. Uranium and thorium are dense, so a few years of fuel can be stored in a small space. A nuclear nation is harder to blackmail.

She also reminds us of the poor. According to the International Energy Agency, about 720 million people still lived without electricity in 2025, most of them in sub-Saharan Africa. To them, electricity means light to read by, refrigeration for food and vaccines, clean water pumping, and work that does not depend on daylight. If we love our neighbor, we cannot tell the world's poor to wait in the dark while the rich nations debate their fears.

Now the third question: would I want this done to me? Would I live next to a nuclear plant? Yes. Given the choice, I would much rather live beside a reactor than downwind of a coal plant. That is what the death rates in the table above mean in practice.


---


## Why the Fear Was Understandable

I do not want to dismiss the people who fear nuclear power. A fear that has lasted fifty years is reaching for something real, and it deserves to be understood.


 	- **The fear was formed by real terror.** The bomb was real, and the generation that grew up under it had good reason to dread the atom. Their fear was misplaced in its object, but it was not foolish.

 	- **The accidents were real.** Chernobyl contaminated a region and displaced hundreds of thousands of people. Fukushima's evacuation caused real suffering. A technology whose rare failures are so visible will always face a higher standard, and it should.

 	- **Radiation cannot be seen.** People rightly fear harms they cannot sense, measure, or control themselves.

 	- **Trust was broken.** Governments and industry have at times been secretive about nuclear matters, and the cost overruns at Vogtle and elsewhere were real. Skepticism about promises of "safe and cheap" was earned.

 	- **The money cuts both ways.** Fossil-fuel interests have long benefited from public fear of their main carbon-free competitor. Nuclear vendors, in turn, profit from public enthusiasm. I treat advocacy from either side as interested testimony and weigh the data rather than the advocate.


Understanding the fear does not mean deferring to it. The answer to a reasonable fear is evidence, honestly presented, and the evidence is now in.


---


## Conclusion

Ms. Boemeke closes by saying she is not asking us to build a reactor, rewrite *The Simpsons*, or sign a petition. She asks only that we help spread a better meme: nuclear energy is cool.

I would put it a little differently. Nuclear energy is safe, clean, reliable, and ready to be built at scale. The case against it rested on a fear that was understandable when it formed and that the evidence has since answered. Holding onto that fear now is no longer prudence. It costs lives through air pollution, it leaves people in the dark, and it funds hostile regimes that sell us fuel.

So my answer to the three questions is this. It is righteous: it tells the truth about the evidence and cares for creation. It is loving: it gives clean air to the city and light to the village. And I would want it done for me. I stand for nuclear power, and I invite you to examine the evidence and stand with me.

**Limits of this sourcing.** My sources lean heavily on the mainstream record: UN agencies, the IEA, academic studies, and the nuclear and renewable trade press. Each has interests. The nuclear trade press wants reactors built, renewable advocates want their share of the grid, and Chinese state announcements about Linglong One and TMSR-LF1 have not been independently verified. Death-toll estimates for both air pollution and Chernobyl rest on statistical models, and reasonable researchers disagree about them by factors of two or more. Cost figures depend on assumptions about interest rates, plant life, and grid backup, which can be chosen to favor either side. I have tried to state ranges where they exist and to give the strongest case against my own position. I welcome correction from anyone who can show better evidence.
