# 261009 - An Israel Palestine Historical Perspective

# The Land Twice Promised: An Honest History of the Israeli–Palestinian Conflict


*Dr. Thomas for President — History and Policy Background* | 09 *October 2026*


---


## Why Accuracy Matters Here


Few subjects are argued with more heat and less care than this one. Each side tells a story where it is only the victim and the other is only the aggressor, and each story is built by leaving things out. A citizen who loves his neighbor cannot settle for either version, because a policy built on a half-truth will wrong someone — usually the people with the least power to object.


This essay was prompted by Robert Cherry’s October 2026 column, “Stop rewriting 1948,” which argues that Palestinian Arab society in 1948 was too divided and too poorly led to amount to a national movement. Some of his points are well founded and are often ignored. Others are wrong in their particulars, and the column omits events that any fair account must include. What follows is an attempt to tell the whole story in proportion, noting where historians still disagree. A section at the end lists specific corrections to the column.


---


## 1. Two Peoples, One Small Land (to 1917)


The land between the Jordan River and the Mediterranean has been home to Jews since antiquity. After the Roman wars of the first and second centuries, most Jews lived in exile, but a Jewish presence never entirely disappeared, centered in later centuries on Jerusalem, Hebron, Safed, and Tiberias. Jewish prayer and memory remained fixed on the land for nearly two thousand years.


From the seventh century on, the population became predominantly Arab and Muslim, with a substantial Christian Arab minority. Under Ottoman rule (1517–1917) there was no political unit called “Palestine”; the area was divided among Ottoman districts. Its Arab inhabitants identified mainly by family, village, religion, and region, and increasingly, by the early twentieth century, as Arabs and as Palestinians.


Modern Zionism arose in the 1880s and 1890s in response to persecution of Jews in Eastern Europe and to the failure of emancipation to end antisemitism in the West. Theodor Herzl’s *The Jewish State* (1896) and the First Zionist Congress in Basel (1897) gave it organized political form. Jewish immigrants bought land, founded agricultural settlements, and in 1909 founded Tel Aviv. On the eve of World War I, the land held roughly 600,000–700,000 Arabs and perhaps 60,000–85,000 Jews.


Arab opposition to Zionist immigration and land purchases appeared early, notably among peasants displaced when absentee landlords sold land to Jewish buyers, and in the Arab press by the 1910s.


## 2. Conflicting Promises and the British Mandate (1917–1935)


During World War I, Britain made overlapping commitments. In the Hussein–McMahon correspondence (1915–16) it promised Arab independence in exchange for revolt against the Ottomans, with boundaries left ambiguous. In the secret Sykes–Picot Agreement (1916) it divided the region with France. In the Balfour Declaration (November 1917) it endorsed “a national home for the Jewish people” in Palestine, while stating that nothing should prejudice the civil and religious rights of the existing non-Jewish communities.


After the war, the League of Nations gave Britain a Mandate over Palestine (1920–22) that incorporated the Balfour Declaration. Transjordan, east of the river, was administratively separated and closed to Jewish settlement.


Arab opposition organized through the Palestine Arab Congresses and the Arab Executive, demanding an end to Jewish immigration and a representative government in which the Arab majority would rule. Violence erupted repeatedly: in Jerusalem in 1920 (the Nebi Musa riots), in Jaffa in 1921, and in 1929, when Arab rioters killed 133 Jews — including 67 in the ancient community of Hebron — and British police killed over a hundred Arabs.


A central figure throughout was Haj Amin al-Husseini. Convicted by the British of incitement in the 1920 riots, he was nonetheless pardoned and appointed Mufti of Jerusalem in 1921, and he became head of the Supreme Muslim Council. Palestinian Arab politics in this period were sharply divided between his Husseini faction and the rival Nashashibi family, a division that would matter greatly in 1948.


Meanwhile the Jewish community (the *Yishuv*) built institutions that functioned as a state in waiting: an elected assembly, the Jewish Agency, the Histadrut labor federation, the Hebrew University, and the Haganah, an underground defense force. After Hitler came to power in 1933, Jewish immigration surged, and by 1936 Jews were nearly 30 percent of the population.


## 3. The Arab Revolt and the First Partition Plan (1936–1939)


In 1936 Arab leaders called a general strike that grew into a three-year revolt against both British rule and Jewish immigration. Its demands were an end to Jewish immigration, an end to land sales to Jews, and an independent Arab government — the program of a national movement, though a divided and often coercive one.


In 1937 the British Peel Commission concluded that the Mandate was unworkable and proposed partition: a small Jewish state of roughly one-fifth of the territory, a larger Arab area to be joined with Transjordan, and a British-held corridor around Jerusalem. The Zionist leadership, after bitter internal debate, accepted the principle of partition while objecting to the borders. The Arab Higher Committee, led by the Mufti, rejected it entirely.


The revolt turned inward as well as outward. The Mufti’s forces assassinated Arab rivals, moderates, and suspected collaborators; historians estimate that hundreds of Arabs, perhaps a thousand or more, were killed by other Arabs. Britain crushed the revolt with great force, killing roughly 5,000 Arabs, imprisoning thousands, and exiling much of the leadership. The Mufti fled in 1937. This matters for 1948: Palestinian Arab society lost much of its leadership and fighting strength a decade before the decisive war, while the Haganah gained experience fighting alongside the British.


Having won militarily, Britain conceded much politically. The White Paper of 1939 limited Jewish immigration to 75,000 over five years — just as European Jews were trying desperately to escape — and promised an independent, Arab-majority Palestine within ten years.


## 4. World War II and the Holocaust (1939–1945)


Six million Jews were murdered in the Holocaust. The White Paper’s closed door meant that Palestine could not serve as a refuge when it was most needed.


The Mufti, meanwhile, went to Iraq, where he supported the pro-Axis Rashid Ali coup of 1941. After the coup collapsed, a pogrom in Baghdad (the *Farhud*, June 1941) killed some 180 Jews. The Mufti fled to Italy and then Germany, met Hitler in November 1941, broadcast Nazi propaganda to the Arab world, and helped recruit Bosnian Muslims into a Waffen-SS division. His collaboration is well documented and is a permanent stain on the leadership that Palestinian Arabs carried into 1948 — though it should not be imputed to Palestinian Arabs as a whole, many of whom served with the British.


After the war, Jewish underground groups — the Irgun and Lehi, and at times the Haganah — fought the British to open immigration, including the 1946 bombing of the King David Hotel, which killed 91 people. Exhausted, Britain handed the problem to the United Nations.


## 5. Partition and War (1947–1949)


On November 29, 1947, the UN General Assembly passed Resolution 181, proposing two states — Jewish and Arab — joined in economic union, with Jerusalem under international administration. The Jewish state was allotted about 55 percent of the land (much of it the Negev desert) for a Jewish population of about 600,000; the Arab state about 43 percent for an Arab population of roughly 1.2 million. A large Arab minority would have lived in the Jewish state. The Jewish Agency accepted the plan; the Arab Higher Committee and the Arab states rejected it, holding that the majority population should decide the future of the whole country.


**The civil war (November 1947 – May 1948).** Fighting began immediately. Arab irregulars attacked Jewish neighborhoods and besieged Jerusalem’s road links; Jewish forces retaliated and, from April 1948, went on the offensive under Plan Dalet to secure the territory of the Jewish state and its access routes. Atrocities occurred on both sides. At Deir Yassin (April 9), Irgun and Lehi fighters killed about 100–110 villagers, many of them non-combatants; four days later, Arab forces ambushed a medical convoy to Hadassah Hospital and killed 78 Jewish doctors, nurses, and fighters. In May, Arab forces killed most of the defenders of the surrendered Kfar Etzion bloc.


Here Cherry’s main point has real merit. Palestinian Arab forces were fragmented. The Arab Liberation Army, organized by the Arab League under Fawzi al-Qawuqji, was composed largely of volunteers from other Arab countries. The Mufti’s own Army of the Holy War was small. Many villages fought only locally or sought accommodations with their Jewish neighbors, and the wealthier urban classes left early. When the cities of Haifa and Jaffa fell in April and May, Arab society collapsed faster than almost anyone expected.


**The invasion (May 1948 – 1949).** Israel declared independence on May 14, 1948. The next day the armies of Egypt, Transjordan, Syria, and Iraq entered the country, with a token Lebanese role. The Arab League’s declared aim was to prevent the Jewish state. Their efforts were poorly coordinated, and their rulers had competing aims: King Abdullah of Transjordan, whose Arab Legion was the best Arab army, concentrated on the areas allotted to the Arab state and on Jerusalem, and had quietly discussed dividing the land with the Zionist leadership. After the first weeks, Israeli forces outnumbered the Arab armies in the field, were better organized, and gained arms during the truces. By early 1949 Israel held about 78 percent of Mandate Palestine. Transjordan took the West Bank and East Jerusalem (annexing them in 1950), and Egypt held the Gaza Strip. About 6,000 Israelis — roughly one percent of the Jewish population — died in the war.


**The refugees.** Some 700,000–750,000 Palestinian Arabs — more than half the Arab population — fled or were expelled, and more than 400 villages were depopulated. The causes were several and varied by place and month: the fear and flight that accompany any war, the departure of the leadership, news of Deir Yassin, Arab orders or encouragement to evacuate in some places (Haifa is the most argued case), and outright expulsions by Israeli forces in others, most notably at Lydda and Ramle in July 1948, where some 50,000–70,000 people were driven out. Historian Benny Morris, who documented these events from Israeli archives, concluded that the refugee problem was “born of war, not by design” — neither a master plan of expulsion nor simple voluntary flight. What is not disputed is that Israel decided in 1948 not to allow the refugees to return, and later used their land for new settlement. Palestinians call this the *Nakba*, “the catastrophe.”


**The other exodus.** Over the following two decades, some 850,000 Jews left or were driven from Arab countries and Iran, where Jewish communities had lived for millennia — through riots, legal persecution, confiscation of property, and expulsion. Most were absorbed by Israel. Unlike the Palestinian refugees, they were resettled and naturalized, and their descendants are not refugees today. Palestinian refugees in most Arab states were kept in camps and, outside Jordan, generally denied citizenship, and a dedicated UN agency (UNRWA, 1949) has administered them since, extending refugee status to descendants.


## 6. Between the Wars (1949–1967)


The 1949 armistice lines (the “Green Line”) were never recognized as permanent borders. Arab states refused to recognize Israel; Palestinian *fedayeen* raided across the borders and Israel retaliated, sometimes harshly. In 1956 Israel joined Britain and France in attacking Egypt during the Suez Crisis, then withdrew under American pressure. Within Israel, Arab citizens received the vote but lived under military government until 1966.


In 1964 the Arab League created the Palestine Liberation Organization; by 1969 Yasser Arafat and his Fatah movement controlled it. Its charter called for the elimination of Israel through armed struggle.


## 7. The Six-Day War and Occupation (1967–1987)


In May 1967 Egypt expelled UN peacekeepers from Sinai, closed the Straits of Tiran to Israeli shipping, and massed troops on the border, with Syria and Jordan joining a military alliance. Israel struck first on June 5. In six days it took Sinai and Gaza from Egypt, the West Bank and East Jerusalem from Jordan, and the Golan Heights from Syria. A second wave of Palestinians, about 250,000–300,000, fled or were displaced.


In September 1967 the Arab League at Khartoum declared “no peace, no recognition, no negotiations” with Israel. In November the UN Security Council passed Resolution 242, calling for Israeli withdrawal “from territories occupied” in exchange for peace and recognized borders — the “land for peace” formula that has framed diplomacy ever since.


Israel annexed East Jerusalem and, from the late 1960s, began building civilian settlements in the occupied territories, at first in strategic areas and, after 1977, across the West Bank as a matter of ideology and policy. Most of the international community considers these settlements illegal under the Fourth Geneva Convention; Israel disputes this. The Palestinians of the West Bank and Gaza lived under military rule without citizenship.


The PLO turned to terrorism and guerrilla war: airline hijackings, the murder of Israeli athletes at the 1972 Munich Olympics, and attacks from Jordan (from which King Hussein expelled it in 1970) and then Lebanon. Egypt and Syria attacked Israel in October 1973; after initial losses Israel prevailed. That war led, through American mediation, to Egyptian President Anwar Sadat’s visit to Jerusalem (1977), the Camp David Accords (1978), and the Egypt–Israel peace treaty (1979), under which Israel returned all of Sinai. It was the first proof that land for peace could work. Sadat paid for it with his life in 1981.


Israel invaded Lebanon in 1982 to drive out the PLO. During the occupation, Lebanese Christian militiamen allied with Israel massacred hundreds of Palestinian civilians in the Sabra and Shatila camps; an Israeli inquiry found Defense Minister Ariel Sharon indirectly responsible.


## 8. Intifada, Oslo, and the Peace That Failed (1987–2005)


In December 1987 a mass uprising — the First Intifada — broke out in Gaza and the West Bank, largely through strikes, boycotts, and stone-throwing. The same month the Muslim Brotherhood in Gaza founded Hamas, whose 1988 charter called for Israel’s destruction and contained explicitly antisemitic language. In 1988 the PLO declared a Palestinian state and accepted Resolution 242, implicitly recognizing Israel.


The Madrid Conference (1991) led to secret talks in Norway and the **Oslo Accords**, signed at the White House on September 13, 1993. Israel and the PLO recognized each other; a Palestinian Authority was created with limited self-rule in parts of Gaza and the West Bank; the hardest questions — borders, Jerusalem, refugees, settlements, and security — were deferred to final-status talks. Jordan signed a peace treaty with Israel in 1994.


Extremists on both sides attacked the process. In February 1994 an Israeli settler, Baruch Goldstein, murdered 29 Muslim worshippers in Hebron. Hamas and Islamic Jihad launched suicide bombings in Israeli cities. In November 1995 an Israeli religious nationalist assassinated Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin. Settlement building continued through the Oslo years, and Palestinian violence continued; each side saw the other’s conduct as proof of bad faith.


At Camp David in July 2000, Prime Minister Ehud Barak offered a Palestinian state on most of the West Bank and all of Gaza with a share of East Jerusalem; Arafat rejected it without a counter-proposal. Participants still dispute how generous the offer was. The Clinton Parameters (December 2000) and the Taba talks (January 2001) narrowed the gaps further, but time ran out. The **Second Intifada** (2000–2005), far more violent than the first, was marked by waves of suicide bombings in Israeli buses, cafés, and hotels and by heavy Israeli military operations in Palestinian cities. About 1,000 Israelis and over 3,000 Palestinians were killed. Israel began building a separation barrier in 2002; it sharply reduced attacks, but in many places it cuts deep into the West Bank.


## 9. Gaza, Hamas, and the Frozen Conflict (2005–2023)


In 2005 Israel withdrew all soldiers and settlers from Gaza. In 2006 Hamas won Palestinian legislative elections, and in 2007 it seized Gaza by force from Fatah. Israel and Egypt imposed a blockade. The Palestinian national movement has been split ever since: the Palestinian Authority, under Mahmoud Abbas, in parts of the West Bank, and Hamas in Gaza. In 2008 Prime Minister Ehud Olmert offered Abbas a state on territory equal in size to the West Bank and Gaza through land swaps; Abbas did not accept it.


Hamas fired thousands of rockets at Israeli towns, and Israel fought wars in Gaza in 2008–09, 2012, 2014, and 2021, each killing far more Palestinians than Israelis. In the West Bank, the settler population passed 500,000, settler violence against Palestinians rose, and Palestinian attacks on Israelis continued.


In 2020 the Abraham Accords normalized relations between Israel and the United Arab Emirates, Bahrain, Morocco, and Sudan, setting the Palestinian question aside. By 2023, talks of normalization with Saudi Arabia were advanced.


## 10. October 7 and the Gaza War (2023–2026)


On October 7, 2023, Hamas and allied fighters broke through the Gaza border fence and attacked Israeli towns, kibbutzim, army posts, and a music festival, killing about 1,200 people, most of them civilians, and taking 251 hostages. It was the deadliest day for Jews since the Holocaust, marked by deliberate murder of families in their homes and documented sexual violence.


Israel declared war with the aims of destroying Hamas and recovering the hostages. The campaign that followed devastated Gaza. Gaza’s Hamas-run health authorities report more than 72,500 Palestinians killed; that figure does not distinguish combatants from civilians and is disputed by Israel, but independent analyses indicate that a large share of the dead were women and children. Most of Gaza’s population was displaced, much of its housing destroyed, and in August 2025 a UN-backed food security body determined that famine conditions existed in Gaza City. Israel’s conduct of the war became the subject of a genocide case brought by South Africa at the International Court of Justice and of International Criminal Court arrest warrants for Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and former Defense Minister Yoav Gallant, as well as for Hamas commander Mohammed Deif. Israel rejects these proceedings. The war also widened: Hezbollah fired on northern Israel and was heavily degraded by Israel in 2024, and Israel and Iran exchanged direct strikes, culminating in a twelve-day war in June 2025 that included American strikes on Iranian nuclear sites.


A U.S.-brokered ceasefire took effect on October 10, 2025, under President Trump’s 20-point plan, which the UN Security Council endorsed in November 2025. All living hostages were released in October 2025 and the last remains returned in January 2026.


**As of October 2026,** the ceasefire formally holds, but its later stages have stalled. Israel’s army continues to control a buffer zone covering more than half of Gaza and conducts frequent strikes against what it identifies as militant targets; Gaza medical officials reported over 850 Palestinians killed in the first seven months of the truce, and Israel reported four of its soldiers killed in the same period. Hamas has not disarmed, the international stabilization force has not been fully deployed, and reconstruction has barely begun. The UN official overseeing the plan called the ceasefire working but far from perfect.

## What the Record Shows


Read whole, the history does not support either of the simple stories.


It does not support the story in which Jews arrived as foreign colonizers and took the land of a peaceful people. Jews had an ancient and unbroken bond to this land, many arrived as refugees from murder, they bought land before they ever conquered any, and they accepted partition in 1937 and 1947 while the Arab leadership rejected every division and chose war. Arab leaders who led those refusals — including a Mufti who collaborated with Hitler — bear real responsibility for what followed, and Hamas’s deliberate massacre of civilians on October 7 cannot be excused by any grievance.


Nor does it support the story in which Palestinians are a fiction and their dispossession either did not happen or was entirely self-inflicted. Palestinian Arabs were the majority in the land, they had political aspirations that were real even when badly led, and hundreds of thousands of them lost their homes in 1948, many by expulsion, without being allowed to return. Millions have lived for generations either as stateless refugees or under a military occupation in which they have no vote over the government that rules them, while settlements expand on land they expected for their state.


Both peoples have been sinned against, and both have sinned. Peace has come only when leaders on each side accepted the other’s right to exist and paid a real price for it — as Sadat and Begin did, and as Rabin and King Hussein did. Every honest policy must begin from that record rather than from either side’s mythology.

## Notes on Robert Cherry’s Column


Cherry is right that Palestinian Arab society in 1948 was fragmented, that its leadership was compromised by the Mufti’s violence and Nazi collaboration, that most Arab Liberation Army fighters came from outside the country, that the Arab states pursued their own interests, and that Israeli forces outnumbered them for most of the war. These points are often missing from popular accounts. The following specifics, however, need correction:


- **Peel Commission share.** The 1937 plan allotted the Jewish state roughly one-fifth of the land, not 12 percent.

- **The Mufti and the 1921 and 1929 riots.** He was convicted for incitement in the 1920 Jerusalem riots. His role in 1929 is debated; the British Shaw Commission criticized him but did not find him directly responsible. Saying he “fomented pogroms” in 1921 and 1929 goes beyond the evidence.

- **The 1936–39 revolt as non-nationalist.** Its demands — end to immigration and land sales, and an independent Arab government — were nationalist demands. It was also anti-Jewish and internally brutal. Both things are true. The column also omits that Britain’s suppression of the revolt destroyed much of the leadership and fighting capacity Palestinians would have had in 1948.

- **Iraq’s contingent.** It was not small. Iraq’s force in Palestine grew to roughly 15,000–18,000 men and held the northern West Bank, including a successful defense of Jenin in June 1948.

- **Lebanon.** Lebanon formally joined the war but its army played only a minimal role; “refused to send troops” overstates it.

- **Haifa.** Arab leaders did choose evacuation over the surrender terms offered, and outside Arab authorities encouraged it, while the Jewish mayor urged Arabs to stay. That is a fair example, but Haifa was not typical of 1948, and the column omits the expulsions at Lydda, Ramle, and elsewhere, as well as the massacre at Deir Yassin.

- **Meaning of “Nakba.”** Constantin Zureiq coined the term in August 1948 for the Arab defeat and the founding of Israel, so Cherry is right that it first named the defeat. But the refugee question was central to Palestinian and Arab demands from 1948 onward — UN Resolution 194 (December 1948) addressed return, and Arab states pressed it at the 1949 Lausanne conference. It did not originate with Oslo.

- **Date of Oslo.** The Oslo Accords were signed in 1993, not 1992.

- **Jordan’s treatment of refugees.** Jordan granted citizenship to most Palestinian refugees in the 1950s. The column’s point applies to Lebanon, which does deny them citizenship and restricts their work and property, and in varying ways to other Arab states, but not to Jordan in general.

- **“No Palestinian nationalist movement in 1948.”** There was one — weak, divided, and badly led, but real, going back to the Palestine Arab Congresses of the 1920s. Its failures explain why it lost; they do not mean it did not exist.

- **Unverified claims.** The characterization of the 2025 documentary *1936* and the specific attribution of the 90 percent figure to Ilan Pappé could not be checked here; the general claim that the ALA was mostly foreign volunteers is consistent with other historians.
